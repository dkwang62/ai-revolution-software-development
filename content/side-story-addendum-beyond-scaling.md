# Beyond Scaling: What a Body Might Teach a Mind

## The Central Question

For most of the last decade, the story of artificial intelligence has been a story of arithmetic. Take a neural network, make it larger. Take a dataset, make it larger. Take a cluster, make it larger. In this context, **scaling** means exactly that: increasing the size of the model, the amount of training data used to train it, and the amount of computing power devoted to the training run. Progress, for a remarkably long stretch, behaved like a dial you could simply turn up. This was not a trivial insight — it took real courage and real capital to bet that intelligence might be, in some deep sense, a function of scale rather than of clever design. And the bet paid off spectacularly.

But bets like this have a peculiar property: their success eventually reveals their own limits. When Ilya Sutskever argues that we are leaving the "age of scaling" for an "age of research," he is not announcing a failure. He is announcing a graduation. The scaling era proved that raw statistical learning over enormous corpora produces systems with startling competence. What it did not settle is a much older and harder question, and it is worth stating that question plainly, because everything that follows is an attempt to answer one piece of it:

> **What does an AI system actually need in order to develop robust, general intelligence — the kind that doesn't merely perform well, but holds up when the situation changes?**

Each section below answers one piece of that question, and construction robotics turns out to be an unusually good place to test the answers, because a construction site refuses to let a machine get away with mere pattern-matching.

## Why This Argument, From This Person

It's worth pausing on who is making this argument, because the source matters here in a specific way. Ilya Sutskever is not an outside critic of the scaling paradigm — he is one of the people who built it. He worked on AlexNet in 2012, the image-recognition network that helped establish deep learning as a practical technology rather than an academic curiosity. He later contributed to sequence-to-sequence learning at Google, work that fed directly into neural machine translation and much of what followed in language modeling. He then co-founded OpenAI as its chief scientist, helping shape the research direction that produced the GPT line of models — arguably the clearest demonstration the field has ever produced that scaling works. More recently he left to co-found Safe Superintelligence, a company explicitly organized around research rather than around shipping a broad consumer product.

Laid out this way, his career traces almost the entire arc of the scaling story: neural networks prove viable, deep learning outperforms older approaches, scaling those networks produces astonishing capability gains, and capability accumulates for the better part of a decade. What makes his current argument worth taking seriously isn't that he is famous or influential — credibility of that kind does not turn a hypothesis into a fact, and it shouldn't be treated as though it does. What makes it worth taking seriously is that the argument comes from someone who has repeatedly been in the room when the field discovered what actually worked, now arguing that the thing he helped prove works is not, by itself, a complete theory of intelligence. That is a different kind of claim than an outsider's skepticism, and it deserves to be examined on its merits rather than accepted on his authority — which is exactly the spirit in which the rest of this essay tries to treat it.

## Why Isn't Capability Enough? — Jaggedness

The unevenness of modern AI — brilliant at olympiad mathematics, occasionally confused by a shift in phrasing — is usually treated as an embarrassing footnote to otherwise impressive benchmarks. It deserves more respect than that. This unevenness has a name: Sutskever calls it **jaggedness**, and it is diagnostic rather than merely embarrassing. It tells us that whatever these systems are doing when they solve a hard problem is not the same operation a human performs when navigating an unfamiliar kitchen, a new job, or a broken elevator.

Human competence is *robust* in a specific way: it degrades gracefully. Put a person in a slightly unfamiliar situation and their performance dips a little. Put a large language model in a slightly unfamiliar situation and its performance can fall off a cliff, even though it just cleared a bar most humans couldn't touch. One interpretation of this pattern is that human intelligence is built on a scaffolding of general-purpose **priors** — here, a prior simply means knowledge or expectations a system brings to a new situation before it has had any direct experience of that particular situation. Priors like physical intuition, causal reasoning, and social modeling don't need to be relearned for every new domain. Current AI, by contrast, often seems to be assembling competence domain by domain, pattern by pattern, without a unifying skeleton of priors underneath.

Now transplant that fragility onto heavy machinery, and the stakes change character entirely. An excavator operating at 99.9% reliability on familiar soil sounds like a solved problem — until it meets a slope condition it has never encountered, and reliability doesn't degrade gracefully to 90%. It can collapse to something much worse, all at once, with consequences measured in tonnes rather than typos. This is why jaggedness, which reads as a curiosity in a chatbot, reads as a safety requirement in a machine that can move earth. The real objective was never "maximum average performance." It was always **graceful degradation when something unexpected happens** — and a system's ability to recognize, in effect, *my model of this situation doesn't match my sensors, I should stop and ask*, may end up mattering more than raw capability.

## Why Do Humans Learn From Fewer Examples? — Priors and Sample Efficiency

There's a classic argument in linguistics called the "poverty of the stimulus," used to explain why children acquire language so fast from so little input: they must be bringing innate structure to the problem. The relevance to AI is a parallel question — **how much experience does a learner actually need before it can extract a general concept?** — and it's worth borrowing the linguistics framing because our models fail this test in a strange way. They face an *abundance* of stimulus and still can't match a toddler's rate of concept formation from a handful of examples. The child who touches a hot stove once never needs a second demonstration. A model, by contrast, often needs a failure mode described, and described again, and described from a hundred more angles, before the lesson generalizes reliably. This capacity — extracting a useful general rule from relatively few examples — is what researchers call **sample efficiency**, and it is exactly the concept the rest of this essay keeps returning to.

Why the gap? A child's single trial is not really a single trial — it arrives already wrapped in proprioception, pain response, a working theory of causality, and an active hypothesis about what "hot" even means as a category. The data point is thin; the prior is thick. Our models have inverted that ratio: thin priors, extraordinarily thick data. This suggests sample efficiency and generalization may not be two separate research problems — they might be the same problem wearing two names.

This is also, not coincidentally, the exact gap that separates a model looking at a photograph of a concrete block from a machine that has actually tried to lift one. A vision system can produce an estimate — *that looks like roughly five hundred kilograms* — from correlations between pixels and labels it has seen before. But an estimate derived from appearance is not the same as an expectation derived from consequence. The machine that has felt hydraulic pressure spike, watched a bucket bind against unexpected resistance, and sensed its own center of gravity shift under an unbalanced load has acquired something the image alone cannot give it: a thick prior, built from a thin number of trials — exactly the ratio biological learning depends on. Keep this contrast in mind; it becomes the essay's running example.

## How Should a System Decide What Matters? — Value Functions

Physical intelligence raises a further question once perception and action are both on the table: when several actions are possible, what should the system value? This is where Sutskever's discussion of emotion becomes relevant, and the connection is worth stating explicitly, because it is not an obvious one. Sutskever uses the idea of emotions as a kind of **value function** — a mechanism for answering "how important is this outcome to me?" — to illustrate how biological systems might encode information about what matters for action, without needing to recompute that judgment from scratch every time. This is best treated as an interpretive analogy rather than a settled scientific fact: neuroscience has not conclusively proven that emotion simply *is* a value function, but the analogy is a useful lens for thinking about compressed decision-making.

Under that lens, "fear" is not a primitive irrational reflex bolted onto a rational mind — it can be understood as millions of years of accumulated statistics about which situations tend to end badly, compressed into a single, fast signal that can steer behavior before slow deliberate reasoning even finishes booting up. If this interpretation holds, an AI system without something functionally equivalent to emotion isn't more rational than a human — it is simply missing a layer of compressed, battle-tested priors that would otherwise save it enormous amounts of trial and error.

A construction machine makes this idea concrete almost immediately. It cannot simply optimize "finish the excavation as fast as possible," because that objective, taken alone, is indifferent to a destabilized slope or a nearby worker. What it actually needs is something structurally similar to an emotional hierarchy: progress weighed as a modest positive, efficiency weighed a little higher, safety weighed enormously higher, and anything resembling risk to a human weighed so heavily that it dominates every other term in the calculation. This is not a separate "ethics module" bolted onto a planning algorithm after the fact — it is the same compressed-value architecture Sutskever is describing, translated into a domain where getting the weighting wrong has immediate physical stakes rather than merely reputational ones.

## What Does "Understanding the World" Actually Mean? — World Models

It helps to notice that a **world model** — an AI's internal representation of how the world works — is not one thing but a ladder of increasingly demanding claims:

**Recognition** → "This is a rock."

**Description** → "This is a large rock resting on loose soil."

**Prediction** → "If the bucket pushes here, the rock will likely move in that direction."

**Causal reasoning** → "The rock moved *because* the bucket applied force at that point, overcoming friction with the soil beneath it" — the system now models *why* something happened, not merely *that* it happened, which is what allows it to reason forward: "if I apply more force at this angle, I expect this different consequence."

**Physical reasoning** → "This much force at this angle should move it without destabilizing the machine."

**Intuition** → "I know how this is likely to behave" — no calculation, just an expectation, arrived at instantly.

That top rung is what "physical intuition" actually means, and it is very hard to see how a system reaches it without something like a body accumulating something like experience. A camera gives a system correlation. A body operating in the physical world gives a system something categorically different — *consequence*. Push too hard, the object tips. Grip too loosely, it slips. Misjudge the friction of wet gravel, the machine slides.

Consequence is a teacher that cannot be talked out of its lesson. A language model trained purely on text can be told, in ten thousand different phrasings, that heavy objects require more force to move — and still fail to apply that fact correctly in a new configuration it hasn't seen described. A system with actuators and force sensors doesn't need the fact stated at all; it needs to attempt the lift once, feel the torque spike, and update. This is closer to what Sutskever gestures at when he talks about learning through interaction rather than through static datasets: it isn't merely a different data source, it's a different **epistemology** — a different way of acquiring knowledge. Text describes the world secondhand. A body encounters it firsthand, and firsthand encounters carry a kind of ground truth that no amount of secondhand description can fully substitute for.

It is worth being precise about what kind of difference this is, because it is easy to mistake it for simply "more data." Scaling, in the sense defined at the start of this essay, gives a model more observations of the same basic kind — more images, more sentences, more examples of a pattern. Embodiment does not primarily offer more observations of that kind; it offers a structurally different kind of observation, one embedded in a closed loop: *command → movement → resistance → force → hydraulic response → outcome*. A system generating data this way isn't just accumulating more entries in a table. It is accumulating entries in which each one already carries a candidate answer to "what caused what," because the system itself produced the action and felt the consequence. This distinction — more data versus data embedded in an action-consequence loop — is arguably the intellectual core of the entire argument being made here.

### Does Intelligence Actually Require a Body?

Before going further, the argument deserves to be tested against its strongest objection, rather than simply asserted. A skeptic could reasonably respond: *maybe a sufficiently advanced AI can build an adequate world model without ever having a body at all.* This is not a weak objection. A system could, in principle, learn a great deal about physics from video, from simulation, from scientific literature, from human demonstrations recorded on camera, from synthetic physics engines, and from large-scale multimodal data combining all of these. None of that requires actuators or force sensors, and all of it is far cheaper to generate at scale than physical trials with real machinery.

The honest answer is that embodiment is not obviously *necessary* in some absolute sense — it is a claim about what embodiment makes *easier* to acquire. What a body supplies, and what passive observation of video or simulation supplies less directly, is exactly the causal, consequence-bearing loop described above: the system doesn't just watch something happen, it makes something happen and feels what comes back. A sufficiently good simulation could in principle approximate this, which is precisely why simulation appears later in this essay as a legitimate and even necessary part of the learning pipeline, not as a poor substitute for the real thing. The more defensible version of the thesis, then, is not "intelligence requires a body," but something narrower and more testable: **direct causal interaction supplies a kind of training signal that is difficult, though perhaps not impossible, to replicate through observation alone** — and real embodiment remains the most reliable way we currently know of to generate that signal at the fidelity physical tasks demand.

## Why Is Construction So Difficult? — Generalization Under Variation

This is where the abstract argument earns its keep, because it's worth asking directly: why would construction robotics be *unusually* hard, harder than the industrial robotics we've already more or less mastered? It isn't primarily hardware — modern excavators already have more than enough strength and precision for the physical work. It isn't even perception and control in the narrow sense; computer vision, LiDAR, localization, and motion planning are difficult but tractable engineering problems, visibly improving year over year.

The deeper answer is that a factory and a construction site represent two almost opposite philosophies of environment:

**Factory** → repetition → predictability → specialization is enough.

**Construction site** → variation → uncertainty → generalization is required.

A factory offers the same object, the same position, the same lighting, the same task, over and over — precisely the repeatable structure that lets a robot get away with memorizing a procedure rather than understanding one. Construction offers new terrain, new weather, new materials, unpredictable obstacles, and humans and machines both moving unpredictably through the same space. It does not reward memorization. **It punishes it.** A robot that has only learned "execute this known procedure extremely reliably" will eventually meet a site condition its procedure never anticipated — and that is the jaggedness problem, arriving in physical form.

Put differently: construction functions as an adversarial curriculum for exactly the kind of generalization current AI struggles with. That reframes the question. It stops being "how do we make AI good enough for construction?" and becomes something closer to: could construction be one of the environments that makes AI good at generalization in the first place?

## How Does a Machine Learn Safely? — From Simulation to Deployment

Imagine, concretely, what it would look like for a machine to close the loop between action and consequence:

**Attempt** the dig → **unexpected resistance** at the bucket → **sensor detects mismatch** with the predicted force → **model updates** its expectation → **next similar encounter** is handled differently → **better prediction** → repeated over thousands of trials, this becomes **generalization** rather than memorization.

The machine isn't recalling *that exact patch of ground* — it has extracted the underlying physical relationship and can apply it to ground it has never touched. That is the transition scaling alone doesn't reliably produce.

But here the analogy to human or animal learning breaks down in an instructive way, and the catch deserves to be stated honestly rather than skated past. A language model can be wrong at essentially zero cost — you correct it, nothing is damaged, the next attempt begins clean. An excavator that is wrong about slope stability does not get that luxury. So "learning by doing" has to mean something different for a physical system. In practice it means a graduated curriculum, with the cost of error rising only as competence is demonstrated:

**Simulation** (failure is free, environment is generic) → **Digital twin** built from a real site's actual geometry and soil data (failure is still free, but the environment is specific) → **Controlled physical trial under direct supervision** (failure is costly but contained) → **Real site with a human monitoring closely** → **Increasing autonomy**, where a human is called in only when the machine's own model of the situation stops matching its sensor readings.

Physical experience can be accumulated without paying the full price of every physical mistake — but only if the learning environment is engineered in stages like this one.

## Robots That Share a Mind — From One Machine to an Ecosystem

It's tempting, once embodiment enters the picture, to imagine the goal as building one increasingly capable machine. But a more interesting possibility doesn't require any single machine to be humanoid, or even particularly general on its own. It requires several specialized machines to share something more valuable than a body: a world model.

Consider an excavator that knows how much material it has removed from a foundation cut, a fleet of haul trucks that know how much of that material they've actually transported, a surveying drone that continuously updates the site's terrain geometry, and a crane that knows where it's safe to swing a load given everyone else's position. None of these machines needs to resemble a person, or even resemble each other. What they need is a shared, continuously updated representation of the site's state — *the foundation excavation is seventy percent complete, the western slope has been more unstable than modeled* — that a site-level planning intelligence can draw on to coordinate all of them.

That shared representation has to be built from somewhere, and it draws on two distinct layers of sensing. The first is a visual layer — cameras and LiDAR — which supplies the site's shape and appearance. The second is a physical layer — force, torque, and hydraulic-pressure sensors — which supplies the grounded, consequence-tested feedback that turns a visual estimate into a tested expectation, in the same way the concrete-block example distinguished appearance from consequence earlier in this essay. At that point the interesting unit of analysis is no longer "the robot." It's the ecosystem: a distributed sensing and acting system whose intelligence lives partly in each machine and partly in the shared model connecting them.

## Can Learning Continue After Deployment? — Breaking the Frozen-Model Paradigm

Most current AI systems are effectively frozen after training: they don't continuously rewrite their underlying model as they operate in the world. Instead they follow a **train → freeze → deploy → wait → retrain** cycle. It's worth noticing that this pattern, so familiar it barely seems worth stating, may be a historical accident of how we happened to build the first generation of large models, rather than a fundamental feature of machine intelligence. No animal learns this way. A dog doesn't get "trained," frozen, and deployed for six months before its next update. Learning and living are the same continuous process for every biological intelligence we know of.

A fleet of construction machines makes it easy to picture what breaking that paradigm would look like: deploy the machine, let it accumulate real operating experience, let that experience update its model of soil, load, and slope, and let the improved model inform the next day's work — rather than waiting for a scheduled retraining cycle months later. That continuous loop is exactly the kind of sample-efficient, context-sensitive adaptation that static pretraining struggles to produce.

But every advantage of continuous learning is, from a safety perspective, also a liability, and the two cannot be neatly separated. A system that never stops updating is a system whose values and behaviors are never fully fixed and verified. This is why the value-function question from earlier and the continuous-learning question turn out to be the same question wearing different clothes: a machine that keeps learning from its own operation needs a value hierarchy robust enough that *what* it learns to prioritize under novel conditions still keeps safety non-negotiable, even as everything else about its behavior is free to adapt.

There is a second, quieter problem worth naming, because this essay has repeatedly framed physical learning as the antidote to memorization — and that framing needs a caveat. Physical experience is not automatically immune to memorization; it can simply memorize at a different level. Suppose an excavator encounters wet soil five hundred times and becomes measurably better at handling it. It might have learned the intended lesson — *wet soil has different friction characteristics, adjust force accordingly*. Or it might have learned an accidental correlation instead — *whenever the sky is cloudy, reduce digging force* — which happened to work during training because, at that particular site, cloudy weather and wet soil were reliably correlated. The second "lesson" will fail the moment the machine operates somewhere the correlation doesn't hold. This is the physical-world equivalent of overfitting, and it means continuous learning from real experience does not, by itself, guarantee generalization. The machine has to be learning the right underlying variable, not merely a convenient statistical shadow of it — and confirming which one it actually learned is a genuinely hard verification problem, not a detail that resolves itself once enough physical data is collected.

## What Happens After Scaling? — Research Taste

If the scaling paradigm really is reaching its limits, progress depends increasingly on discovering which new questions are worth asking in the first place — and that scarce skill is what Sutskever means by **research taste**: a learned sense for the *shape* of a good question, built from an enormous private history of failed hunches and half-successes that no textbook records. It resists being scaled simply by hiring more researchers or buying more compute, which is a genuinely humbling thought for a field that has spent a decade treating almost every bottleneck as solvable through more resources.

The construction framing sharpens this rather than distracting from it: deciding whether an excavator fleet should learn primarily in simulation or invest earlier in physical trials, whether to centralize intelligence in one planning model or distribute it across specialized machines — these are exactly the judgment calls that more compute cannot make for you.

## What Construction Cannot Teach — and Why Construction, Specifically?

Two qualifications are needed before the argument is allowed to stand, and both push back against a stronger claim than this essay actually wants to make.

The first is that construction is not a stand-in for general intelligence. It is very well suited to teaching certain things: material properties, force and friction, terrain and spatial relationships, uncertainty, machine coordination, and safety under physical unpredictability. It is not obviously suited to teaching language, abstract mathematics, social intelligence, long-horizon scientific reasoning, or most of the accumulated cultural knowledge that fills a library. So the conclusion this essay is entitled to is not "construction will make AI generally intelligent." It is the narrower and more defensible claim that **construction could supply one unusually valuable component of general intelligence — grounded physical intelligence — without supplying the rest.**

The second qualification is that the argument, as developed so far, risks a mild circularity: it observes that AI needs better generalization, notes that construction is a difficult environment, and concludes that construction can teach generalization — without asking why construction, specifically, rather than any of the other embodied environments available, from warehouses and agriculture to autonomous vehicles or space robotics. The honest answer is a comparison along two axes rather than a claim of uniqueness: how much physical *consequence* an environment carries, and how much *variation* it contains. A factory has real consequence — a dropped part is a real cost — but comparatively little variation, since the same objects arrive in the same positions under the same conditions. A simulation can offer enormous variation cheaply, but its consequences are not fully real; a simulated failure teaches something, but not everything a physical failure would. Construction is distinctive because it plausibly sits high on both axes at once: the physical stakes are as real as a factory's, and the environmental variation — new terrain, weather, materials, and moving humans and machines — is closer to what a rich simulation can offer. That combination, more than any claim that construction is *the* answer, is what makes it worth taking seriously as a training environment rather than merely an application.

## Conclusion: What Comes After Scaling

It is worth returning, at the end, to where this essay began: with a person who helped prove that scaling works, now arguing that scaling alone is not a complete theory of intelligence. That argument could have stayed abstract. Instead, tracing it through jaggedness, priors, embodiment, world models, and continuous learning turns it into something closer to a research agenda than a slogan — a set of specific, checkable questions about what a learning system actually needs, rather than a general sense that "bigger models" has run its course.

Construction served this essay as a lens, not as the point. It was useful precisely because it forced abstract claims to earn their keep: a claim about causal interaction had to explain what a bucket meeting unexpected resistance actually teaches; a claim about value functions had to explain how a machine weighs progress against a worker's safety; a claim about continuous learning had to confront the real possibility of learning the wrong lesson from real experience. Held up to that kind of scrutiny, the scaling-to-research transition stops sounding like a mood shift in the AI industry and starts looking like an unusually well-specified scientific problem: current systems have shown they can absorb an enormous amount of the world's recorded text and imagery, but absorbing description is not the same as accumulating experience, and the missing piece may be less about the size of the model than about the kind of learning loop it sits inside.

None of this settles the question Sutskever raised. It only sharpens it. If the age of scaling really is giving way to an age of research, the interesting work ahead is not simply building larger systems and hoping generality arrives as a side effect — it is identifying, testing, and sometimes discarding specific hypotheses about what robust intelligence actually requires: better priors, richer causal interaction, more honest value functions, learning that survives contact with a changing world. Construction offered one plausible place to run that test. It will not be the only one, and it may not even be the best one. But the underlying question it was used to illustrate is the one that will outlast this particular example: not whether models can be made bigger, but what, beyond size, intelligence actually turns out to be made of.
