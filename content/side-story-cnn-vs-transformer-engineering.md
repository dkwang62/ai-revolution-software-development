# The Gate That Remembers Your Face: What the CNN-to-Transformer Shift Actually Costs and Buys You

A companion essay covers the intuition behind CNNs and Transformers — detail to pattern to whole, versus relationship to context to meaning. This one picks up where that leaves off: not what the ideas *are*, but what they *cost*, and why "the newer architecture" isn't automatically "the right one" for a given job. The fastest way into that question is a concrete system, so we'll use the same one as before — a condominium gate that opens when it recognizes your face — and look at what it actually costs to run, and where its choice of architecture stops paying for itself.

## What the Gate Is Actually Running

Quick recap of the plumbing, stated plainly rather than intuitively: the camera converts your face into a list of a few hundred numbers, an embedding, and the gate compares that embedding against everyone's stored embeddings, looking for the closest match. That comparison step is called a **similarity search**, and it shows up everywhere in modern AI — face gates, document search, recommendation systems — anywhere a system needs to find "the closest thing to this" rather than "the exact match for this." At the scale of one condominium, perhaps a thousand residents, that search doesn't need anything as elaborate as a dedicated vector database. Comparing a new face against a thousand stored ones is computationally trivial; an ordinary database table with the comparison math bolted on handles it fine. The interesting engineering question isn't the search. It's what produces the embedding in the first place — and for most of the last decade, the answer was a CNN.

## What a CNN's Design Choices Actually Buy You

The first essay covered what a filter does. What's worth examining here is why a CNN is built the way it is — because those choices are not arbitrary, and they explain both the architecture's strengths and, eventually, its limits.

**Weight sharing.** A CNN uses the *same* filter across the entire image, rather than learning a separate filter for every possible location. This is a deliberate bet: an edge detector that works in the top-left corner should work equally well in the bottom-right, so why learn it twice? It's the software equivalent of writing one reusable function instead of copy-pasting the same logic at every screen coordinate, and it turns out to be an extremely good bet for images specifically.

**Locality.** Each filter only looks at a small neighborhood of pixels at a time — a 3×3 patch, say — not the whole image at once. This mirrors something genuinely true about most images: whether a pixel belongs to an eye depends mostly on the pixels immediately around it, not on a pixel in the far corner of the frame. As layers stack, the effective area each part of the network can "see" does grow — a concept called the **receptive field** — but early on, the network is deliberately near-sighted.

Between blocks of convolutions, CNNs typically also run a step called **pooling**, which shrinks the representation down — replacing, say, every 2×2 block of numbers with just its maximum value. This makes the network faster to run, and, usefully, a bit more tolerant of a feature shifting slightly, since it's compressing "roughly where" rather than "exactly where."

Here's the part that matters for engineering, not just theory: locality and weight sharing don't just make CNNs efficient to *train*. They make CNNs cheap to *run*. That second fact is why a CNN could comfortably live on the modest chip mounted beside a gate for years, drawing little power, responding in a fraction of a second, with no need to phone home to a server anywhere.

One more structural fact worth being explicit about here, because it's a real cost lever rather than an implementation detail: both architectures are *deep* — built from many stacked layers, each one refining what the last one produced, not just a single pass of filters or attention. That's true of Transformers as well as CNNs; a Transformer isn't one round of attention, it's attention and refinement repeated across dozens of layers, each pass making the representation a bit more contextual than the last. Depth is part of why these models work as well as they do — but every additional layer is also more computation, in training and, just as importantly, at inference time, on the gate's hardware, every time someone walks up to it. How many layers to use is therefore a genuine engineering decision, not something that comes for free: more layers can mean better accuracy, but also more latency and more silicon needed to hit a real-time response, and someone sizing a model for a small embedded chip has to make that tradeoff explicitly rather than inheriting it by default.

## Where the CNN's Bet Starts to Cost You

The same assumption that makes CNNs efficient — that what matters most is *nearby* information — starts to strain in specific, identifiable places.

Consider a sentence: "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because *it* was too big." To resolve what "it" refers to, you need to relate a word near the end of the sentence to a word near the beginning, potentially many words away. A CNN-style architecture *can* eventually connect distant pieces of information, but only indirectly — by stacking enough local layers that the receptive field grows wide enough to reach. That's a roundabout route to a relationship that might genuinely be direct. And this isn't only a language problem: understanding that a particular pixel belongs to "a person's hand" can depend on context from clear across the frame, not just the pixels immediately touching it.

Text had its own dominant architecture before the Transformer, and it ran into this same wall from a different direction. The **Recurrent Neural Network**, or RNN, processed a sentence one word at a time, carrying forward a running summary as it went — which meant a word early in a long sentence had to survive being repeatedly compressed and overwritten before it could influence a word much later on, often getting diluted or lost along the way. It's largely obsolete now, but worth naming, because it's the architecture the Transformer actually displaced for language.

What the field needed, for problems like this, was an architecture that could relate any two pieces of information directly, regardless of distance, and that could be trained by throwing enormous amounts of parallel computation at the problem. That architecture arrived in 2017: the Transformer.

## What a Transformer Actually Trains

The first essay described attention as asking, for each piece of information, which other pieces matter right now. Worth being precise about what's actually happening underneath that, because it's a genuinely different kind of learned object than a CNN filter — not just a renamed version of the same idea.

Every piece of input — a word, a sub-word, an image patch — is called a **token**, and gets converted into a vector of numbers, much like the face embedding described earlier. Each token then effectively produces two things: a **query** ("here's what I'm looking for") and a **key** ("here's what I contain"). Every token's query is compared against every other token's key, producing a relevance score, which determines how much information — the token's **value** — should blend in from every other token when updating its own representation.

So what does a Transformer actually learn, if not filters? It learns a set of **weight matrices** — the ones that produce each token's query, key, and value — plus additional feed-forward layers, applied identically to every token, the same way a CNN filter is reused across every position. That part is analogous to a CNN. But the *pattern of who attends to whom* is not stored anywhere. It's recomputed fresh for every new input, on the fly, from those fixed matrices. A CNN filter always looks at the same relative neighborhood no matter what image you feed it. A Transformer's attention pattern depends on content: for one face, the patch over the left eye might attend strongly to the patch over the right eye; for a different image entirely, the pattern could look nothing like that. The weights are learned. The attention pattern is something that happens at inference time, produced fresh whenever those weights meet a new input.

That every-token-compares-against-every-token mechanic is also where the Transformer's real cost lives, and it's worth naming directly, because it's the flip side of the long-range advantage from earlier. Comparing every token against every other token means the amount of computation grows roughly with the *square* of how much input you feed in — double the length of text or the number of image patches, and the attention step gets roughly four times more expensive, not twice. That's the practical reason a Transformer can't simply be handed an unlimited amount of context for free, and it's part of why, even for models built specifically to work across very long documents, reliably relating something at the very start to something at the very end tends to get harder as that document grows, despite technically being within reach in a single attention step. A CNN's local, fixed-size filters never pay that particular tax; it's a cost that comes specifically with buying the ability to relate anything to anything.

That distinction has two consequences, and together they explain most of why Transformers spread as fast as they did.

**Direct, long-range relationships.** Any token can relate to any other token in a single step, not through many layers of accumulated locality. Distance in the input stops being an obstacle.

**Parallelization and scaling.** CNNs already parallelize reasonably well — filters can be applied to many positions at once. But because every token's attention calculation can, in principle, happen simultaneously with every other token's, Transformers lean into that parallelism even more directly, which suits modern hardware built for enormous numbers of simultaneous calculations. This turned out to matter enormously once researchers started asking what happens if you just make the model bigger and feed it more data: Transformers kept improving in fairly predictable ways at scale, without hitting the kind of wall earlier architectures ran into. That scaling behavior is a large part of why the large language models reshaping software development are Transformers underneath, not CNNs.

## What the Gate's Network Is Actually Trained to Do

CNN and Transformer are architectures, not objectives. Either one can be pointed at classifying images, generating text, predicting depth in a photo, translating languages — neither comes pre-loaded with "recognize faces" as a purpose. But for face recognition specifically, both architectures are typically trained toward the same target, and naming that target precisely clears up a lot.

The goal isn't "notice patterns" in some vague sense. It's a specific, comparative goal: make embeddings of the same person land close together in that numerical space, and embeddings of different people land far apart. This falls under **metric learning** — judged on the relative *distance* between examples, rather than on getting one single "correct label" right the way a simple classifier is. Some systems train on this directly, with explicit pairs of "same person" and "different person" examples; others use a classification-style objective during training that produces embeddings with the same separating property as a side effect. Either way, the target ends up the same: separate people cleanly in that numerical space.

This objective also explains a distinction worth keeping straight when thinking about what a system like this can and can't do. Your condo gate performs **verification** — does this one face match this one enrolled resident? That's a one-to-one comparison, and a fundamentally easier problem. Contrast that with **identification** — given a face, find out who it is among a huge, open population, the way border control scans against a national database. Identification at scale, along with harder conditions generally (extreme angles, occlusion, poor lighting, large gaps in time between photos), is where the extra capacity of a Transformer tends to earn its cost. Your gate never has to solve that problem, which turns out to matter a great deal for which architecture makes sense.

## Where the Training Actually Happens

There's a common point of confusion worth clearing up directly, since "AI" has become nearly synonymous with "LLM" in a lot of public conversation: a face-recognition model has nothing to do with a Large Language Model. An LLM is a Transformer trained on text, to predict and generate language. A face-recognition model — CNN, Vision Transformer, or hybrid — is trained on images, to produce good face embeddings. They can share an architectural family, but they are entirely separately trained, separately purposed systems. Training the condo gate's model has never required an LLM at any point.

Could that training happen on a local server? Technically yes — nothing about the process is inherently tied to one company's infrastructure. What it actually requires is a large labeled dataset — historically hundreds of thousands to millions of face images, multiple photos per person — one or more capable GPUs, and real time, days to weeks depending on model and dataset size, because training runs both a forward pass and backpropagation repeatedly across the entire dataset, many times over.

In practice, almost nobody deploying a face-recognition product does this from scratch, and the reason is really about data, not compute. Assembling a dataset large and diverse enough to produce a genuinely good general-purpose face model is the kind of undertaking only a handful of organizations have done. Instead, vendors adopt an already-trained model — open-source or commercially licensed — and either deploy it as-is or apply lighter **fine-tuning**: a smaller, cheaper additional training pass on a more specific dataset, perhaps to adjust for a particular camera's characteristics. Fine-tuning is genuinely lighter, plausible on modest local hardware or a few hours of rented cloud GPU time, unlike training from zero.

So the real chain, for something like your condo gate, looks like this: a large organization trains a general face model on a huge dataset with a serious GPU cluster over weeks; a vendor adopts that pretrained model and possibly fine-tunes it lightly for their hardware; the model ships to the condo, frozen; the condo enrolls residents, and no training happens at that step at all. When management photographs a new resident, the neural network isn't retrained — no backpropagation, no changed weights. The photo is simply run through the already-trained model to produce an embedding, which is stored. Adding your face to the condo database is much closer to adding a new document to a search index than to retraining a model. The model already knows how to recognize faces in general; enrollment just tells it whose face is whose.

## Does the Layman's Instinct — "No Real Benefit Here" — Hold Up?

Largely, yes, and it's worth stating plainly rather than hedging it away. For a small, closed population like a condo, on constrained embedded hardware, with careful enrollment, a well-tuned CNN or CNN-heavy hybrid is often the more straightforward engineering choice — not because a Transformer is incapable here, but because the extra flexibility it buys (long-range relationships, robustness on huge and varied populations) isn't worth much when the problem is already this small and this constrained. Reaching for a full Transformer for a problem this size is a bit like standing up a distributed database cluster to store a grocery list: more capable in the abstract, pointless in practice, and probably slower for the job at hand.

That's also the fuller explanation behind an observation like "the gate is fast and it always works" — though it's worth being honest about what that observation can and can't tell you. Fast-and-reliable is a real signal about the system, but not a reliable way to identify the architecture from the outside. It could come from a small CNN, a lightweight Vision Transformer, a hybrid, aggressive quantization, a dedicated accelerator chip, a small candidate pool, or, most likely, several of these together. What "fast and it always works" tells you with more confidence is that the whole pipeline has been tuned for this specific job — camera, preprocessing, model, hardware, enrollment, matching threshold — all matched to a small, closed population where distinguishing a few hundred or thousand candidates is a genuinely easier problem than distinguishing one face among billions worldwide, which is what most face-recognition headlines are actually about.

## A Checklist for When the Case Gets Stronger — or Flips Entirely

It's worth stress-testing the condo gate's conclusion against genuinely different kinds of images, because the factors that made a CNN the sensible choice there don't all point the same direction everywhere. Four things tend to determine which way the case tips: how much labeled data is actually available, whether there's a hard real-time latency budget, whether the task depends on relating *distant* regions of the input to each other, and whether the system needs to reuse one backbone across several tasks or data types at once.

**Medical imaging — a genuine trade-off, not a clean win.** Take a chest X-ray. Several factors favor Transformers here: a radiologist's read often depends on weighing a finding in one lung against the shape of the heart or the position of the diaphragm clear across the frame — a long-range relational pattern attention is built to catch directly rather than build up through many local layers. Diagnostically important signal is also often faint and diffuse — early nodules, subtle opacities — rather than sharp-edged, which plays against a CNN's strength at crisp local features. And there's no embedded-chip latency constraint; a radiology read can run on a proper GPU, not a gate box. But the case doesn't tip cleanly, because labeled medical data is scarce and expensive to produce — it needs expert annotation, and rare conditions are, by definition, rare in any dataset — and Transformers tend to need more data than CNNs to reach the same accuracy. That's exactly why the field's actual answer here is mostly hybrid: CNN layers for cheap, data-efficient low-level feature extraction, with attention layered on top for the long-range reasoning step.

**Satellite and aerial imagery — the data problem disappears.** A single satellite image can span thousands of pixels, and a lot of what matters spans huge distances within the frame — whether a river system connects to one thirty kilometers away, whether a road network forms a coherent grid, whether a deforestation pattern is part of a larger regional trend. That's the same long-range case as the X-ray, but here the data-scarcity problem that held Transformers back in medicine doesn't apply: there's an enormous and growing supply of satellite imagery, much of it unlabeled, which is exactly the setting where a Transformer can be pretrained at scale on the unlabeled bulk and fine-tuned on a much smaller labeled task. No embedded-latency constraint either. This is close to an unambiguous win.

**Multi-camera fusion, as in autonomous driving.** A self-driving car isn't reasoning about one image — it's fusing input from several cameras, often with lidar and radar, covering different and overlapping fields of view, into one coherent understanding of the scene. That's fundamentally a "relate distant, differently-sourced information to each other" problem: a token doesn't have to represent a patch from one image, it can represent a patch from any sensor, with the model learning which piece of information from which source matters for which part of the scene. Data is abundant — millions of hours of driving footage — and while there is a real latency constraint here, unlike the condo gate the stakes justify the extra engineering effort to hit it, which is part of why some autonomous-driving companies have moved away from separate per-camera CNN pipelines stitched together afterward, toward Transformer-based fusion that lets the cameras inform each other directly.

**Image and text together — an obvious win almost by construction.** The moment a task requires an image and text to inform each other — captioning a photo, answering a question about what's in it, generating an image from a written description — you need one architecture that can represent both as a shared sequence of tokens attending to each other. A CNN has no natural mechanism for a text token and an image patch to talk to one another; it was built for one modality. This is why current multimodal AI — image generators, vision-language models — is built on Transformers almost without exception. It's less that Transformers won this particular fight, and more that the fight can't really be fought with a CNN in the first place.

Line these four cases up against the condo gate and a pattern falls out: massive or fast-growing datasets, no hard millisecond-scale latency budget, relationships that genuinely span distant parts of the input, and — for the multimodal case especially — a need to unify fundamentally different kinds of data in one system. Whenever those line up, the case for a Transformer stops being "probably worth the extra cost" and starts being closer to "there isn't really a competitive alternative." When none of them line up — small closed population, tight latency budget, information that's mostly local, one narrow task — you get the condo gate, and a CNN remains the sensible default.

## What "Upgrading" the Gate Actually Improves

If a vendor announces an upgrade, the useful question is: upgrade to what, and improving what, exactly? A few concrete things can get better, worth separating from each other. Fewer false rejects and false accepts — a better-trained model produces embeddings that are more stable across irrelevant variation (lighting, angle, glasses, aging) while staying sensitive to what actually distinguishes one person from another. Better handling of hard cases — twins, masks, low light, spoofing attempts like a photo held up to the camera — exactly the long tail where older or smaller models tend to fail. A speed-versus-accuracy tradeoff, not a free win — swapping a well-tuned CNN for a naive Transformer could mean slower response for the same accuracy, or the same speed for better accuracy, depending what the vendor optimized for, which is exactly why hybrids exist. And potentially less enrollment fuss, since a better-trained model may need fewer registration photos to build a solid embedding, having already learned from a large training set what tends to matter about a face and what doesn't.

What does *not* change with an upgrade: nothing about the policy layer sitting on top of the model — how close counts as "close enough," what happens on a failed match, whether there's a human fallback. Those are business decisions, not neural-network improvements, and a vendor's "upgrade" can improve one dimension while leaving another unchanged or even slightly worse.

## Why a Software Developer Should Care

The CNN-to-Transformer story isn't just an architecture debate — it's a big part of why the current AI moment looks the way it does for people building software, and the condo gate's mundane reliability is a useful corrective to the hype.

**One architecture, many domains.** Before Transformers, an image problem and a text problem called for genuinely different toolkits. A Transformer doesn't inherently care whether its tokens came from words, image patches, or audio — the core mechanism doesn't change. That's a big part of why one wave of tooling swept across nearly every part of the software stack at once, instead of arriving domain by domain over decades.

**The scaling behavior that made "foundation models" possible.** Because Transformers parallelize so well and scale so predictably, it became feasible to train enormous, general-purpose models once, at huge cost, and reuse them across countless downstream tasks — the direct ancestor of the "pretrained model plus your own data" pattern, and, fittingly, of the gate's own already-trained-model-plus-enrollment design.

**Embeddings as a shared currency.** Both CNNs and Transformers ultimately produce the same kind of output at their core: a vector representing "meaning" — of a face, a sentence, a code snippet. That shared currency is what makes similarity search, retrieval, and the broader ecosystem built on embeddings possible across such different kinds of data.

**And, perhaps most usefully: bigger and newer is not automatically better for your specific problem.** The Transformer's real advantages — long-range relationships, scale, reuse across tasks — are genuine, but they come with a genuine cost: quadratic attention, heavier compute, more engineering effort to squeeze onto constrained hardware. That's not a knock against Transformers. It's a reminder that architecture should follow the actual constraints of the problem, not the direction of the hype cycle.

## Back to the Gate

Does your condominium's gate use a CNN? Possibly — a fast, reliable system on modest embedded hardware makes it a reasonable guess — but it could just as easily be a lightweight Vision Transformer or a hybrid, and the gate's behavior alone can't tell you which. What matters more than the label is what we now know about the whole system underneath it: a model trained once, far upstream, by an organization with a large dataset and real compute; shipped frozen to the gate; enrolled with your face through a process that involves no training at all; running entirely on local hardware with no need for the internet; performing one-to-one verification rather than the harder identification problem; and tuned, deliberately, for a small, closed population where speed and reliability matter more than the kind of long-range, massive-scale accuracy a Transformer is built to chase.

The gate doesn't need the most fashionable architecture. It needs the architecture that fits its constraints. CNNs assume useful information is mostly local, and build understanding gradually, layer by layer, from a small neighborhood outward, at a cost that stays cheap and predictable. Transformers assume any two pieces of information might matter to each other regardless of distance, and pay a real, structural cost — that quadratic attention bill — to let everything talk to everything else directly. Neither assumption is universally correct. The AI revolution didn't retire one of these approaches in favor of the other; it expanded the menu, and left the actual choice, as always, to whoever is closest to the problem's real constraints.

That trade — expensive and flexible versus efficient and locally biased — is one of the central trades running underneath the entire AI revolution in software development. Once you can see it in something as small as a gate lifting for your face, it's hard not to notice it running through nearly everything else built on top of these two architectures.
