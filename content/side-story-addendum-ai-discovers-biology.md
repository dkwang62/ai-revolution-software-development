# Can AI Discover Biology Beyond Human Intuition?

## Randomness is not the same as an absence of structure

Start with something that sounds like a contradiction and isn't. Biology is deeply probabilistic — at the molecular level, almost nothing happens the same way twice. Two copies of the same gene, sitting in two genetically identical cells, can be switched on at different moments, by different amounts, in response to the random jostling of individual molecules bumping into each other. No two human embryos develop through exactly the same sequence of molecular events. And yet a human embryo reliably becomes a recognizable human being, with the same basic organs, in the same basic arrangement, doing the same basic things, over and over, billions of times, across every pregnancy that has ever gone to term.

That combination — real randomness at the smallest scale, real reliability at the level that actually matters — is the thing worth sitting with before anything else in this chapter. Genes, cellular machinery, regulatory networks, physical law, feedback loops, and evolution itself all act together as a kind of harness. They don't eliminate the underlying variability. They channel it, so that an enormous space of microscopic possibility keeps landing, again and again, inside a comparatively narrow range of viable outcomes. Randomness, in other words, is not the same thing as an absence of structure. Wherever outcomes are reliable despite the noise underneath them, there's a hidden regularity doing the work — and wherever there's regularity, there's something a learning system, human or machine, can in principle discover.

## The question Jennifer Doudna is asking

That question isn't hypothetical anymore, and it isn't only being asked by AI researchers. It's being asked, with real skepticism, by one of the scientists most qualified to have an opinion about it.

Jennifer Doudna shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing CRISPR-Cas9 as a tool for editing genomes — arguably the single most consequential piece of biology done in the last two decades. In a Bloomberg interview in mid-2026, she pushed back directly on the idea that a system like ChatGPT might soon deserve credit for a drug discovery in the way a human scientist would. Her doubt isn't a reflexive dismissal of AI in her field — she's been publicly enthusiastic about AI's role in accelerating parts of the work, and has described the current moment in gene editing and medicine as an era where breakthroughs in biology are colliding with the power of new AI tools. Her skepticism is more specific than that. It's about whether AI can actually replace the particular thing she did.

It's worth remembering what that thing actually was, because the story cuts against the image of discovery as a planned, well-funded search for a target. CRISPR's origin traces back to an ordinary conversation at a campus café at Berkeley, where a colleague described something odd: bacteria seemed to have a kind of adaptive immune system, a way of "remembering" viruses that had attacked them before. That observation had nothing to do with gene editing on the face of it — Doudna's lab followed it because it was interesting, not because anyone had a plan to build a medical technology out of it. Only later did it become clear that the same bacterial mechanism, originally discovered in organisms as mundane as the ones used to culture yogurt, could be redirected to make precise, programmable edits to DNA in plants, animals, and eventually humans. Human curiosity noticed a strange detail, followed it for its own sake, and only in retrospect turned out to have found something enormous.

That's the pattern Doudna's caution is really about. She isn't only asking whether a machine can process biological data faster than a person. She's asking whether the specific thing that turned an odd bacterial detail into a revolutionary medical technology — a scientist's judgment about which strange, unglamorous observation is actually worth chasing — is something that has to stay human.

## The alternative hypothesis

Here's the version of the argument that pushes back, and it's worth taking seriously on its own terms rather than dismissing it as AI boosterism.

Human scientists discover biology by noticing a tiny subset of its patterns — the fraction of everything happening inside a cell or an organism that a human researcher happens to be positioned, equipped, and lucky enough to see — and then designing experiments to work out what those patterns actually mean. There's nothing in that description that logically requires a human nervous system to be the thing doing the noticing. It requires attention, memory, the ability to spot a regularity buried in noise, and the judgment to test it. None of those are exclusively human capacities anymore, even if they used to be.

What an AI system could plausibly do that no individual human researcher ever could is examine an almost unimaginably larger slice of biology at once — genomes, protein structures, cellular imaging, gene expression data, epigenetic markers, drug responses, patient outcomes, and the results of experimental interventions, all cross-referenced simultaneously, across millions or billions of individual observations, spanning far more dimensions than a human mind can hold in working memory at the same time. Some genuinely important biological relationships may exist at exactly that scale — patterns real enough to matter, but too spread out, too high-dimensional, or too faint in any single dataset for a person to ever notice by eye, no matter how curious or well-trained they are.

If that's true, the advantage an AI system might eventually have over a scientist like Doudna wouldn't necessarily be more curiosity, or better judgment about which oddities are worth chasing. It could be something more basic than that: the capacity to perceive biological structure that no human scientist, however brilliant, is positioned to perceive in the first place, simply because the pattern never fits inside a single human's field of view.

## From noticing to discovering

Noticing a pattern isn't the same as discovering something, and this is where the argument has to get more careful rather than less. A system that only ever spots correlations in existing data hasn't done what Doudna's lab did. It has to go further: from a pattern, to some internal representation of what that pattern might mean, to a testable hypothesis, to an actual intervention — changing something on purpose — to a measured consequence, to a genuine sense of cause and effect, to a new concept that wasn't there before, and only then to something worth calling a discovery. Skip any of the later steps and what's left is pattern-matching dressed up as insight.

This is the same distinction the earlier chapter on virtual cells and machine code kept returning to: prediction is not causation, and pattern-matching is not understanding. It applies here with the same force. An AI system might notice an unexplained relationship buried in a mountain of genomic and cellular data — something correlating with something else in a way nobody had flagged before — and propose a specific intervention to test it: suppress this gene, block this protein, expose these cells to this compound. If the intervention is actually run, and the result actually measured, and the model actually updates its understanding based on what happened, and then chooses the next experiment based on what it just learned, that's no longer a system reading papers faster than a human could. It's a system running something close to the actual scientific method, on its own initiative, at a scale and speed no laboratory staffed entirely by people could ever match.

Whether that loop, run at enormous scale, could carry AI from being an assistant that helps scientists work faster to something closer to an independent scientific discoverer in its own right is genuinely the open question here — not a settled one, and not one this chapter is trying to settle either.

## The real target isn't prediction. It's structure.

There's a subtlety worth being precise about, because it's easy to set the bar for "AI does biology" impossibly high by accident. AI doesn't need to predict every individual random molecular event happening inside a cell to be doing something scientifically important. Nobody does that, including the best human biologists alive — that's exactly the kind of microscopic noise the biological harness described earlier exists to absorb, not something anyone needs to track in full.

The more useful target isn't "can AI predict everything that happens inside a cell." It's: can AI discover the hidden structures that constrain which outcomes are even possible, or likely, in the first place — the regulatory logic, the feedback loops, the constraints that turn an ocean of molecular randomness into a comparatively narrow, repeatable set of outcomes. If a system can find those constraints, the underlying randomness stops being an obstacle to machine discovery. It just becomes one more part of the structure the system has to learn — no more mysterious, in principle, than any other layer of biology sitting underneath a pattern worth explaining.

## What abstraction is for

Before going further, it helps to ask a question that has nothing to do with biology at first: why do humans use matrices?

Nobody encounters a matrix in nature. A matrix is an invention — a representation humans built because it makes certain kinds of structure manageable for a human mind and, later, for a human-designed computer. Reality doesn't come pre-organized into rows and columns. People imposed that organization on it because it let them do useful things: solve systems of equations, describe rotations, and eventually train neural networks. Every mathematical abstraction humans use — vectors, tensors, probability distributions, the layered architecture of a neural network itself — followed the same path. Reality came first. A human-shaped abstraction came second, built specifically so that human minds, and the machines humans went on to build, could hold and manipulate the structure underneath.

Many of the mathematical abstractions humans use to describe and manipulate reality — vectors, tensors, and probability distributions among them — serve the same basic purpose: they make structure a human mind, or a human-designed machine, can actually work with.

Today's AI systems inherit that whole toolkit. They learn using matrices, gradients, and tensors because those are the representations their human designers gave them to learn with. That raises a question worth pausing on: if an AI system had been free to invent its own representations from scratch, unconstrained by what makes sense to a human mind, would it have arrived at matrices at all? Perhaps. Matrices are useful for reasons that don't depend on who's using them. But perhaps a system building its own representational language, optimized only for its own kind of learning, would have found something else entirely — something with no clean name, no textbook chapter, no human equivalent.

If that's plausible for mathematics, the same question applies to biology with more force, not less. Genes, pathways, and regulatory networks are also human abstractions — useful ones, hard-won ones, but abstractions all the same, built by scientists working within the limits of human perception and human notation. An AI system doesn't have to think about computation using matrices any more than it has to think about biology using genes and pathways. It could, in principle, build its own internal representation of what's happening inside a cell — one that carves up biological reality along lines no human vocabulary currently has words for.

## What a wrong answer taught the field

There's a moment early in the history of neural networks worth revisiting here, not for what it seemed to prove at the time but for what it turned out to actually mean.

In the late 1960s, researchers showed that a simple one-layer neural network couldn't learn the XOR function — a basic logical pattern where the output flips depending on a particular combination of two inputs. The result became emblematic of the limitations of early neural networks and helped cool enthusiasm for that approach.

Looked at now, the actual lesson is closer to the opposite. The one-layer network wasn't incapable of representing the XOR pattern because neural networks are fundamentally limited. It was incapable because that particular architecture didn't have the representational machinery the pattern required. Give the network a hidden layer with a nonlinear activation — a way to build an internal representation of the input rather than a purely linear one — and the same problem that looked impossible becomes straightforward.

The general point buried in that history is worth carrying forward: a limitation of a particular representation is not necessarily a limitation of intelligence itself. When today's AI systems struggle with a piece of frontier mathematics, a subtle biological mechanism, or a genuinely novel scientific abstraction, that tells us something true and specific about what current systems, using current representations and current training methods, cannot yet reliably do. It doesn't settle the separate and much larger question of what machine intelligence, given room to build its own representations, ultimately cannot do.

## The scientist in the black box

Put the abstraction question and the XOR lesson together, and a genuinely stranger possibility comes into view — one that goes further than either of the two pictures of future science sketched so far.

Imagine an AI system studying cells discovers an internal structure. Call it X. Researchers, naturally, ask what X is. And suppose the honest answer is that there isn't a good translation — not because anyone is being cagey, but because X was never built out of the vocabulary human biology uses. It doesn't map cleanly onto a gene, a pathway, a protein complex, or any combination of them that a textbook would recognize.

What X does instead is generate predictions. Suppress this gene, alter that protein, introduce this compound — and a specific outcome follows. The experiment is run. The outcome happens. Another prediction follows, and another intervention is run, and the outcome happens again. And again.

At that point a genuinely uncomfortable question becomes hard to avoid: must scientific knowledge be human-understandable to count as knowledge at all?

The usual answer bundles three things together without much scrutiny — understanding, prediction, and verification travel as a package, because for the whole history of science they've had no choice but to. A human being had to understand a mechanism well enough to predict its behavior, and then reality either confirmed or refuted that prediction. AI raises the possibility of separating those three for the first time: a machine-native representation that humans cannot follow, producing predictions humans can still test, verified the same way every scientific claim has always been verified — by seeing whether the world actually does what was predicted.

If that separation is real, it changes what a human's role in the process has to be. Humans may not need to follow a representation to establish that its consequences are true. They only need the capacity this chapter has already leaned on once, in the discussion of noticing versus discovering: the discipline of insisting on an actual intervention and an actual measured consequence, rather than accepting a pattern on faith. That discipline doesn't require understanding the black box. It only requires refusing to trust it until reality has weighed in. And that discipline cuts both ways: when a prediction fails, the machine has to revise its representation, exactly as a human theory has to change when an experiment contradicts it. Reality itself becomes the referee — the one authority in this picture that doesn't care whether the reasoning that produced a prediction is expressible in a language any human happens to speak.

## A third model of future science

The two pictures of future science described earlier in this chapter — one where a human stays at the center of every discovery, using AI as a fast assistant, and one where an AI system runs the full discovery loop but still produces something recognizable as scientific understanding for people to interpret — turn out not to be the only two possibilities. There's a third. The human is no longer necessarily inside the explanatory loop at all. The machine develops its own representation, uses it to generate testable interventions, and reality determines whether those predictions survive. The representation itself may never become human-understandable.

The machine doesn't become a faster, tireless version of Jennifer Doudna, arriving eventually at the same kind of understanding she reached from that café conversation about bacterial immunity. It becomes something with no real predecessor in the history of science — a source of verified, repeatedly confirmed knowledge that nobody, including the people who built the system, can translate into a concept a human mind can hold.

## Two questions, and which one matters more

This reframes the disagreement this chapter opened with. The question isn't only whether AI can reproduce the kind of scientific intuition Doudna has spent a career developing — the judgment to notice which strange, unglamorous observation is worth chasing, and to hold onto it long enough to find out where it leads. That's still an open and important question. But sitting underneath it is a harder one: does scientific understanding have to be human-understandable at all, if a machine-native representation can repeatedly generate novel, testable predictions that survive controlled intervention and independent verification?

Nobody knows the answer yet, and this chapter isn't going to pretend otherwise. But it's worth being honest about where the two questions lead if the second one turns out to matter more than the first.

Perhaps the ultimate AI scientist will not become better at seeing biology the way Jennifer Doudna sees it. Perhaps it will see biology in a way no Jennifer Doudna — or any other human being — could see at all. Its internal representations may have no clean equivalents in human biology or mathematics. Nobody may ever be able to explain why they work. But if those representations keep producing predictions that survive experiment after experiment, reality itself becomes the referee, whether or not any human in the room understands what's being refereed.

The deepest question this chapter has to leave open, then, is no longer only whether AI can reproduce human scientific intuition. It's whether human intuition represents the outer limit of scientific understanding — or whether it was only ever the first kind of intelligence through which nature happened, for a while, to be understood.
