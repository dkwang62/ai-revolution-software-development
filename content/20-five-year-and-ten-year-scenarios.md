The future described in this chapter does not depend on one all-powerful model. It depends on several forces improving together: capability, cost, memory, tools, verification, physical infrastructure, and institutions.

These are conditional scenarios, not promises or timetables. They ask what may become possible if the cost of a dependable completed task keeps falling—and what could prevent the benefits from spreading.

## A Glimpse of Abundant Intelligence

> **Why it matters:** Cheap generation creates opportunity only when evidence and authority still govern what may be done.

Imagine two tasks in this possible future.

A teacher notices that one student understands spoken explanations but struggles with written exercises. She describes the difficulty, supplies examples of the student's work, and asks for a two-week experiment. An AI system creates a small learning tool, checks it against the school's privacy rules, and adapts it as results arrive. The software may be discarded when it has served its purpose.

At a factory, a team wants to reduce waste from one production line. AI analyses sensor records, proposes a process change, generates supporting software, and prepares a bounded trial. Nothing touches the machinery until safety rules are satisfied and an authorised person approves it.

The contrast establishes the principle. AI makes both proposals cheaper. The cost of trust remains different. A temporary learning tool can tolerate rapid iteration; a physical system demands stronger evidence, permissions, and professional judgement.

Abundant intelligence means that expertise and creation are no longer as tightly constrained by the number of specialists available. It does not mean a world without work, disagreement, failure, or responsibility.

## Abundance Means Falling Task Cost

> **Why it matters:** Transformational change can arrive through steady reductions in the cost of useful work without infinite energy or intelligence.

Abundant intelligence does not mean free computation. It means that the total cost of a useful result falls below the value of many more tasks.

That total is larger than a model's advertised token price:

```text
model computation
+ reasoning
+ context and memory
+ tools and integration
+ verification and correction
+ expected cost of failure
= cost per dependable completed task
```

A cheap answer that requires hours of professional repair is expensive intelligence. A more costly agent that completes the work, supplies evidence, and survives checking may be economical.

Efficiency can lower the cost of one task while total energy demand still grows because usage expands. The International Energy Agency reported in 2026 that energy use per AI task had been falling rapidly, while forecasting that global data-centre electricity demand would more than double by 2030 and demand from AI-focused data centres would more than triple. [IEA, _Key Questions on Energy and AI_](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

This is how technological abundance usually spreads. Lower cost releases new demand; demand attracts investment in processors, memory, data centres, power generation, and grids. The path does not require nuclear fusion. It requires continuing engineering improvement and large-scale construction using available technologies.

## The Obstacles Are the Road Map

> **Why it matters:** Today's weaknesses show where technical, organisational, and institutional work must concentrate.

Five barriers stand between impressive demonstrations and widespread dependable intelligence.

### 1. Capability Must Remain Coherent

AI can succeed at a short, clear task and still lose the thread during a long project. It may repeat a failed approach, follow the easiest interpretation, or declare success too early.

Progress therefore involves more than improving one model. Systems must divide work into stages, preserve state, gather evidence, use specialised tools, and escalate when confidence is insufficient.

### 2. Reliability Must Be Engineered

Society already relies on imperfect people and machines because systems detect and contain error. AI can likewise be surrounded by tests, mathematical checks, simulations, permission rules, audit trails, independent review, and human approval.

The model proposes. Deterministic software checks what can be checked. People retain authority where consequences demand it.

### 3. Intelligence Needs the Right Context

A general model does not arrive knowing a child's learning history, a hospital's procedures, or why an old bank routine changes at quarter-end. Organisations must keep their knowledge current, connect it to sources, retrieve what matters, and control who may use it.

This is the practical work of the [[19-enterprise-intelligence-layer|Enterprise Intelligence Layer]].

### 4. The Physical World Moves More Slowly

Software can be copied almost without cost. Food, houses, medicines, and machines still require materials, energy, factories, and transport. A mistake in a document may be reversible; a mistake involving a vehicle or surgical tool may not be.

Physical progress need not wait for a universal humanoid robot. Specialised laboratory equipment, warehouse vehicles, agricultural systems, inspection devices, and machines designed for controlled processes can spread first.

### 5. Abundance Must Be Distributed

Even if intelligence becomes cheap to produce, access may remain concentrated among organisations that own processors, models, data centres, energy, or valuable data.

Competition, public infrastructure, education, open technologies, regulation, and business models will influence who benefits. Technology can expand what society produces. It cannot decide how gains are shared.

The road map can be summarised in four sentences:

> **Technology determines capability. Cost determines diffusion. Verification determines trust. Institutions determine distribution.**

## Signals Already Pointing Forward

> **Why it matters:** A scenario is more credible when research and investment are already attacking its necessary constraints.

Several visible directions support the scenarios without proving them.

Researchers are exploring alternatives and complements to standard attention for long sequences and persistent memory. State-space models such as [Mamba](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752) and memory research such as Google's [Titans](https://research.google/pubs/titans-learning-to-memorize-at-test-time/) suggest that future systems may combine attention, recurrence, and learned memory rather than depending on one mechanism alone.

Reasoning is becoming adaptive: simple tasks can receive fast, inexpensive treatment while difficult tasks receive more planning, competing attempts, tools, and checks. Computing platforms are also being designed as coordinated systems of processors, high-bandwidth memory, networking, and storage rather than as one isolated faster chip.

Coding agents provide an early commercial signal. They increasingly work across files, builds, tests, screenshots, and deployment rather than stopping at an isolated answer. This does not establish a ten-year forecast. It shows the direction of travel from model responses towards completed, checked workflows.

## Five Years: Intelligence Becomes Ordinary Infrastructure

> **Why it matters:** The most consequential AI may become less visible as it becomes part of routine work.

By approximately 2031, people may increasingly begin software projects by describing behaviour, supplying examples, and defining tests. Agents will work across files, tools, and existing systems. Routine tasks will use inexpensive models; difficult or consequential work will receive more computation and supervision.

Inside organisations, more work may begin with a goal rather than with one named application. An employee might ask an approved agent to identify conflicting customer records, show the required approvals, and prepare changes for review. The agent would use existing customer, document, and reporting systems as tools. The underlying model could change while the organisation's knowledge, permissions, and records remained under its control.

Early gains are likely where work can already be verified: code maintenance, documentation, customer support, compliance evidence, analysis, and bounded administrative processes. Legacy modernisation may matter because AI can help recover knowledge from systems no one fully understands.

Professional engineers will remain essential, while more of their work moves towards architecture, integration, security, and verification. Domain experts will initiate more projects themselves. The boundary between user and builder will blur.

Adoption will remain uneven. A personal tool may be created in hours; a change to a medical or payment system may still require months of evidence and approval. The model can be identical while the cost of trust differs completely.

## Ten Years: Intention Becomes an Interface

> **Why it matters:** The deeper trajectory is towards systems that combine memory, tools, and checking around models—not one magical replacement for every technology.

By approximately 2036, an AI system may combine several models, persistent memory, specialised reasoning, and tools operating at different costs. Attention may remain one component rather than defining the whole system.

An organisation may keep its accounting platform, customer system, document store, factory controls, and specialist applications because each still performs valuable work. People may increasingly begin with an agent that interprets the goal and coordinates approved action across those systems.

The important chain is:

```text
human intention
↓
AI interpretation and planning
↓
software instructions and tool use
↓
tests, permissions, and verification
↓
action by digital or physical systems
```

Computing began by forcing people to express intention in the language of the machine. This chain would allow machines to meet people closer to the language of human purpose.

Software is the first major case because it is already made of information. Science, administration, and carefully bounded physical systems could follow. The change would not require consciousness or an all-knowing genie. It would require intelligence that is capable, affordable, and controlled well enough to coordinate existing technologies.

## Where the Story Can Branch

> **Why it matters:** Capability and the cost of trust can improve at different rates, producing very different futures.

|                                   | Verification and integration remain expensive                                                                   | Verification and integration become much cheaper                                                              |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Capability improves gradually** | **Bounded assistant:** AI remains valuable in well-specified, low-risk tasks, with people carrying most review. | **Ordinary infrastructure:** AI becomes routine across much of software and knowledge work.                   |
| **Capability improves rapidly**   | **Generation outruns trust:** output grows, but security, maintenance, and coherence remain bottlenecks.        | **Agentic organisation:** intent-driven creation and coordinated work become plausible under human authority. |

Different industries and countries may occupy different cells at the same time. The path to abundance is unlikely to be one dramatic crossing. It will be a succession of tasks becoming cheap and dependable enough to move.

## Beyond Ten Years: At the Gates of Abundance

If these trends continue, intelligence may become an ordinary ingredient of education, medicine, science, government, business, and personal life, much as computation became an ingredient of almost everything.

Software could become more personal and temporary. Expertise could be available on demand. Small teams could command capabilities once reserved for large organisations. Specialised machines could carry more intelligence into laboratories, factories, farms, transport, and care.

This is not paradise. Abundant answers can make truth scarcer. Abundant content can consume attention. Abundant software can create disorder. Productive power can be captured by those who own the infrastructure.

The same technology can widen human agency or concentrate control. We need cheaper computation, more electricity, and better grids—not infinite energy. We need reliable systems—not an infallible mind. We need useful physical automation—not a perfect servant. We need institutions capable of spreading opportunity—not a change in the laws of physics.

For most of history, humanity has been constrained by what it could produce. In an age of abundant intelligence, we may increasingly be constrained by what we can wisely choose.

Technology may bring us to the gates of abundance. It cannot decide what kind of world lies beyond them.
