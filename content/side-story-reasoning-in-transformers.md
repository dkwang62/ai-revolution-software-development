# Where Does "Reasoning" Actually Happen in a Transformer?

*This continues from a companion essay on how attention and layered representation turn a prompt into a coherent, generated answer. That essay left one thing open: where does reasoning — tracing logic, catching a mistake, working through a hard problem step by step — actually fit into that picture? This essay is the answer.*

Hand a coding assistant a large, messy program and ask it to find a subtle bug, and it might pause, trace through several parts of the code, form a theory, check it, discard it, and try another before finding the real problem. Ask it to fix an obvious typo, and it answers instantly. Some products even let you choose a setting — low, medium, high reasoning — before you've asked anything at all. It's tempting to picture a separate "reasoning module" switching on for hard problems. That picture is wrong: reasoning isn't a distinct piece of logic bolted onto the side of the language-generating part. It emerges from the same mechanism that produces ordinary text — repeated attention and transformation, layer after layer. What changes between a typo fix and a hard bug hunt isn't which machinery gets used. It's how much of that same machinery gets put to work before the system commits to an answer.

## Watching Reasoning Happen, One Layer at a Time

Imagine a sign-up form: after someone signs up, the program looks up their new account to send a welcome email — but sometimes crashes, because for a brief moment right after sign-up the account hasn't fully finished being created, so the lookup comes back empty, and the email step uses that empty result anyway. A person spots this almost instantly. Inside a Transformer, something structured produces that same conclusion. Early layers, working on the code as tokens, notice that the account gets looked up in one place and used again a few lines later — two separate facts. Deeper layers use that shared variable as a bridge, connecting "this lookup can come back empty" to "this result gets used right after" into one concern: nothing checks in between. Only in later layers does the actual question — "why does this crash sometimes?" — get folded in, producing a well-supported answer. Nobody wrote a rule for this; it emerged from training. But the shape is genuinely structured: facts, then connected concerns, then an answer to the actual question — the same zooming intuition from earlier in this series, applied to code instead of pictures or paragraphs.

## The Two Workers Inside Every Layer

Every layer does two jobs. **Attention** asks: *what else, out of everything available, is relevant to what I'm looking at right now?* — reaching back to notice where the account came from, and whether anything checked it. **The MLP** (multi-layer perceptron, also called the feed-forward network) asks: *now that I have this, what should I make of it?* — turning "this can be empty" plus "this gets used here" into something closer to "this is why it crashes." Attention gathers; the MLP interprets. The simplest way to hold it: attention is LOOK, the MLP is THINK — and a Transformer isn't LOOK-THINK once, it's LOOK, THINK, LOOK, THINK, dozens or hundreds of times, each layer nudging an already-richer understanding a bit further rather than solving the whole problem alone.

## Two Different Kinds of Reasoning, Easy to Conflate

There are really two reasoning scales, easy to mix up. The first happens *within* a single pass — the LOOK-THINK cycle across layers, entirely in numbers, invisible to you. The second happens *across* generated tokens: modern reasoning models write out intermediate steps — "the account gets looked up right after sign-up — that lookup can fail if the account isn't fully created yet — the email step uses that same result two lines later — nothing checks in between — that's the cause" — and each clause, once written, becomes context for the next. This is a scratchpad: write a step, treat it as part of the problem, write the next step in light of it. It's why more room to "think" genuinely helps on hard problems — not because the model gets smarter mid-conversation, but because each step becomes solid material to build the next deduction on, rather than holding everything in working memory at once.

One honest caveat: it's natural to read the scratchpad as a transparent window into the model's actual thinking, but that's not guaranteed. Generating plausible-looking steps is itself just another act of prediction. A model can write a confident chain that doesn't fully match why it landed on its answer, or reach the right answer for a reason its own scratchpad never mentions. The trace is real, useful computation — it does shape what comes next — but it's a helpful approximation of the model's reasoning, not a guaranteed complete account of it.

## What "Reasoning Effort" Actually Controls

Low, medium, high reasoning settings aren't switching between different levels of intelligence — they control how much computation the system spends before committing to an answer. Picture three people given the same bug report: at "low," someone skims, spots something plausible, and proposes a fix unchecked; at "medium," they trace the actual sequence the report describes; at "high," they trace multiple possible causes, check each against the evidence, discard a theory that doesn't quite fit, and only then settle on a verified fix. Same person, same skill — what changes is how much of it gets used. The underlying Transformer machinery never changes between settings; what changes is how long a scratchpad the system generates and feeds back into its own context. That's also why maxing out reasoning effort on an obvious typo wastes computation with nothing to trace or verify, while pointing it at a tangled, intermittent bug gives it something real to do.

More effort isn't a safe default even on hard problems, though — it isn't "more can only help, at worst it's wasted." A longer scratchpad also gives a model more chances to talk itself out of a correct early instinct or drift down an unproductive tangent. And this behavior isn't automatic just because a model is asked to "think harder": models with an effort dial have typically gone through additional training specifically aimed at producing good step-by-step reasoning, often checked against problems with a verifiable answer. The effort setting unlocks a model separately trained to use a longer scratchpad well — not just any model given more room to talk.

## Then Why Not Just Let the Model Decide?

A capable model can often judge difficulty on its own — "fix this typo" needs little, "why does this fail only for some customers sometimes" needs a lot. So why expose a manual dial at all? Because difficulty isn't the only thing at stake — quality, speed, and cost pull against each other, and a longer scratchpad means real added time and, on most paid systems, real added cost per request. A model might correctly judge a bug deserves serious effort, while you, mid-experiment, would rather have a rough guess in ten seconds for less. Or a request might look simple on the page — "is it safe to remove this check?" — while you know the code handles payments, where a wrong answer is expensive, and you're willing to pay for a careful check even though the wording gives no hint of the stakes. The manual control is closer to a camera's auto mode than an engineers-only setting: automatic is usually right, but when you know something the system doesn't, an override lets you say so directly. Most ordinary use shouldn't need it at all.

## What Reasoning Effort Is Really Buying You

It's tempting to call this "sharpening the context," but that's not quite right, especially for a bug that was fixed once and quietly comes back after the code gets reorganized. The code itself doesn't change with reasoning effort. What changes is how much the model manages to extract *from* it — with more computation, it has room to trace *why* the original fix worked, not just *that* it worked: which check caught the empty result, whether it survived the reorganization, whether it got moved or quietly dropped. A low-effort pass might just re-apply the original fix without asking why it stopped working — risking the same problem a third time. A higher-effort pass checks whether anything ever actually tested for this case, so it doesn't silently break again. Same code every time; more computation produces a sharper working understanding of it, and that's what makes a fix durable rather than temporary.

Reorganizing code adds a wrinkle debugging doesn't have: debugging converges toward one right answer, but restructuring often doesn't — several legitimate trade-offs between clarity, performance, and disruption can all be reasonable. Extra effort there mostly buys thorough verification that a restructuring preserves behavior, and wider exploration of options — not certainty about which single approach is best, which stays a judgment call no amount of computation resolves.

The most accurate definition, then: reasoning is the computation a model spends turning the information already in its context into a clear enough understanding to act on — tracing, connecting, checking, reconsidering, using the same attention-and-MLP machinery behind everything else in this series. Low effort gives it one quick pass. High effort gives it room to trace more than once and check whether the picture it's building actually holds together before it shows you the fix.

## Can the Tool Loop Do the Reasoning For You?

Coding assistants rarely reason alone — they can run the code, run the tests, see the actual error, and try again. So why pay for high reasoning effort at all, if a failing test will just send the model back for another attempt anyway? In one narrow case, that instinct is basically right: if the feedback is fast, cheap, and trustworthy — a compiler refusing to build, a test whose pass condition genuinely captures "correct" — a light attempt followed by a quick correction can reach the same place as one slower attempt, faster. The loop is quietly supplying the checking step higher effort would otherwise do internally.

But a failing test only tells you *that* something's still wrong, not *why*, and doesn't guarantee the next attempt understands any better. A low-effort pass on the sign-up bug might patch just enough to make one failing test pass — a check in that one spot — without tracing back to the real gap, that the account can come back empty in more places than this one. The test goes green; the underlying issue resurfaces the next time the code changes. Worse, a light attempt doesn't fail usefully at random — if it tends to overlook a certain kind of issue, it may make that same mistake on attempt one, three, and seven, patching the wrong thing each time rather than converging, because whether an attempt actually gets smarter depends on using the failure as evidence to reconsider — exactly what higher effort is for. And the loop only forces rigor where the feedback is complete; a lot of what makes a bug hard has no clean pass-or-fail signal at all — a silent behavior change nothing asserts against, two reasonable restructurings with no test able to say which is better. Extra attempts aren't free either: if five light-effort loops take longer overall than one careful medium-effort pass, nothing's been saved — the cost just moved from thinking to trial and error.

So: a tight, fast, trustworthy feedback loop really can substitute for a good chunk of what higher reasoning effort buys, and for narrow, well-covered problems, always-light-plus-retry is reasonable. But once a task is underspecified, feedback is incomplete, or getting it wrong quietly is expensive, "always set it light" stops being a free optimization and becomes a bet that repetition stumbles onto the right answer rather than the model reasoning its way there — a bet that tends to lose on exactly the problems that matter most.

## Pulling It Together

Nothing here is a separate reasoning engine. It's the same LOOK-THINK cycle — attention gathering, the MLP interpreting — repeated across layers for a single answer, and, for models trained to do it well, repeated again across a written scratchpad that folds each step back in as context for the next. A reasoning-effort setting doesn't change that machinery; it changes how long the scratchpad runs, at a real cost in time and money, with no guarantee it's used well or that its steps are a complete account of what actually happened. A surrounding tool loop can substitute for some of that effort when its feedback is fast and trustworthy, but it can just as easily paper over a shallow fix that happens to satisfy today's test. None of that makes reasoning less real — it just means it's worth seeing for what it is: more computation, spent more carefully, on the same machinery that produces everything else a Transformer writes.
