# Where Does "Reasoning" Actually Happen in a Transformer?

*This continues from a companion essay on how attention and layered representation turn a prompt into a coherent, generated answer. That essay left one thing open: where does reasoning — tracing logic, catching a mistake, working through a hard problem step by step — actually fit into that picture? This essay is the answer.*

Hand a modern AI coding assistant a large, messy program and ask it to find a subtle bug, and it might pause, trace through several parts of the code, form a theory, check that theory, discard it, and try another before finally pointing at the real problem. Ask it to fix an obvious typo, and it answers almost instantly. Some products even let you choose a setting — low, medium, high reasoning — before you've asked anything at all. It's natural to picture this as a separate "reasoning module" somewhere inside the system, a dedicated logic engine that switches on for hard problems and stays quiet for easy ones. That picture is wrong, and understanding why turns out to be one of the more useful things to know about how these tools actually work — not least because it explains why a slow, careful bug hunt and a two-second typo fix are, underneath, running on exactly the same machinery.

There is no separate reasoning component sitting inside a Transformer. Reasoning isn't a distinct piece of logic bolted onto the side of the language-generating part. It emerges from the exact same mechanism that produces ordinary text: repeated attention and transformation, layer after layer. What changes between fixing a typo and hunting a nasty bug isn't which machinery gets used. It's how much of that same machinery gets put to work before the system commits to an answer.

## Watching Reasoning Happen, One Layer at a Time

The clearest way to see this is with a small, concrete bug — one that doesn't require knowing how to code to follow. Imagine a website's sign-up form. When someone signs up, the program is supposed to look up their new account and send them a welcome email. Every so often, though, the program crashes with an error. The reason turns out to be this: right after someone signs up, the program goes to fetch their new account — but for a brief moment, that account hasn't fully finished being created yet, so the lookup comes back empty. A few lines later, the program tries to send a welcome email using that empty result, and because there's nothing there to send an email to, it crashes. A person reading through this recognizes the problem almost instantly — the lookup can come back empty, and nothing checks for that before trying to send the email. But it's worth slowing that instant down, because something genuinely structured is happening inside a Transformer to get there.

The code arrives as tokens — small chunks of text — each one starting out as a bare numerical representation, disconnected from the rest. In the layers nearest the input, attention does its most basic job: it notices that the account gets looked up in one place, and that the same account gets used to send an email a few lines later. At this stage, the network has essentially identified two separate facts: where the account comes from, and where it gets used.

Deeper layers do something a first pass couldn't. Because that same account shows up in both places, attention can use it as a bridge, connecting "this lookup can come back empty" to "this result gets used here, right after" — building, out of two separate facts, a single connected concern: nothing between the lookup and the email-sending step checks whether the lookup actually found anything. Only in later layers still does the actual question — "why does this crash sometimes?" — get folded in, letting the network relate the idea of "sometimes crashes" to the specific gap it just identified, and arrive at a well-supported answer: the account lookup can come back empty right after sign-up, and nothing checks for that before trying to email the account.

Nobody wrote a rule anywhere saying "when something might come back empty and gets used right after without a check, flag it." That behavior emerged from training, the same way everything else in these networks does. But the shape of what's happening is genuinely structured: small pieces of code turn into facts, facts get connected into a concern, and that concern gets related to the actual question being asked. It's the same zooming intuition from earlier in this series, applied to code instead of pictures or paragraphs — start in close on individual lines, and each layer pulls a bit further back until the shape of the actual bug comes into view.

## The Two Workers Inside Every Layer

Every Transformer layer is, at its core, doing two jobs in sequence, and it helps to think of them as two different workers with two different questions to ask.

The first worker is attention, and its question is simple: *what else, out of everything currently available, is relevant to what I'm looking at right now?* When the network is processing the line that sends the welcome email, attention is the part that reaches back and notices where the account came from a few lines earlier, and whether anything checked it along the way. Attention's whole job is gathering — pulling the right pieces of context together in one place, the way a person's eyes jump back up the page to check where something came from.

The second worker goes by two names that mean the same thing — the MLP, short for multi-layer perceptron, or the feed-forward network. Its question is different: *now that I have this information in front of me, what should I actually make of it?* Where attention gathers, the MLP interprets. It's the part of the layer that takes whatever attention just assembled — "this lookup can come back empty" plus "this result gets used right here" — and does something with it, transforming that raw connection into something closer to "this is probably why it crashes." If attention is the part of you that scrolls back up to check where something came from, the MLP is the part that actually concludes "ah, that's the bug" once you've found it.

The simplest way to hold onto this: attention is LOOK, the MLP is THINK. And a Transformer isn't LOOK-then-THINK once. It's LOOK, THINK, LOOK, THINK, LOOK, THINK — the exact same two-step cycle, repeated across dozens or even hundreds of layers, each one operating on an understanding the layer before it already made a little richer. No single layer has to spot the whole bug by itself. It just has to nudge the understanding slightly further along, and hand it off to the next layer to nudge again.

## Two Different Kinds of Reasoning, Easy to Conflate

There's a distinction worth being precise about here, because it's where a lot of the confusion around "reasoning models" actually comes from. There are really two different things happening at two different scales, and they're easy to mix up.

The first is reasoning *within* a single pass through the network — the LOOK-THINK-LOOK-THINK cycle just described, happening across the layers as the model works out how to produce its very next word. This kind of reasoning is invisible. It happens inside the machinery, entirely in numbers, and you never see it directly.

The second is reasoning *across* words the model has already generated. Modern reasoning models don't just produce a final answer directly. They generate intermediate reasoning — working steps, checks, partial conclusions, written out as plain sentences — and, crucially, that generated text gets fed back into the model as new context for producing whatever comes next. On the sign-up bug above, that might look like the model writing out, in effect: "the account gets looked up right after sign-up — that lookup can fail if the account isn't fully created yet — then the welcome email gets sent using that same result two lines later — and nothing in between checks whether the lookup actually succeeded — that's the cause." Each of those clauses, once written, becomes part of the context the next clause gets generated from. This gives the system something like a scratchpad. It writes down a step, then treats that step as part of the problem going forward, then writes the next step in light of it, and so on, gradually narrowing in on the bug the way a person talking themselves through unfamiliar code out loud might.

This second kind of reasoning is why giving a model more room to "think" can genuinely improve its performance on hard bugs. It isn't that the model becomes smarter mid-conversation. It's that each intermediate step becomes new material the model gets to reason from, the same way jotting down "the account lookup can come back empty" while debugging gives you something solid to build the next deduction on, rather than trying to hold the whole sequence of events in your head at once.

One honest caveat belongs here. It's natural to read a model's written-out scratchpad as a transparent window into what it's actually thinking — as if the numbered steps are a faithful transcript of the reasoning happening inside the network. That's not guaranteed. The scratchpad is text the model generates, and generating plausible-looking steps is itself just another act of prediction, the same LOOK-THINK cycle producing any other output. A model can write a clean, confident chain of reasoning that doesn't fully match why it actually landed on its answer, or land on the right answer for a reason its own scratchpad never mentions. The written trace is genuinely useful — it's real computation, and it does shape what comes next, which is why longer scratchpads tend to help — but it's worth treating it as a helpful approximation of the model's reasoning rather than a guaranteed, complete account of it.

## What "Reasoning Effort" Actually Controls

This brings us to those low, medium, and high reasoning settings some AI products expose. The natural assumption is that they're switching between fundamentally different kinds of intelligence — that "high" unlocks some smarter version of the model. That's not quite it. What they're actually controlling is far more mundane, and far more useful to understand correctly: how much computation the system is allowed to spend working through a problem before it commits to an answer.

Picture three people handed the same unfamiliar program and the same bug report. Told to work at "low" effort, someone skims the most likely spot, spots something that looks plausible, and proposes a fix without checking it further. At "medium," they trace the actual sequence of steps the bug report describes before proposing anything. At "high," they take their time — trace multiple possible causes, check each one against the actual behavior described in the report, catch that their first theory doesn't actually explain a detail in the report, discard it, and only then settle on a fix they've verified against the evidence. It's the same person in all three cases, with the same underlying skill. What changes is how much of that skill actually gets used on this particular bug.

That's exactly what's happening underneath a reasoning-effort setting. The Transformer machinery — attention, then MLP, then attention, then MLP, layer after layer — doesn't change at all between settings. What changes is how many intermediate reasoning steps the system generates and feeds back into its own context before it's willing to stop and answer. Low effort means a short scratchpad, or none at all. High effort means a longer one, with more room to trace, reconsider, and check itself along the way. This is also exactly why cranking reasoning effort up to maximum doesn't automatically make every answer better. Asking a system to spend its highest reasoning setting on fixing an obvious spelling mistake wastes computation on a task that never needed a scratchpad in the first place — there's nothing to trace or verify. Point that same high setting at a genuinely tangled bug, one that only shows up sometimes and under specific conditions, or a big reorganization of code that touches many connected parts, and the extra computation has something real to do.

It's also worth being clear that more effort isn't a safe default even on hard problems — the relationship isn't "more can only help, at worst it's wasted." A longer scratchpad gives the model more chances to talk itself out of a correct early instinct, chase a plausible-looking but wrong theory further than a shorter pass would have, or drift away from the actual question while exploring a tangent. Reasoning effort buys more opportunity to check and reconsider; it doesn't guarantee that opportunity gets used well every time.

One more thing worth being upfront about: this behavior — writing useful intermediate steps, actually using a failed theory to reconsider rather than just restating it — isn't something every Transformer does automatically once you ask it to "think harder." Models built with an effort dial have typically gone through an additional stage of training specifically aimed at producing good step-by-step reasoning, often checked against problems with a verifiable right answer. The underlying attention-and-MLP machinery is the same as in any other Transformer. What the effort setting is really unlocking is a model that's been separately trained to make good use of a longer scratchpad, not just any model given more room to talk.

## Then Why Not Just Let the Model Decide?

If reasoning effort is really about how much computation to spend, a fair question follows immediately: can't the model just look at a request and figure out for itself how hard it is? Largely, yes — and a well-designed system generally should. Recognizing that "fix this typo" needs almost no extra work, while "figure out why this only breaks for some customers sometimes, not others" needs quite a lot, is itself a judgment a capable model can make reasonably well on its own.

So why expose a manual control at all, if the system can often estimate this itself? Because difficulty isn't the only thing at stake. There are really three things pulling against each other at once: how good the answer needs to be, how fast you need it, and how much computation you're willing to spend getting it. And that third one isn't abstract — a longer scratchpad means more text the model has to generate before it answers, which shows up directly as more time waiting and, on most paid systems, more cost per request. A model might correctly judge that a bug deserves serious effort — a genuinely subtle, hard-to-reproduce one — while you, in the middle of a quick experiment, would honestly rather have a rough guess in ten seconds, and for a few cents less, than wait and pay for a thorough trace. Or the reverse: a request might look short and simple on the page — "is it safe to remove this check?" — while you know the code handles something sensitive, like payments, where a wrong answer is expensive, and you're willing to spend the extra time and cost to have the system verify its conclusion carefully, even though nothing about the wording signals that stakes are high.

That's the actual purpose of a manual reasoning control. It's closer to a camera's auto mode than a hidden setting only engineers care about: most of the time, automatic is exactly right, and the system should just quietly estimate difficulty, stakes, and uncertainty, and allocate an appropriate amount of computation on its own, the way a camera picks sensible settings without being asked. But when you know something the system doesn't — that this particular fix really matters, or that you'd rather trade some thoroughness for speed while you're still exploring — a manual override lets you say so directly, rather than hoping the model reads your mind from the wording of the request alone. Most ordinary use shouldn't require touching that dial at all. It exists for the moments when it's genuinely useful to override the default.

## What Reasoning Effort Is Really Buying You

There's a more precise way to describe what's happening as reasoning effort goes up, and it connects directly back to the zoom-in, zoom-out intuition running through this whole series. It's tempting to describe higher reasoning effort as "sharpening the context" — but that's not quite right, and the correction matters, especially for a case like this: a bug that was already fixed once quietly comes back after the code gets reorganized.

The code in front of the model is just the code — reasoning effort doesn't alter a single character of it. But with more computation to work with, the model has more opportunity to actually trace *why* the original fix worked in the first place, not just *that* it worked: which specific check was responsible for catching the empty result, whether that check survived the reorganization intact, and whether the reorganization moved, merged, or quietly dropped it. A low-effort pass might just notice "the crash is back" and re-apply the original fix without ever asking why it stopped working — which risks the exact same problem happening a third time, the next time the code gets touched. A higher-effort pass has room to check whether the fix depended on something that moved during the reorganization, whether the same logic got copied somewhere else in a slightly different, unprotected form, or whether anything was ever actually testing for this case in the first place, so it doesn't silently break again. Same code in every case. More reasoning computation produces a richer, more carefully checked working understanding of that code, and that sharper understanding is what makes the fix durable rather than temporary.

Reorganizing code adds one further wrinkle worth being honest about. Debugging has a single correct answer to converge toward — there either is or isn't a bug, and it's either found or it isn't, so more reasoning effort has a clear target to chase. Reorganizing code more often doesn't have one right answer at all; there can be several legitimate ways to restructure the same program, each a reasonable trade-off between clarity, performance, and how much gets disturbed along the way. Extra reasoning effort there is better understood as buying more thorough verification that a given reorganization actually preserves everything the program used to do, and a wider exploration of plausible approaches, rather than certainty about which single approach is best. That last part remains a judgment call, not a fact the model can compute its way into no matter how much effort it spends.

That's really the most accurate layman's definition available: reasoning is the computation a model spends turning the information already sitting in its context — the code you handed it — into a clear enough understanding to actually act on, not simply "thinking for longer," but tracing, connecting, checking, and reconsidering, using exactly the same attention-and-MLP machinery already running underneath everything else this series has covered. Low effort gives that machinery one quick pass over the code. High effort gives it room to trace things more than once, connect a fix back to the reason it worked, and check whether the bigger picture it's building actually holds together — before it ever shows you the fix.

## Can the Tool Loop Do the Reasoning For You?

Modern coding assistants rarely reason in a vacuum. They can run the code, run the tests, see the actual error, and try again. That raises a genuinely tempting idea: if a failing test will just send the model back for another attempt anyway, why pay for high reasoning effort at all? Why not always set effort to low, and let the surrounding loop of "try it, see what breaks, try again" do the hard work instead?

It's worth taking that idea seriously, because in one narrow situation, it's basically right. If the feedback is fast, cheap, and trustworthy — a compiler flatly refusing to build, a test whose pass-or-fail condition genuinely captures what "correct" means here — then a light attempt followed by a quick correction really can get you to the same place as one slower, more careful attempt, and do it faster. The loop, in that case, is quietly supplying the checking step that higher reasoning effort would otherwise have had to do internally.

But a failing test only tells you *that* something is still wrong. It doesn't tell you *why*, and it doesn't guarantee the next attempt understands the problem any better than the last one did. Go back to the sign-up bug — the welcome email crashing because the new account isn't fully ready yet. A low-effort pass, faced with a failing test, might notice which specific line the error points to and patch just enough to make that one test pass — perhaps adding a check in that one spot — without ever tracing back to the actual root cause, that the account can come back empty right after sign-up in more than just this one place. The test goes green. The underlying gap is still there, waiting to resurface the next time the code around it changes — which is exactly the "bug comes back after the next reorganization" pattern from earlier in this essay. The loop rewarded a fix that was correct enough to satisfy today's test, not necessarily the fix that was actually right.

There's a second problem with leaning on the loop alone: a light attempt doesn't necessarily fail in a useful, random way. If a model's quick pass tends to overlook a certain kind of issue — say, never checking whether a lookup can come back empty — it may make that same kind of mistake on the first attempt, the third attempt, and the seventh, patching the wrong thing each time rather than genuinely closing in on the cause. A test failing again doesn't force *better* reasoning on the next try. It only forces *another* try. Whether that next attempt is actually smarter, rather than just a nearby variation of the same guess, depends on whether the model uses the failure as new evidence to reconsider its theory — and that's precisely the kind of tracing and reconsidering that higher reasoning effort is for. Low effort plus a loop can still end up looping in place.

And the loop only forces real rigor where the feedback is trustworthy and complete in the first place. Tests only catch what someone thought to test for. A lot of what actually makes a bug hard, or a reorganization risky, is exactly the part with no clean pass-or-fail signal attached — a silent behavior change nothing asserts against, two reasonable ways to restructure the same code with no test able to say which is better, a fix that happens to work but for the wrong reason. "The loop will catch it" quietly assumes the test suite is doing more work than it usually is. Nor are extra attempts free: each one is another full round trip, more tool calls, more time. If setting effort low and looping five times takes longer, in total, than getting it right in one careful pass at medium effort, nothing has actually been saved — the cost just moved from thinking to trial and error, and trial and error is rarely the cheaper of the two once you count every attempt.

So the sharper version of the idea is this: a tight, fast, trustworthy feedback loop really can substitute for a good chunk of what higher reasoning effort buys you, and for narrow, well-covered problems, always-light-plus-retry is a perfectly reasonable strategy. But the moment a task is underspecified, the feedback is incomplete, or getting it wrong quietly is expensive, "always set it light" stops being a free optimization. It becomes a bet that the loop will happen to stumble onto the right answer through repetition, rather than the model actually reasoning its way there — and that's a bet that tends to lose exactly on the problems that matter most.

## Pulling It Together

Nothing described in this essay is a separate reasoning engine. It's the same LOOK-THINK cycle — attention gathering, the MLP interpreting — repeated across layers for a single answer, and then, for models built and trained to do it well, repeated again across a written scratchpad that folds each step back in as new context for the next. A reasoning-effort setting doesn't change that machinery; it changes how long the scratchpad is allowed to run, at a real cost in time and money, with no guarantee that a longer scratchpad is used well or that its steps are a complete account of what the model actually did. A surrounding tool loop can substitute for some of that effort when the feedback it provides is fast and trustworthy, but it can just as easily paper over a shallow fix that happens to satisfy today's test. None of that makes "reasoning" less real. It just means it's worth seeing for what it is: more computation, spent more carefully, on the same machinery that produces everything else a Transformer writes.
