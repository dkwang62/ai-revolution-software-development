# When the Software Industry Converged on the Agentic Enterprise

## Different Companies, the Same Destination

For most of the history of enterprise computing, the major software companies occupied distinctly different territories. Microsoft dominated operating systems and office productivity. SAP and Oracle built the systems that ran finance, manufacturing, and corporate operations. Salesforce made customer relationship management a cloud service. ServiceNow concentrated on workflows and IT service management. Google built its empire around search, advertising, and cloud computing. IBM sold technology, infrastructure, and services to the world's largest organizations. They competed, but they did not necessarily look alike.

Over the course of 2026, artificial intelligence produced something unusual: these companies arrived, independently, at remarkably similar ideas about how enterprise computing should work.

IBM has spent the year turning watsonx Orchestrate into what it calls an Agentic Control Plane — announced at its Think conference in May, and explicitly framed as a single governed layer for agents built anywhere, from any vendor, running on any infrastructure. Microsoft shipped Agent 365 and Entra Agent ID to general availability that same month, extending the identity infrastructure it built for human employees to cover AI agents instead. Salesforce has pushed Agentforce into a genuinely large, fast-growing business — over a billion dollars in annual recurring revenue, growing north of 100 percent year over year — built around multi-agent orchestration rather than one do-everything assistant. SAP has rolled out Joule Agents across more than two hundred specialized roles under a banner it calls the "Autonomous Enterprise." ServiceNow unveiled Otto, an expanded Autonomous Workforce, and a five-part AI Control Tower at its own Knowledge conference in May, with Nvidia's CEO calling it, on stage, a candidate to become "the operating system of enterprise AI agents." Google has built out the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, complete with agent identity, a registry, and gateways. Oracle introduced twenty-two Fusion Agentic Applications, spanning finance, HR, supply chain, and customer service, as part of its Release 26B rollout in March.

The names are different. Underneath them, a common architecture is emerging: a human states an intention, an AI orchestrator interprets it, specialized agents carry out pieces of the work, those agents reach into tools, data, and existing applications, and an action actually happens in the world — a purchase order gets created, a ticket gets closed, a campaign goes live. Surrounding the whole system, in every single one of these platforms, sits the same second layer: identity, security, permissions, governance, monitoring, evaluation, and, where the stakes are high enough, a human who has to sign off before anything moves.

This convergence may prove more important than any individual AI product. It suggests the industry has begun to agree on what enterprise computing looks like once artificial intelligence stops being a feature bolted onto software and starts being an active participant in operating it.

## From Software That Answers to Software That Acts

The first generation of generative AI was overwhelmingly conversational: a person asked a question, and the AI produced an answer. That was revolutionary, but structurally simple — a prompt went in, and a response came back out, with nothing in between except the model itself.

The next step was the copilot. AI moved inside existing applications and helped a human perform a task — summarizing an email, generating a spreadsheet formula, writing a stretch of code, drafting a sales message. The human, however, remained the one actually operating the software. The copilot suggested; the person clicked.

The emerging agentic architecture changes that relationship. An AI agent is now expected not merely to explain what should be done, but to perform at least some of the work required to accomplish it directly: formulate a plan, retrieve information, invoke software tools, communicate with other systems, evaluate its own intermediate results, and keep going until the objective is met or a decision genuinely requires a human. The interaction shifts from "tell me how to do this" to "do this for me" — from a prompt to a delegated objective.

This is why apparently mundane enterprise AI products matter more than they sound. An AI that writes a clever paragraph is impressive in the moment. An AI that checks inventory, obtains quotations, verifies purchasing policy, prepares an order, requests the appropriate approval, and updates the accounting system — the way ServiceNow's own L1 IT Service Desk specialist is already resolving assigned cases 99 percent faster than a human agent, inside ServiceNow's own help desk — represents a change in the architecture of work itself, not just a faster way to draft an email.

## Humans Have Been the Integration Layer

Consider an ordinary employee working inside a modern corporation. A procurement manager reads an email from a department requesting equipment. She checks inventory in one system, looks up approved suppliers in another, opens an ERP application, downloads quotations, compares them in a spreadsheet, consults purchasing policy, requests approval from a manager, creates a purchase order, and sends the result on to finance.

Every one of the company's systems in that story is already computerized, and yet the person performs a crucial function that's easy to overlook entirely: she integrates the software. She carries information and context from one application to the next. She understands what one system means and translates it into whatever the next system needs. In that sense, human beings have spent decades quietly functioning as the API layer between computer systems that were never designed to talk to each other.

Agentic AI attacks precisely this layer of human work. The agent can carry the context. The agent can call the APIs. The agent can retrieve the document, enter the transaction, ask another specialized agent for help. What the human increasingly supplies instead is something different in kind: the objective, the constraints, the judgment calls, the exceptions, and, ultimately, the accountability. That's a much bigger shift than simply making existing software more pleasant to use.

## IBM: The Enterprise Harness

IBM offers perhaps the clearest illustration of the infrastructure that has to surround an agent before an enterprise will trust it with real work.

At Think 2026 in May, IBM announced the next generation of watsonx Orchestrate, moving it from a workflow-automation product into what the company explicitly calls an agentic control plane for the multi-agent era — a single place where an organization can deploy agents from any source and apply consistent policy enforcement and accountability across all of them. The framing IBM's own CEO Arvind Krishna used on stage was pointed: the enterprises actually pulling ahead with AI aren't the ones deploying the most AI, they're the ones redesigning how the business operates around it. The bet is that most large organizations will end up running agents built in-house, agents bought from SaaS vendors, and agents pulled from open marketplaces, all at once — and that the bottleneck has already shifted from building agents to governing thousands of them at once, across teams and vendors nobody fully controls.

The terminology is revealing. A powerful model by itself is not enough to operate a corporation. The enterprise needs to know which agent is acting, what it's permitted to do, what information it can access, which tools it may invoke, what a given action costs, whether the result can be trusted, and what happened if something went wrong. IBM's control plane wraps identity, permissions, shared context, routing, policy, guardrails, monitoring, and cost controls around the model itself — the model supplies intelligence, and the surrounding architecture is what turns that intelligence into something an operations team can actually run in production.

IBM's approach illustrates a broader principle worth stating plainly: as AI becomes more capable, the infrastructure surrounding it does not become less important. It becomes more important. An unreliable chatbot can give someone a wrong answer. An unreliable autonomous agent with authority over purchasing, payroll, or infrastructure can do considerably more damage — and capability, in that sense, creates its own demand for control.

IBM's other major 2026 launch, IBM Bob, reached general availability in April, just ahead of Think, and was featured in nearly every demo and keynote at the conference. Bob is explicitly positioned across the entire software development lifecycle rather than as a code-completion tool — planning, implementation, testing, security scanning, debugging, documentation, and legacy modernization all fall inside its scope, with security and cost controls built into the workflow rather than bolted on afterward. It's a clean illustration of the same underlying migration playing out inside software engineering specifically: the AI is no longer merely suggesting the next line. It's increasingly being asked to accomplish the engineering task, end to end, with a human reviewing the result rather than writing it.

## Microsoft: Giving the Machine an Employee Badge

Microsoft arrives at much the same destination from a different starting point.

Copilot began as an assistant embedded throughout Microsoft's enormous software ecosystem. Over 2026, Microsoft built the infrastructure for organizations that will eventually contain large populations of AI agents running alongside their human employees. Agent 365, reaching general availability on May 1, 2026, acts as a control layer through which agents can be discovered, governed, monitored, and secured — a registry that gives an IT team a complete inventory of every agent operating inside the organization, whether it was built with Microsoft's own tools or a third-party framework.

The more revealing piece is Microsoft Entra Agent ID, the identity foundation underneath Agent 365. For decades, enterprise identity systems answered a fixed set of questions about people: who is this employee, what applications can she access, what information is she allowed to see, what privileges does she have, and when should those privileges expire. AI creates an entirely new category of actor that needs the same questions answered — an autonomous agent that can access a database, call an API, read confidential information, or initiate a transaction on someone's behalf. Microsoft's answer has been to extend the exact logic of human identity management to machines: an agent can now have its own identity, its own scoped permissions, a named human owner or sponsor, a full audit trail of its actions, time-bound access that expires automatically, and — eventually — a formal, logged retirement when it's decommissioned. According to Microsoft's own 2026 Secure Access report, 97 percent of organizations had already experienced an identity or network access incident in the past year, and 70 percent of those incidents were tied to AI-related activity — which is the concrete security problem this entire piece of infrastructure exists to solve.

This sounds like an administrative footnote, but it reveals something larger: AI agents are becoming first-class actors inside the organization, with their own accountable identity, in exactly the sense a new employee would have one on day one.

Microsoft's platform also shows how human oversight is evolving in practice. Rather than requiring a person to supervise every single step of a workflow, agents can run autonomously right up until they hit a defined approval boundary — the process pauses, a human exercises judgment, and the agent picks back up from there. Human-in-the-loop stops being a vague promise that "a person will remain involved" and becomes a specific, engineered checkpoint built into the architecture itself.

## SAP: Intent Becomes the Interface

SAP may offer the clearest view of where the user's actual experience of enterprise software is heading.

Joule, SAP's assistant, now coordinates specialized Joule Agents across business functions and across both SAP and non-SAP systems — the platform runs on multiple underlying language models simultaneously, including GPT, Gemini, and Claude, chosen per task rather than locked to one vendor. By mid-2026, Joule was embedded across thirty-five SAP solutions with more than 2,400 individual skills, and SAP had made the agent runtime free to customers through the end of the year — described by Forrester as the most aggressive commercial move the company has made in this space. SAP's own framing for the resulting system is the "Autonomous Enterprise," built around more than two hundred specialized agents rather than one generalist.

The important idea underneath all of that isn't simply that SAP added an AI assistant to its products. It's that a human should increasingly be able to state what needs to be accomplished without manually coordinating every application required to get there. That changes the interface itself. Traditional enterprise software asks the employee to understand the software — which application, which menu, which screen, which field, which workflow. The emerging architecture instead asks the system to understand the employee: what outcome are you actually trying to achieve.

SAP has gone as far as describing agents as becoming an important new class of *user* of enterprise software in their own right — a claim worth taking seriously rather than treating as marketing color. For the entire history of business software, applications were designed for human users: screens, menus, buttons, dashboards, and forms all existed because a person needed some way to manipulate a digital system. An agent doesn't strictly need any of that. It can communicate through APIs and structured, machine-readable interfaces instead. Enterprise software is therefore acquiring a second population of users — humans and machines both — and it's entirely plausible that machines end up generating far more application interactions than people do within a few years.

## Salesforce: A Society of Specialists

Salesforce illustrates another important development in this convergence: the limits of expecting one giant, do-everything agent to handle a whole business.

A single agent loaded with responsibility for sales, service, finance, human resources, technical support, and everything else eventually becomes difficult to manage and tends to reason less reliably as its scope grows. Salesforce's architecture leans into multi-agent orchestration instead — one agent interacts with the customer or employee, while specialized agents behind it handle particular jobs. A customer might experience a single, coherent request: "resolve this customer's problem." Behind that one sentence might sit a customer-service agent, an order agent, a billing agent, a logistics agent, and a refund agent, coordinating with each other without the customer ever knowing how many separate specialists were actually involved. It resembles an organization chart more than a conventional computer program — the orchestrator doesn't necessarily know everything itself, it just knows which specialist should handle which piece.

This architecture has become a genuinely large business rather than a demo: Agentforce reached roughly $1.4 billion in annual recurring revenue by early 2026, growing at 114 percent year over year, and Salesforce describes it as the fastest-growing product in the company's history.

Salesforce is also central to a second, quieter development worth naming directly: the emergence of standard interfaces for agents to talk to tools and to each other. The Model Context Protocol, MCP, gives agents a standardized way to interact with tools, APIs, and data sources. Agent2Agent, A2A, gives agents a way to communicate and delegate work to other agents, including agents built by entirely different vendors. These matter because the future enterprise is extremely unlikely to buy every single agent from one company. A Microsoft agent may need a Salesforce agent to complete a CRM task, which then hands a result to an SAP system before the whole chain passes back to another specialist. Whether MCP and A2A in their exact current form remain the permanent standard is beside the point — standards change constantly. What matters is that the industry has recognized, all at once, that it needs standard interfaces for agent-to-tool and agent-to-agent communication, and that recognition is what makes a real cross-vendor ecosystem possible at all.

## Google: Building the Agent Platform

Google's Gemini Enterprise architecture arrives at essentially the same structural conclusion from the direction of AI research and cloud infrastructure rather than business applications.

Its enterprise agent platform includes facilities for building agents, running them, discovering them, assigning them identities, connecting them to tools, evaluating them, observing their behavior, and governing how they interact with everything around them — which amounts to a new kind of operating environment: a registry describing every agent, identities establishing who each one is, gateways controlling what they're allowed to talk to, protocols connecting them to tools and to each other, evaluation systems measuring whether they behave correctly, and observability tools watching what they actually do once deployed. As with IBM, Microsoft, and the rest, the model itself is only one component of a much larger system.

Google's partnership with SAP, announced in April 2026, is a concrete example of this cross-vendor pattern in action: Joule Agents inside SAP's customer-experience solutions can now hand off to Gemini Enterprise as a central hub, letting agents take action across both SAP's and Google's platforms based on a high-level goal a marketing team sets rather than a series of manually stitched-together steps.

Google's platform also highlights an important departure from the chatbot paradigm that most people still associate with AI: the long-running agent. A chatbot normally lives for the duration of a single conversation. A sufficiently capable agent can be given an objective and keep working for hours, or potentially days — researching, planning, executing, observing its own results, adapting, waiting on an external event, and resuming once that event happens. The natural unit of AI interaction is moving from the prompt toward the delegated objective, and that shift may end up being one of the defining differences between generative AI as most people experienced it in 2023 and agentic AI as it's actually being deployed now.

## ServiceNow: The Autonomous Workforce

ServiceNow is perhaps the most explicit of any vendor about the labor analogy running underneath all of this.

At Knowledge 2026 in May, ServiceNow — which now describes itself, in its own official language, as "the AI control tower for business reinvention" — unveiled three pieces working as one system. Otto merges the company's earlier Now Assist product, its recently acquired Moveworks platform, and its AI Experience tools into a single interface handling conversational AI, enterprise search, voice agents, and data exploration. The Autonomous Workforce expanded from a single L1 IT service desk specialist, launched earlier in the year, into "AI teammates" covering CRM, employee services, and security and risk — described deliberately as role-scoped digital employees embedded directly in existing workflows, working alongside human colleagues rather than replacing an entire department at once. And the AI Control Tower, first introduced a year earlier purely as a visibility tool, grew into a five-part governance system — discover, observe, govern, secure, and measure — with thirty new integrations reaching across AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, SAP, Oracle, and Workday, and new capability to bring non-human identities and connected physical devices under the same governance model as software agents.

ServiceNow's own language for its earlier products is useful here: it describes the previous generation of enterprise AI as the "sidecar AI era" — AI attached alongside conventional applications while the underlying way of working stayed largely unchanged. The company's bet is that the genuinely agentic alternative integrates AI directly into workflows, data, security, and governance, rather than sitting beside them as an add-on. Putting a chatbot next to every application, in other words, is not the same thing as an AI-native architecture — a genuinely agentic one asks whether the applications need to remain the center of the user's experience at all.

Its Control Tower reinforces the industry-wide convergence in the most concrete way possible: IBM has one. Microsoft has one. Google is building the equivalent. ServiceNow has one, and has extended its governance specifically across the Microsoft Agent 365 ecosystem so the two companies' agents can be monitored under one roof. Salesforce has its own governance and gateway layers. The vocabulary differs because each vendor comes from a different tradition — but the architectural requirement underneath the vocabulary is close to identical everywhere you look.

## Oracle: The Application Begins to Perform the Work

Oracle offers another variation on the same theme, from a starting point closer to the data itself.

Oracle's enormous installed base sits inside finance, human resources, supply chains, and procurement systems, and its agents inherit something especially valuable as a result: proximity to the systems where business actions actually happen, with native access to the underlying data layer instead of having to translate everything through an external API. In March 2026, as part of its Release 26B rollout, Oracle introduced twenty-two Fusion Agentic Applications spanning ERP, HR, supply chain, and customer experience — described internally as outcome-driven systems backed by teams of specialized agents that reason, coordinate, and decide, then actually execute work through Fusion's business objects, workflows, approvals, and logged actions. Oracle's own framing draws a sharp line between the previous generation and this one: a copilot suggests; an agentic application acts. A traditional chatbot can tell you an invoice is overdue. An agentic application evaluates the overdue balance, assesses the risk signals around it, prioritizes which collection actions to take, drafts the customer communication, and routes any exception it can't confidently resolve to a human — without anyone stepping through each of those decisions by hand.

In July 2026, Oracle opened its AI Agent Studio to pro-code developers and to AI coding agents directly, signaling that the company isn't simply adding AI features to its existing cloud suite — it's repositioning Fusion as a full development platform for building autonomous enterprise applications from scratch. Like IBM and Google, Oracle also supports multiple underlying models, tools, retrieval, and memory rather than betting the entire platform on one model provider, treating the foundation model as a replaceable component inside a much larger architecture rather than assuming any single model has to supply everything.

## Different Roads, Same Destination

The convergence becomes much clearer once the marketing terminology gets stripped away. IBM brings watsonx, agents, and an Agentic Control Plane. Microsoft brings Copilot, Agent 365, and Entra Agent ID. Salesforce brings Agentforce, multi-agent orchestration, and agent gateways. SAP brings Joule, Joule Studio, and the Autonomous Enterprise. ServiceNow brings Otto, the Autonomous Workforce, and the AI Control Tower. Google brings Gemini Enterprise, agent identity, registries, and gateways. Oracle brings Fusion Agentic Applications and its own agent infrastructure on Oracle Cloud.

Underneath every one of them sits increasingly the same stack: human intent enters through some kind of AI front door, gets interpreted by an orchestration layer, gets handed to specialized agents, which draw on models, tools, APIs, and enterprise data to actually get something done, reaching down into the existing applications underneath and, ultimately, producing a real change in the world — a purchase order created, a ticket resolved, a campaign launched. And surrounding every layer of that stack, in every vendor's version of it, sits the same second system: identity, permissions, security, policy, governance, observability, evaluation, and a human approval step wherever the stakes are high enough to need one.

That is too much convergence to dismiss as coincidence. The major enterprise vendors appear to be discovering the same requirements independently because autonomous software creates the same engineering problems regardless of who happens to be building it.

## Where They Still Differ

Convergence does not mean uniformity. Each company is approaching the same destination from its own historical strength.

Microsoft has an extraordinary advantage in the human work surface itself — Windows, Microsoft 365, Teams, Azure, GitHub, and enterprise identity all place Microsoft unusually close to both the employee and the developer, which is exactly why its agentic strategy leans so heavily on Copilot, organizational identity, and productivity tooling. SAP and Oracle hold something different: deep, structural access to transactional systems. They know where the orders, invoices, payroll records, inventory, and supplier relationships actually live, which lets their agents act very close to a corporation's real economic machinery. Salesforce's center of gravity sits around customers, sales, and service data, and its multi-agent architecture grows naturally outward from those relationships. ServiceNow starts from workflow itself, which makes its vision unusually process-oriented — it treats agents less as isolated assistants and more as participants inside workflows that already connect departments and systems to each other. Google approaches the problem from AI research, cloud infrastructure, and data, which is why its architecture looks especially like a general platform for building agent ecosystems rather than a set of pre-built business functions. IBM remains characteristically enterprise-oriented — heterogeneous systems, hybrid environments, governance, and integration across vendors nobody expects to standardize on a single supplier, which is exactly the acknowledgment built into its emphasis on orchestration and control.

There's a further split worth naming specifically in software development: IBM Bob, Microsoft's GitHub and Copilot ecosystem, and Google's developer-facing agents approach the agentic transition through the act of creating software itself. SAP, Oracle, Salesforce, and ServiceNow are more strongly positioned around agents that operate the business processes their platforms already represent. Those are genuinely different bets, and they'll shape the competition in different ways even as the underlying architecture keeps converging.

Each vendor, unsurprisingly, wants the orchestration layer to sit as close as possible to its own strategic asset. That sets up a real fight over a single, enormously valuable question: who owns the layer through which human intention actually enters the enterprise.

## The Model May Become Less Important Than We Expect

The current AI industry encourages an obsessive focus on the model itself — which one benchmarks highest, which has the largest context window, which reasons best, which costs least. Those questions matter. But the architecture emerging across every one of these platforms suggests the long-term system may not revolve around a single model at all.

An orchestrator can select a different model for a different task, on the fly. A difficult reasoning problem might justify an expensive frontier model. A routine classification might run on a tiny model hosted locally. A confidential task might need a model running entirely inside the company's own infrastructure. A huge batch job might use whichever model offers the cheapest acceptable result. SAP's own Joule already does exactly this in production, routing tasks across GPT, Gemini, and Claude depending on what the task calls for, rather than committing the entire platform to one provider. A coding agent might use one model while a legal agent uses a different one entirely.

The architecture, in other words, becomes something closer to: an objective arrives, an agent picks it up, the agent selects whichever model is appropriate, the agent uses whatever tools it needs, and the result gets verified before it's trusted. Models begin to resemble interchangeable sources of machine intelligence inside a much larger system, rather than the system itself. They won't necessarily become pure commodities — real capability differences remain genuinely valuable — but the surrounding architecture makes substituting one source of intelligence for another increasingly possible. That's exactly what makes the harness around the model strategically important in its own right, independent of which model happens to be running inside it at any given moment.

## The Harness Becomes Infrastructure

An AI model knows how to generate outputs. An enterprise system has to know a great deal more than that. Who requested this action? What is the agent actually allowed to do? Which data can it see? Which tools can it use? How much money can it spend before someone has to approve it? Which model should handle this particular task? When does it need to stop and ask a human? How is the result verified before it's trusted? What happens if it fails partway through? Who is ultimately responsible for what it did? Can every action it took be reconstructed after the fact, for an auditor or a regulator?

Those questions explain why control planes, identity systems, gateways, registries, evaluation systems, and observability tools have appeared simultaneously across the industry, from every major vendor, within the same twelve-month period. The more autonomous the AI becomes, the more consequential the harness around it becomes — which produces a real paradox. It's tempting to imagine that increasingly intelligent models eventually eliminate the need for most of the surrounding software. In practice, the opposite seems to be happening: intelligence is creating an enormous new category of software whose entire purpose is to control intelligence.

## Verification Becomes More Important as Creation Becomes Cheaper

The same principle shows up inside software development specifically. When every line of code has to be written by a human, human labor naturally limits how much software can get built. When AI can generate code at enormous speed — the exact capability IBM Bob and its peers across the industry are built around — generation stops being the dominant constraint. The problem moves downstream instead: does the software actually work, is it secure, does it match what the person actually wanted, did the agent choose a sound architecture, has it introduced some dependency nobody noticed, will it behave correctly in a situation nobody explicitly tested for.

The natural response is more machine verification, not less: AI writes, AI tests, AI reviews, AI monitors what's running in production, AI identifies failures, AI attempts repairs — with humans increasingly evaluating the system at a higher level rather than checking every individual line. This pattern extends well beyond programming. An agent making a real business decision can be checked by a second, independent system before it's ever allowed to execute. The result is an increasingly machine-mediated environment in which machines create, operate, and verify the work of other machines, with human judgment migrating upward rather than disappearing — spent on setting policy and handling genuine exceptions, rather than checking routine work line by line.

## Human-in-the-Loop Does Not Mean Human-in-Every-Step

This distinction is becoming genuinely important, and it's easy to get wrong. It's easy to insist that humans must remain "in the loop" — but if an organization is running millions of agent actions an hour, which is now a realistic figure for a company the size of ServiceNow's own largest customers, no person can meaningfully review every single one of them.

Instead, organizations are defining decision boundaries. An agent may purchase routine office supplies entirely on its own. A purchase above some threshold requires a manager's sign-off. A purchase above a much higher threshold requires several layers of approval. An unusual transaction gets automatically escalated, regardless of size. A low-confidence medical, legal, or financial decision gets blocked from proceeding autonomously at all, no exceptions. Human oversight becomes risk-based rather than universal: the machine handles the ordinary flow of work, and people handle the consequential decisions, the genuinely ambiguous cases, the exceptions, and the accountability that comes with all of it. That looks considerably more like management than like traditional computer operation — a person supervising a team of specialists, rather than a person operating a single tool.

## The Graphical Interface May Lose Its Monopoly

There's a further consequence of agentic computing that's easy to underestimate.

For decades, software companies competed heavily on the graphical interface itself — menus, windows, icons, forms, and dashboards determined how a human actually communicated with a computer. Agents don't need software presented that way at all. They work through APIs, structured data, and machine-accessible tools instead, and the newest wave of platform moves reflects exactly that: ServiceNow's Action Fabric, launched at Knowledge 2026, lets its platform run headless, so any agent that speaks MCP can plug directly into ServiceNow's workflows without ever touching a screen — a direct response to the reality that an enterprise's agents might just as easily be running inside Anthropic's Claude or OpenAI's ChatGPT as inside ServiceNow's own interface. Salesforce made the equivalent move before ServiceNow did.

If a human increasingly communicates with an agent, and the agent in turn communicates with the underlying applications, the graphical interface stops being the default path between a person and a piece of enterprise software. It doesn't disappear — people still need to inspect information, explore alternatives, and exercise judgment directly, and there will always be moments that call for a human looking at a screen rather than delegating the whole task away. But it may lose its position as the dominant way people interact with enterprise computing, which would be a genuine reversal of a design principle the industry has followed for forty years: for decades, the entire discipline of software design was about making machines easier for humans to operate. The agentic enterprise is, in a real sense, teaching machines to operate the machines instead, with people stepping in at the points that actually require a person.

## From Applications to an Agentic Operating Layer

This points at an even larger possibility. Today's corporation owns hundreds or thousands of separate applications, and employees have to know which one performs which task. In an agentic environment, the user increasingly encounters a single intelligent front door instead.

Someone says: "prepare a revised forecast for next quarter, identify the three largest deviations from plan, determine their causes, and schedule meetings with the responsible managers." The system works out everything underneath that sentence on its own — one agent retrieves the financial data, another performs the analysis, another pulls operational context, another prepares the actual report, another checks everyone's calendars, another sends the meeting invitations. The employee may never learn which seven separate applications were actually involved in getting the job done. The applications remain underneath, doing real work — but they stop defining the user's mental model of what "using a computer" even means. The organizing layer becomes the agentic system itself, sitting on top of everything else.

That's a large part of why every vendor covered here is racing to own the orchestrator, the control plane, or the AI front door specifically. Whoever controls that layer potentially mediates the user's entire relationship with everything running beneath it — which is exactly why this is simultaneously a story about shared technical architecture and a genuinely fierce commercial fight.

## The Battle Hidden Inside the Convergence

This is the great exception to the apparent agreement running through everything above. The vendors agree on the architecture. They do not agree, even slightly, on who should control it.

Microsoft naturally wants Copilot and its agent infrastructure to become the user's gateway. SAP wants Joule coordinating work around SAP's enormous body of enterprise data and process knowledge. Salesforce wants Agentforce sitting as close as possible to customer relationships. ServiceNow wants workflows coordinated through its own platform, which is exactly why it describes itself as the AI control tower for the entire business, not just for IT. Google wants Gemini Enterprise to become the general platform everyone else's agents plug into. Oracle wants intelligence embedded deep inside Fusion and its own cloud infrastructure. IBM has clear strategic reasons to emphasize heterogeneous orchestration and governance across systems supplied by many different vendors, since it doesn't own the dominant application layer the way the others do.

So the industry is experiencing architectural convergence and platform competition at exactly the same time. Everyone largely agrees on what the future looks like, while fighting hard over who occupies the most valuable position inside it. Open standards like MCP and A2A become strategically important precisely because customers will resist letting any single vendor own every agent, every tool, and every data source they depend on. Interoperability isn't just a technical detail here — it's part of the actual commercial struggle over who controls the agentic enterprise.

Even the economics of the industry are shifting in response to this fight. The per-seat subscription model that dominated enterprise software for two decades is coming under real, visible pressure as agents start replacing some of the human seats those subscriptions were priced around. HubSpot now charges $0.50 per resolved customer conversation rather than per user seat. Salesforce introduced Agentforce Flex Credits, billing $0.10 per standard agent action and $0.15 per voice action. ServiceNow has moved toward consumption-based billing that charges for each individual AI skill an agent executes. Salesforce's own CEO has publicly acknowledged the industry doesn't yet know exactly where this settles, describing his company as following the smaller AI-native startups on pricing rather than leading the change. When the thing being sold shifts from "access for a person" to "work actually completed," the pricing model underneath the entire industry has to shift with it — and nobody has fully worked out yet what the new one looks like.

## From Copilot to Colleague

Put all of this together and a progression becomes visible: a software tool becomes an AI feature, which becomes a copilot, which becomes a genuine AI assistant, which becomes an AI agent, which becomes a coordinated team of specialized agents, which becomes something closer to an agentic organization in its own right.

The language some of these companies use — "autonomous workforce," "digital employee," "AI teammate" — can sound overreaching when it first shows up in a press release. But the underlying architectural direction behind the language is real. Software is acquiring agency. It can increasingly receive an objective, work out the intermediate steps on its own, interact with other software and other agents, draw on organizational knowledge, make bounded decisions within a defined scope, and actually execute actions in the world rather than simply describing what someone else should do. Once that happens, software stops being a purely passive instrument waiting for detailed human instructions at every step. It becomes something closer to a delegated actor — bounded, accountable, auditable, but genuinely delegated.

## What Remains Human?

None of this implies corporations turn into giant autonomous machines with no people involved. It changes where human value actually resides.

When operating software required detailed procedural knowledge, knowing how to manipulate the system was itself a valuable skill. When agents increasingly manipulate the systems directly, other capabilities become relatively more important instead: knowing what should actually be accomplished, understanding which constraints genuinely matter, recognizing the moment an answer doesn't make sense even though it sounds confident, understanding real consequences, choosing between competing objectives when they conflict, handling situations that are genuinely novel rather than variations on a known pattern, and taking responsibility for the outcome.

AI may remove human beings from many procedural loops while simultaneously increasing the relative importance of judgment. Automation, in this sense, doesn't simply eliminate tasks. It rearranges the division of cognition between people and machines — moving the routine downward, onto the machine, and pushing the exceptional and the consequential upward, onto the person.

## The Larger Transition

The AI revolution in software is usually described as a revolution in how software gets created — and that's certainly happening. AI can already translate natural-language descriptions into working code; coding agents can plan, implement, test, debug, and modify real systems; legacy software can be analyzed and reconstructed rather than simply patched around; the cost of turning an idea into a working piece of machinery keeps falling.

But the pattern running across every major enterprise vendor in 2026 reveals a second revolution that may end up being larger than the first. AI is beginning to operate the software, not just write it. That produces a genuine sequence: humans wrote software; then AI helped humans write software; then AI started writing an increasing share of software on its own; then AI began operating software on behalf of humans; then AI agents started coordinating with each other and with other software directly; and, at the far end of that sequence, humans increasingly specify objectives rather than procedures at all.

That final transition is the most consequential one. For nearly the entire history of computing, humans have had to translate their own intentions into a machine-readable procedure — first by hand, as programmers; then with less effort, as higher-level languages absorbed more of that translation work; then through applications, which encapsulated even more of the procedure inside a fixed piece of software; then through graphical interfaces, which made those applications usable by people who'd never touch a line of code. AI is now attacking the translation layer that remained. The person increasingly describes the state of the world they want to exist. The machine works out how to get there.

## A Remarkable Convergence

IBM, Microsoft, Salesforce, SAP, ServiceNow, Google, and Oracle did not begin this year in the same place. Their histories are different. Their customers overlap but aren't identical. Their commercial incentives differ, their underlying technologies differ, and their terminology certainly differs. Yet across a single twelve-month cycle, every one of them has built a variation of the same architecture: intent flows in, gets orchestrated, gets handed to specialized agents, and those agents reach into tools, data, and existing applications to produce a real action — with identity, governance, security, verification, and human judgment wrapped around every layer of it.

The differences between them remain genuinely important, and they'll shape who wins which part of this market. IBM emphasizes heterogeneous enterprise orchestration and governance across vendors nobody expects to consolidate. Microsoft has unusual control over the employee's actual working environment and over organizational identity itself. SAP and Oracle sit close to the transactional heart of large corporations. Salesforce owns the customer relationship. ServiceNow approaches everything through the lens of workflow. Google brings enormous AI research and cloud infrastructure to bear on the problem as a general platform. Those differences will shape the competition for years.

But they no longer obscure the underlying convergence. The industry appears to have discovered, independently and almost simultaneously, that putting a chatbot inside every application was never the final destination. The deeper transformation happens once intelligence becomes an operating layer sitting across applications, rather than a feature living inside any one of them. The application doesn't disappear. The graphical interface doesn't disappear. The employee doesn't disappear. Even the conventional software stack underneath everything doesn't disappear. What's being inserted is another layer above all of it — one capable of interpreting a human objective, selecting the right intelligence for the job, coordinating a team of specialists, invoking whatever software is needed, carrying context between systems that were never designed to share it, acting within a delegated and auditable scope of authority, verifying its own results, and returning control to a person exactly when human judgment is actually required.

For decades, software waited for people to operate it. We learned its commands, navigated its menus, carried information between its silos by hand, and translated our own intentions into procedures it could understand. The emerging agentic enterprise reverses a meaningful part of that relationship. We increasingly tell the system what we're trying to accomplish. The software, more and more, works out how to operate the software.

That may turn out to be the most important consequence of the entire industry's convergence on AI agents — not any single product from any single vendor, but the fact that seven companies with almost nothing else in common all arrived at the same answer to the same question, within the same year, without needing to ask each other first.
