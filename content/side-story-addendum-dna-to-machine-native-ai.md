# From DNA to a Machine-Native AI
## Can a Model Discover Its Own Abstraction Layer?

## The Representation We Didn't Program

Nobody hands a baby a definition of "dog." Nobody writes out a rule — four legs, fur, a tail, a bark — and installs it in a child's head. The child just sees dogs. Different sizes, different colors, from different angles, running, sleeping, barking, sometimes photographed, sometimes real. And at some point, without anyone teaching it directly, something remarkable happens: the child has a working concept of "dog." Show her an animal she's never seen before, some odd, scruffy breed, and she'll usually get it right anyway.

What the child has actually built is a representation — an internal way of organizing raw experience into something useful, without anyone specifying its contents in advance. That single idea, more than any other, is the foundation for everything that follows: the great discovery of modern AI is that humans don't have to design all of a machine's internal representations by hand. A machine can learn useful representations for itself from patterns in data — not because it's biologically or neurologically anything like a child's brain, but because the same basic shape shows up: experience goes in, and something usable comes out the other side, without every step in between being specified by a person.

Once that's established, a sharper question follows, and it's the one this whole chapter is actually chasing: if AI can learn representations from patterns, can it go further, and learn representations of cause and effect — from deliberately changing something and watching what happens, rather than just watching?

That question takes some time to answer properly, and it's worth knowing the route in advance. From here, this chapter follows a short piece of AI history that shows why prediction turned out to matter so much more than anyone expected; then a very small example — a rooster and a sunrise — that shows exactly where prediction alone runs out of road; then biology, where that limit becomes concrete and expensive; and finally computing, where the same idea turns out to be cheap enough to run millions of times over. Each stop adds one piece to the same underlying question, and the ending returns to where this section started.

## The Strange Lesson of Next-Word Prediction

It's worth pausing on how long this took to become obvious, because the delay is itself informative.

In the 1950s, researchers already had an idea that sounds thoroughly modern: maybe a machine could *learn*, rather than simply execute rules a programmer wrote out in advance. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron, built in 1958, was an early attempt at exactly that — a simple learning machine, loosely inspired by a neuron, that adjusted itself based on examples. Naturally, researchers wanted to know what a machine like this could and couldn't compute. One famous limitation, highlighted by Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert a decade later, was that a single-layer perceptron couldn't represent a basic logical function called XOR — a rule that says "yes" only when exactly one of two things is true, not both and not neither. It was a narrow, technical limitation, but it came to symbolize the weaknesses of early neural networks and, alongside limited computing power and disappointing results elsewhere in AI, contributed to a broader loss of enthusiasm for the field.

Notice what kind of question that was: *what specific function can this machine compute?* A fixed target, a fixed answer, a pass-or-fail grade. Here's a useful thought experiment: imagine sending one piece of advice back to those 1950s researchers. It probably wouldn't have been a fix for XOR. It would have been something closer to: stop trying to hit a fixed target, and instead just get better and better at predicting the next word of ordinary text.

XOR is a destination. Once a machine can compute it, there's nowhere further to go — the task is finished. Next-word prediction isn't a destination at all. It's open-ended. If a model can easily finish "The cat sat on the ___," push it further, toward "The central bank raised interest rates because inflation was ___," and further still, toward "Alice is older than Bob, and Bob is older than Charles, so Alice is older than ___." Every one of those is a harder version of the same simple game, and getting better at the game creates pressure toward richer and richer internal representations useful for grammar, facts, arithmetic and reasoning, none of which anyone had to specify by hand. The research question quietly changed from *what can this machine compute?* to *what must this machine learn internally in order to get better at prediction?* That reframing, more than any single algorithm, is why modern AI looks the way it does.

The word *general* matters as much as the word *prediction*. Human writing is an enormous, imperfect record of almost everything people have thought about and experienced — physics, war, love, money, mathematics, cooking, programming, biology, law, relationships, engineering, history. So getting better at predicting general text quietly becomes a far bigger challenge than it sounds: to predict what humans will say about the world, it helps to learn something about the world they're describing. A narrow training objective — guess the next word — can therefore create pressure toward an unexpectedly broad internal model.

## Machines Can Learn Their Own Representations

It's worth stating the resulting discovery plainly, because everything that follows depends on it: nobody hands a language model a dictionary of concepts in advance, any more than anyone hands a baby a definition of "dog." Traditional computing has always worked the other way around — a person understands a problem, invents the relevant concepts, writes rules based on those concepts, and the computer simply executes them. Machine learning introduced a genuinely different path: data goes in, a learning objective — like predicting the next word — gets applied, and the machine develops its own internal representations as a side effect of getting better at that objective. Nobody necessarily decided in advance what every one of those internal representations should be. Training discovered them.

Put this next to the baby again, because the comparison should make the discovery feel less mysterious than it sometimes does. Human beings have always been evidence that useful internal representations can emerge through learning from experience rather than being explicitly taught one by one. A child isn't born with someone having mathematically installed the concept of "dog" in her brain.

But a child isn't a blank slate either. Evolution has already supplied an extraordinarily sophisticated brain, sensory system, learning machinery, and a long list of instincts and biases before any learning from experience even starts. So humans were never a recipe for building artificial intelligence — a set of steps researchers could simply copy. They were something more modest but still important: evidence that intelligence doesn't require every useful representation to be explicitly designed from the outside. AI researchers still had to discover, from scratch, artificial mechanisms capable of doing something analogous.

## But Prediction Is Not Causation

Here's the limit of what pattern-learning alone can do, and it's worth being sharp about it before going any further, because everything from here on exists specifically to push past it.

Suppose an AI observes that, every morning, a rooster crows shortly before the sun rises. Given enough mornings, it becomes extremely good at predicting sunrise from a crow. Has it learned that the rooster crowing *causes* the sun to rise? Obviously not — but notice that nothing about pure pattern prediction, on its own, actually rules that out. A system that only watches may become extraordinarily good at predicting one event from another, but correlation alone cannot reliably tell it whether one actually causes the other. Prediction alone doesn't settle causality. It settles what tends to go with what.

That gap is exactly why the rest of this chapter matters, and it's the reason DNA and cells and machine code are about to become the main event.

## DNA Is More Than a Sequence

Here's where the earlier, more abstract observation finally earns its place. A sentence is a sequence of words. A genome is a sequence of DNA letters. A computer program, at the level a processor actually reads it, is a sequence of instructions too. That's a tidy coincidence, but not the interesting one — the interesting question is what happens once you ask what actually *executes* each of those sequences. A sentence gets read by a person. A genome gets read by a cell. And a genome, unlike a sentence, doesn't do anything on its own until something acts on it.

Genome models such as Evo have already shown that training on next-letter prediction across DNA — the same basic game as next-word prediction in language — doesn't just teach a model to guess letters. It picks up real biological structure along the way, the DNA equivalent of a language model learning that "cat" and "dog" belong to a similar category without ever being told so.

But the comparison has a real limit, and it's the same limit the rooster ran into. A computer program is read by one well-defined system: a processor. DNA is read by something far messier: a living cell. The exact same DNA sequence can do something completely different depending on which type of cell it's in, which genes and proteins happen to be active nearby, and what the cell's environment looks like. DNA isn't the whole program. It only makes sense once it's plugged into the extremely complicated machine running it — the cell.

## The Cell Is the Missing Layer

The idea of a "virtual cell" gets interesting here, because it asks a question the simpler "DNA is just a sequence" framing lets you avoid: if DNA is the information, what's the computer that actually runs it?

The honest answer isn't just "the cell" — that undersells how much work it's doing. A cell is, all at once, the processor, the memory, the factory that builds things, the network that carries signals between components, and the physical environment everything happens inside. Genes switch on and off. RNA gets built and broken down. Proteins get assembled and shipped where they're needed. Much of the resulting behavior isn't written down in the DNA as an explicit instruction — it emerges from how these pieces interact.

This has a genuine parallel in computing, worth spelling out once in full. A programmer writes `total = price * quantity`. That means something clear to them: multiply price by quantity. But the processor never sees that idea. By the time the code reaches it, a compiler has translated it into raw instructions — move this number into this memory slot, multiply two registers, store the result. The processor deals only in registers, addresses, and voltages; it has no concept of "price" at all. DNA doesn't fully explain the cell, any more than one line of code fully explains what the processor is actually doing. In both cases the sequence alone isn't the interesting object. What matters is the sequence plus the machine reading it plus everything that machine does as a result.

## The Virtual Cell Experiment

This isn't just a thought experiment — someone is building it. Silvana Konermann, co-founder and executive director of the Arc Institute, spent years before that as a CRISPR researcher, helping develop tools that let scientists precisely turn genes on and off. She's now leading an attempt to build an AI model that predicts how a cell responds when you deliberately change something about it.

Arc calls this the Virtual Cell Initiative, and it's easy to reach for a shorthand — "GPT for cells" — that's almost exactly the wrong way to think about it. A language model receives enormous quantities of what humans happened to write. The virtual-cell project is trying to receive something a text corpus essentially never provides cleanly: cell states paired with deliberate interventions and their measured consequences. The project received just over a billion dollars in funding in 2025 specifically to scale this up, with a plan to run more than a billion individual cell experiments — not to describe biology the way a textbook does, but to generate exactly the kind of controlled evidence about cause and effect that separates a rooster's crow from an actual trigger.

## Change Something and See What Happens

So far: machines can learn their own representations, but pattern-learning alone can't get past correlation — which is exactly the wall biology runs into too, with the rooster and the sunrise. This is the pivot the whole chapter has been building toward: it turns out there isn't just one further stage past prediction, but two — and together with the two stages already on the table, they form a ladder worth naming explicitly, because everything from here on — Arc, the virtual cell, the environment-as-teacher — climbs it:

1. **Pattern learning** — noticing what tends to go with what. Watch enough transactions, enough sentences, enough mornings, and a system gets good at noticing regularities.
2. **Prediction** — given what's happened so far, what happens next. That's already a real capability; it's most of what today's large language models do.
3. **Intervention** — not watching and predicting, but deliberately changing something and observing what happens as a result. Suppress a gene and measure the cell again. Maybe one downstream gene falls, another rises, a third barely moves. That's the difference between the rooster crowing before sunrise and someone actually silencing the rooster to see if the sun still comes up. But a single intervention doesn't hand over the whole causal mechanism — if suppressing gene A makes gene B fall, A might act on B directly, or A might act on some gene C which then acts on B, or the intervention might have side effects nobody anticipated. One experiment narrows things down. It rarely settles them.
4. **Chosen experiment** — not simply running an experiment you're handed, but deciding which intervention would most efficiently distinguish between the competing explanations still on the table, specifically because it would teach you the most. That's the shift from a system that predicts to one that investigates — and it's repeated, systematic intervention across many conditions, not any one experiment, that actually earns the name "causal knowledge."

Biology forces this distinction into the open because the stakes of getting it wrong are so visible. Suppress the same gene in a different type of cell and the response might look completely different. Change two genes at once and the combined effect might not simply add the two separate effects together. The number of possible experiments — genes, combinations, cell types, doses — is far too large to test one at a time in a real lab, which is exactly why a model that can predict the outcome of an experiment nobody has actually run is worth building in the first place.

## From Prediction to Experimental Intelligence

Put together, this reframes what a virtual cell is trying to be. A language model learns a model of language. A virtual cell is trying to learn a model of change — current state, then a deliberate intervention, then a predicted new state, then a real measurement to check the prediction, then a correction based on how far off it was. That loop has more in common with the kind of learning used in robotics and game-playing AI than with ordinary next-word prediction.

That loop also determines where the training data has to come from, and it exposes a real asymmetry between biology and language. A language model inherits a head start — humans have been writing for thousands of years, so text is already sitting there to train on. Biology comes with no equivalent stockpile. Nature never wrote a textbook explaining why one gene controls another; scientists have to generate that information themselves, one experiment at a time, which makes building a virtual cell partly a data-generation problem before it's a modeling problem.

That's where the fourth stage from the last section stops being hypothetical. A model can be used to spot where its own predictions are least confident, suggest the specific experiment most likely to resolve that uncertainty, have a lab actually run it, and feed the result back in to improve — which then exposes a new gap, and the cycle repeats. At that point AI isn't just reading science faster than a human could. It's starting to influence which experiments get run in the first place.

The important step underneath all of this isn't merely learning another representation. It's learning a **causal representation**: an internal model useful not just for predicting what tends to happen, but for predicting what would happen *if* something were changed. Pattern learning produces a representation good at answering "what usually comes next." Repeated, chosen intervention pushes a system toward a representation good at answering "what would happen if I reached in and changed this." That's a different kind of knowledge, not just a more accurate version of the first kind — and it's the one worth watching for as this argument moves into machine code.

## Four Ways a Machine Can Learn

The four stages aren't unique to biology, and they aren't strict historical generations — a system built one way doesn't retire the ones before it, and the four can coexist in the same system. They describe increasingly direct ways a machine can learn from the world: first from rules humans provide, then from observations, then from the consequences of its own actions, and finally by choosing which actions or experiments would teach it the most. Run that loop enough times and the phrase "the environment becomes the teacher" stops being a metaphor. It's a literal description of where the training signal is coming from.

The same four-part shape shows up outside biology too, once you know to look for it. A coding AI can write a program, compile it, run it, measure how fast it is, and revise — action, then a consequence, then an update, with no human observation in between. A robot can try a physical action, watch what happens, and adjust its behavior the same way. Strip away the field-specific vocabulary and the loop is identical every time: try something, see what happens, learn from it. That loop may matter more to the future of AI than the usual categories of language models, vision models, and biology models, because it's a claim about *how* a system learns, not about which kind of data it happens to be fed.

## Finding the Right Abstraction

None of this requires a system to capture every detail of reality to be useful. A weather model doesn't simulate every molecule in the atmosphere; a flight simulator doesn't track every electron in an aircraft's wiring. A model only needs to keep whichever part of reality matters for the question being asked of it — which points at something worth carrying forward: intelligence might partly be the skill of finding the right simplified version of a system. A perfect simulation of a cell would be impossibly complicated to build, let alone run. A useful virtual cell just needs to preserve enough of the cause-and-effect structure to answer the questions people actually care about. That's exactly what a good abstraction does — it throws away detail that doesn't matter and keeps what does.

This is really what the whole chapter has been circling. The deepest question isn't whether AI can learn our abstractions. It's whether our abstractions are the ones AI would have invented for itself, if nobody had handed them over in advance. Historically the sequence has always run one direction: humans observe reality, humans invent abstractions, humans encode those abstractions into languages and tools, and machines operate through them. The genuinely speculative possibility this chapter has been building toward runs the other way: an AI observes a system and its consequences directly, and discovers its own abstraction, and reasons through that instead.

Why should experiments lead to new abstractions at all? Because a model trying to predict the result of thousands or millions of interventions needs some efficient internal way to organize what it discovers. If certain variables repeatedly move together, or certain interventions keep producing the same family of consequences, treating them as one higher-level thing can make prediction easier — fewer moving parts to track, more of the pattern explained at once. Humans do this constantly: we compress countless details into concepts like "heart," "inflation," or "function," precisely because the compression is useful. A machine under the same pressure to predict consequences, across enough experiments, may discover useful shortcuts of its own kind. The difference is that nobody guarantees those shortcuts will be ours.

A concrete example helps here, because "abstraction" can sound abstract in exactly the wrong way. A heart is an abstraction — when you talk about what a heart does, you don't think about billions of molecular interactions, you just think "it pumps blood." A function, in programming, is the same kind of shortcut: you don't think about the thousands of processor operations a function call triggers, you just think about what the function does. An abstraction is simply a useful higher-level idea that lets you ignore a mountain of lower-level detail. The question worth asking is what abstractions an AI would invent for itself, if nobody handed it "heart" or "function" in advance — and whether we'd even recognize them as useful once it did.

One caveat is worth holding onto before pushing further, because it's easy to let the argument run ahead of itself. There are really three increasingly strong claims stacked on top of each other, and getting the first one doesn't hand you the next: a model predicting correctly doesn't necessarily mean it learned the true causal structure behind that prediction, and even a model that did learn the true causal structure doesn't necessarily mean a human could understand the representation it learned. Arc's 2026 Virtual Cell Challenge is built specifically to probe the first gap, asking models to predict a cell's response in a cell type they've never seen perturbed before — a real test of generalizing to something new, not memorizing familiar patterns. Success there would mean the model carried genuine biological structure into an unfamiliar situation. It wouldn't prove the model found the actual mechanism a biologist would recognize; it might just have learned an extremely effective shortcut. The genuinely interesting possibility, the one worth sitting with, is a model that possesses an extremely useful causal representation that works reliably while remaining difficult to translate into any human concept at all — getting the right answer well before, and perhaps instead of, anything resembling an explanation a person could follow.

## Why Computation Is the Perfect Laboratory

Biology forces this whole argument into the open, but it also happens to be an unusually expensive place to run it. That reframes the question this chapter has to ask about machine code: not "what happens if we train an AI on machine code," which treats code the way an early genome model treats DNA — a sequence to pattern-match — but: what happens if we train an AI directly on the relationship between machine instructions and what those instructions actually cause? DNA alone wasn't enough to explain a cell. Machine code alone may not be enough either. In both cases the useful signal isn't the sequence sitting there by itself. It's cause and effect: change the gene, watch the cell; change the program, run it, measure what happens.

The computing version of that loop has advantages the biological version doesn't. Biological experiments are slow, expensive, and sometimes impossible to run for ethical reasons — a cell may take hours or days to respond, an organism can take years, a clinical trial longer still. Computers don't share that constraint, and the advantage isn't only speed. A computational experiment can be reproduced from the exact same starting state every time, changed in exactly one variable, rerun, measured precisely, and reset to try again — none of which a biologist can promise a cell. A candidate program can run millions of times in an afternoon; a compiler can test thousands of optimizations before lunch; a simulator can explore billions of states without anyone waiting on a lab result. Cheap and fast are only half of it. Repeatable, precisely measurable, and resettable are what make computation something closer to an ideal laboratory for an AI trying to learn cause and effect.

Picture a model generating ten thousand candidate versions of a sorting program. Each compiles and runs immediately. The ones that don't work get discarded on the spot. The survivors get measured for speed and efficiency, and the best become training examples for the next round. Repeat that cycle enough times, and the model stops learning mainly from human descriptions of what good code looks like. It starts learning from code that has actually been tested and shown to work — and it may increasingly stop learning primarily *how humans say software should be built*, and start learning *what computational structures actually produce the desired consequence*, which is a genuinely different thing to have learned.

## Machine-Native AI

Human civilization has spent centuries building layers of abstraction for computing — programming languages, compilers, operating systems — and they're genuinely powerful. They let ordinary people control machines without understanding a single transistor. But they were built around human limitations: human hands, human short-term memory, human patience for detail. They weren't built around whatever representation would actually be most efficient for a machine to think in. A machine doesn't necessarily need "price" and "total" as separate concepts the way a programmer does. A sufficiently capable AI, trained the way the last several sections describe — on consequences, not descriptions — might land on its own representations instead: hard for a person to read, extremely effective for prediction and design.

By *machine-native*, I don't simply mean an internal representation humans can't read — neural networks already contain plenty of those. I mean something stronger: a useful higher-level abstraction discovered through interaction with a system, optimized for predicting and controlling that system rather than for fitting the conceptual categories humans already use.

So the real question isn't whether AI will replace programming languages. It's whether AI could discover computational concepts better suited to machines than the ones humans built for their own convenience. Biology gives real reason to take that seriously: evolution never designed cells with human comprehension in mind. It produced mechanisms that simply worked, judged purely by whether they helped something survive and reproduce, with no regard for whether a person could later make sense of them. Whatever concepts are embedded in biological systems were never obligated to be the ones a human engineer would have chosen.

## Three Ways AI Can Know the World

Two different questions have been running through this chapter side by side. One is about *what kind of knowledge* a system is acquiring — the four-stage ladder from earlier, running from noticing patterns to choosing its own experiments. The other is about *where the information comes from* in the first place, and the two are independent: a system can sit anywhere on the ladder while drawing on any of the three sources below. The virtual cell model, for instance, is domain-native because its knowledge comes from the structure of biology itself rather than a human's description of it — that answers *where*. Once it starts choosing which perturbation would resolve the most uncertainty, it's also at the chosen-experiment stage — that answers *what kind*. Same system, two different axes.

**Language-native AI** learns from human descriptions of the world and answers back in kind — where most of today's large language models live.

**Domain-native AI** learns directly from the structure of a domain rather than a human's description of it. Genome models like Evo and virtual-cell models like Arc's are early examples.

**Reality-native AI** learns continuously from acting and observing the consequences, rather than from any fixed dataset — it acts, the world responds, the model updates, and it acts again, in a loop with no natural end point. It isn't learning what humans wrote about the world. It's learning what the world does when touched.

It would be a mistake to end on the idea that machine-native AI simply replaces language models. The more interesting possibility is that all three end up working together, each supplying something the others can't: language tells you what a person actually wants, domain-specific models understand how the underlying system works, and reality is the only honest judge of whether an attempt succeeded. A full architecture might run: intent expressed in language, translated into a plan; the plan handed to whichever domain-specific model understands the system in question — genetic, cellular, computational; that model produces an action or design in its own native representation; the result tested against reality itself, with the outcome fed back to correct every layer above it. Language becomes the interface to what people want. Domain models become the specialized reasoning engines. Reality becomes the judge.

## From DNA to Machine-Native AI

The story started with a baby learning what a dog is, because that's the least mysterious version of the idea this entire chapter depends on: useful representations don't have to be handed down, they can be discovered. DNA came next because it introduces a different but complementary lesson: a simple low-level sequence can participate in producing staggering complexity once it is interpreted by the right system — in this case, the cell. The virtual cell came after that because a sequence alone was never going to be enough; only a system that could deliberately intervene, and watch the consequence, had any chance of learning cause and effect rather than mere correlation. And machine code came last because it offers something biology can't: millions of cheap, fast, precisely measured experiments, run by a model free to discover whatever internal representation actually works, rather than the one a human happened to find convenient.

The same question sits underneath every stage of that progression: how much can a learning system figure out for itself, once it stops being handed our concepts and starts being given raw sequences, real environments, and real consequences instead?

The deeper progression underneath the chapter is therefore:

### What the machine may learn

**Patterns → Learned Representations → Causal Representations → Machine-Native Abstractions**

The machine moves from discovering what tends to happen, to discovering what happens when things change, to potentially discovering its own way of representing why.

Maybe the honest answer is "not much," and human-built abstractions prove indispensable after all. Or maybe this is an early stage of something bigger — machines that build their own representations, not because they're translating human thought into a form a computer can process, but because those representations are genuinely native to the systems the machine is trying to understand and control. Humans spent thousands of years building abstractions to make the world make sense to us. The next stage of AI might build its own abstractions simply because they make the world predictable and controllable to the machine itself — whether or not any person could ever read them.

If that turns out to be true, the interesting question stops being *can AI think like a human*. It becomes something stranger, and probably more important: what does thinking actually look like, once the intelligence doing it is free to choose its own way of representing the world?
