## The Revolution We Cannot Afford to Avoid

Some industries will adopt AI because it offers a competitive advantage. Others will adopt it because maintaining essential services without it is becoming too costly.

That distinction is the organising idea of this essay. Most discussion of AI's future concerns breakthroughs, corporate profits and rivalry between the United States and China. These matter, but they may obscure a more persistent force. Over the next twenty-five years, ageing populations, climate volatility, water and energy stress, and deteriorating infrastructure will raise the cost of keeping essential systems running with conventional methods, while skilled labour and public budgets stay constrained.

**The decisive economic driver of AI adoption over the next twenty-five years may not be the pursuit of greater profit, but the rising cost of maintaining essential services with conventional methods.**

The claim is deliberately conditional. AI will not be the only answer, and it will not succeed everywhere. Some problems will be better addressed through policy reform, conventional engineering or additional investment. The thesis is that where essential demand keeps rising and human labour becomes scarcer or dearer, AI combined with machinery will increasingly become part of how acceptable service levels are maintained.

### Three claims, three levels of confidence

The argument rests on three separate claims, and they deserve different levels of confidence.

1. **The problem is becoming more severe.** This is strong. Demographic ageing is largely already determined for the next quarter-century, and climate and infrastructure pressures are well documented.
2. **AI is a credible way to address it.** This is plausible but varies by application. Some uses are already proven; others remain speculative.
3. **Societies will therefore need AI because other solutions will no longer suffice.** This is the boldest claim, and the one that needs the most qualification. Alternatives exist in every sector, and the essay treats AI as one tool among several that becomes harder to do without, not as a universal necessity.

Wherever this essay says AI "will" or "must" matter, it means claim 3 under the conditions stated. Where the evidence is thinner, it says so.

### A hierarchy of necessity

Not every application has an equal claim. The sectors discussed here fall into three tiers, ranked by how hard the underlying need is to defer. The ranking is analytical, not universal: a water-scarce country may put water first, an ageing one care.

| Tier | Need | Sectors | Why it ranks here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Physical and demographic necessities | Cannot be deferred without direct harm to people | Elderly care and health capacity; food security and climate adaptation; water; reliable energy | Failure means illness, hunger or loss of basic services |
| 2. Resilience of essential systems | Can be deferred for a time at rising cost | Infrastructure maintenance; disaster response; construction and housing | Neglect compounds: small defects become failures, and events become disasters |
| 3. Important enabling applications | Valuable, but alternatives often exist | Autonomous mobility; waste and sanitation; other service efficiencies | Other approaches can fill much of the gap |

### Three layers of technology

"AI" is often used loosely. The thesis concerns the business case for AI, not for automation in general, so it helps to separate three layers:

1. **Digital intelligence:** prediction, diagnosis, optimisation and decision support.
2. **Physical automation:** machines that move, lift, harvest, inspect or repair, many of which work without any intelligence in the strict sense.
3. **Integrated autonomous systems:** AI that perceives conditions, decides and directs machinery with limited human intervention.

The third layer matters most for labour scarcity, because advice cannot lift a patient or repair a bridge. It is also the hardest to make safe and reliable outside controlled settings. Throughout this essay, claims about layer 1 are generally firmer than claims about layer 3.

### How each sector is tested

For each major sector the essay asks the same five questions: what essential need is growing; why existing approaches are becoming inadequate or expensive; what specific bottleneck AI or robotics can relieve; what evidence suggests it can work; and under what conditions adoption would fail. Where evidence is illustrative or approximate, the text says so.

## 1. Demographic Pressure: More Care, Fewer Carers

Population ageing will raise the cost of care faster than conventional methods can absorb it, unless something raises productivity in care itself. That conditional is the heart of the demographic argument.

### The problem

In many developed countries, and increasingly in middle-income ones, the share of people over 65 is rising relative to the working-age population. Japan is the best-known case, with roughly three in ten people already over 65 (approximate). South Korea, Italy, Germany and China are on similar paths, several of them faster. Fertility in much of East Asia and southern Europe sits well below the replacement level of about 2.1 children per woman; South Korea's has been below one in recent years (approximate). Globally, the over-65 share is on course to rise from about one in ten today to roughly one in six by mid-century, according to United Nations projections (approximate; to be checked against the latest edition).

Because the working-age population of 2051 has largely already been born, this is among the least uncertain long-range forecasts available. The strength of the pressure is debatable; its direction is not.

The pressure takes the form of a double burden. Demand for pensions, healthcare and long-term care rises, while the workforce that pays taxes and social insurance contributions, and that provides care, grows more slowly or shrinks. Economists track this with the old-age dependency ratio, the number of people past retirement age for every hundred of working age. As it climbs, each worker, directly or through taxes, supports more people.

Care is also hard to make cheaper. Under what economists call Baumol's cost disease, sectors whose output depends mainly on human time, such as nursing and personal care, see slow productivity growth, yet must still raise wages to compete for workers. Their relative cost therefore rises over time. An hour of bedside care takes an hour, however advanced the surrounding economy.

Spending is not mechanically determined. It depends on retirement ages, healthy life expectancy, care models, wages, public policy and how much support families provide. A richer society can also choose to spend more. The defensible claim is that the share of national income devoted to health and long-term care is likely to rise unless offset by productivity gains or policy changes. Recent OECD work on fiscal pressure from ageing sets out exactly this range of responses.

### The alternatives, and why they are not enough alone

Ageing societies have many tools besides AI:

- Pension reform, later retirement and higher labour-force participation.
- Immigration, and better pay and conditions to attract and keep care workers.
- Preventive healthcare and redesigned models of care, such as care at home and integrated health and social services.
- Conventional mechanisation, infrastructure investment and better management.

Each helps, and in some countries a few of them will do most of the work. But each has limits. Retirement ages cannot rise indefinitely, particularly for physically demanding jobs. Countries compete for care workers, and the source countries are ageing too. Immigration is also politically contested in many of the places where the need is greatest. Prevention reduces some demand but does not abolish the need for hands-on help in very old age.

So the realistic position is not that AI replaces these measures. It is that after they have been used, a gap may remain between the care required and the human labour available, and AI and robotics are among the few tools that can narrow it.

### The bottleneck AI can relieve

The relevant bottleneck is the amount of skilled human time per person cared for. Three layers of technology address it differently:

- **Digital intelligence** can reduce documentation, scheduling and triage burdens. Clinicians already spend a large share of their working time on administration; returning part of it to patients is a direct productivity gain. Monitoring through wearables, home sensors and fall detection may also catch deterioration earlier and reduce emergency admissions.
- **Physical automation** such as powered lifting aids and transfer devices reduces injuries, which are a leading cause of absence and turnover among care staff.
- **Integrated autonomous systems** that could perform some physical tasks themselves are the most speculative and the furthest from routine use in homes and care facilities.

No single application solves the problem. Together they could raise the number of people a limited workforce can support. The goal is not to remove human caregivers but to protect the human element of care by shifting the time spent on tasks machines can do adequately. The best model is a nurse, a device and a monitoring system together looking after more people, with the nurse's time concentrated on judgement, comfort and company.

### Conditions for failure

Adoption would fail, or do harm, if devices are unreliable in real homes; if monitoring is intrusive or lacks consent; if savings are taken by cutting staff rather than reinvested in quality; or if machines leave people lonelier. Companionship and dignity are central to good care, so standards, evidence and the consent of those receiving care must govern deployment.

## 2. The Economics of Care: A Worked Example

AI-assisted care is unlikely to be dramatically cheaper. Its stronger case is that it may make care deliverable when the staff to provide it cannot be found. A worked example shows why.

The figures below are **illustrative assumptions, not measured results**. They describe a stylised 30-resident care unit over one year and are chosen to be plausible, not to prove a point. Real results will vary widely by country, wage level and technology.

| Cost line (USD per year) | Conventional | AI-assisted | Who bears it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Care staff (24 vs 22 full-time posts at $55,000) | 1,320,000 | 1,210,000 | Care provider |
| Recruitment and turnover | 56,000 | 40,000 | Care provider |
| Staff injuries and absence | 60,000 | 35,000 | Care provider |
| Agency and overtime cover | 120,000 | 60,000 | Care provider |
| Technology: purchase annualised, maintenance, training, supervision | 0 | 175,000 | Care provider |
| Avoidable hospital transfers (10 vs 7 at $9,000) | 90,000 | 63,000 | Health system |
| **Total** | **1,646,000** | **1,583,000** |  |

The assumptions behind the second column: devices and monitoring reduce the need for two posts through lighter documentation and fewer lifting tasks; turnover, injuries and agency use fall as work becomes less physically punishing; and earlier detection avoids three hospital transfers a year.

### What the example shows

- **The saving is modest.** About $63,000, or roughly 4% of total cost. The technology is not free, and it takes up a large share of the benefit.
- **The provider and the payer are different parties.** The care provider saves about $36,000 net ($1,556,000 against $1,520,000); the remaining $27,000 accrues to the health system as fewer transfers. A provider assessing only its own books sees a thin case, and may not buy.
- **The case is fragile.** If the technology costs 50% more than assumed, the total saving turns into a loss of roughly $25,000. If staff do not actually change how they work, the savings vanish and the cost remains.
- **The decisive value is not in the table.** If the 24 posts of the conventional model cannot be recruited at any reasonable wage, the realistic alternative is not $1.32 million of staff cost. It is unsafe staffing, closed beds or longer waits. The relevant comparison then is between a service that exists and one that does not.

This is the pattern the thesis predicts. In essential services, the argument for AI rests less on beating a competent human alternative on cost and more on keeping a service running when that alternative is unavailable. The same exercise could be repeated for water use in irrigation or for predictive maintenance of infrastructure, each with its own assumptions; none should be taken as proven until measured in real deployments.

One further caution: lower cost per hour of care can lead to more hours being delivered, as access widens. That is a benefit, but it means total spending may rise even when each unit of care is cheaper. The test of success is sustainable total cost at maintained quality, not falling spending.

### The mobility gap

Autonomous driving is often presented as a necessity for older people. That claim needs qualifying. What older people need is reliable, affordable mobility. Autonomous vehicles could provide it in some settings, but accessible public transport, community transport, remote services and human-driven ride services may serve better in others.

The stronger argument is that ageing societies will face a growing mobility gap: more people unable to drive, while relatives and paid drivers are not always available. Autonomous vehicles could help close it, especially in car-dependent rural and suburban areas, and could ease the burden on family carers, many of whom reduce paid work to provide unpaid transport and support.

To count as survival-critical, the case would have to show where alternatives cannot economically fill the gap and where autonomous vehicles can operate safely and reliably. Until then, mobility is best classed as a Tier 3 application: high potential, not an assured universal necessity.

## 3. Food, Water, Energy and Infrastructure: One System, Not Four

AI's strategic value in these sectors lies less in any single application than in coordinating systems that depend on one another. Drought reduces crop yields and hydropower, raises cooling demand and intensifies competition for water; a weak grid limits pumping and treatment; poor roads and ports delay food and repairs. Treating each sector separately understates both the risk and the opportunity.

### Food and climate adaptation

**The need.** Food production depends on conditions that are becoming less predictable. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change concludes that climate change is increasingly pressing on food production and food security, with risks rising as warming increases. This does not mean every harvest will decline; some regions may gain for a time. The central problem is volatility, since systems tuned to stable conditions cope poorly with extremes, and poor harvests in several major producing regions at once can transmit shocks worldwide through prices and trade.

**The alternatives.** Adaptation does not depend on AI. Resilient crop varieties, diversified crops, soil conservation, sustainable irrigation and changed planting practices all matter, and the IPCC lists many such measures. AI cannot compensate for absent water, unaffordable inputs, depleted soils or institutions that cannot deliver support.

**The bottleneck AI can relieve.** AI's value lies in making adaptation more localised, timely and scalable:

- Matching irrigation and fertiliser to conditions field by field, rather than by region or calendar.
- Detecting pests, disease and crop stress early, using imagery and sensors.
- Improving seasonal forecasts for planting and harvest decisions.
- Speeding the selection of heat-, drought- and salt-tolerant varieties.
- Automating labour-intensive tasks during planting and harvest peaks, where seasonal shortages already limit output.

**The evidence and its limits.** Pilot results for precision tools are encouraging in well-resourced farms, but the essay does not claim that AI outperforms low-tech methods everywhere. The FAO's work on digitalisation and automation in lower- and middle-income countries identifies investment costs, limited digital skills and weak enabling environments as major barriers for small-scale producers. Smallholders produce a large share of the world's food, so a technology that works only for large farms leaves much of the problem untouched.

**Failure conditions.** Adoption fails where tools are too costly or fragile for smallholders, where connectivity and extension services are lacking, or where data and equipment are controlled by a few suppliers. The risk is a divide in which countries already strong in agricultural science, capital and infrastructure extend their lead.

### Water

Water is indispensable to households, agriculture, industry and public health. Yet many societies manage competing demands, ageing networks, contamination risks and increasingly variable rainfall. In some cities a substantial share of treated water is lost to leaks before it reaches customers. AI can help detect leaks from pressure and acoustic data, forecast availability, optimise reservoir operation and allocate scarce supplies. As with agriculture, it does not create water; it reduces waste and improves decisions about a limited resource.

### Energy

Economies will need reliable power for homes, hospitals, transport, industry and the computing infrastructure AI itself depends on. Grids must balance supply and demand moment by moment while integrating variable renewable generation, storage and equipment that can fail or age. AI can improve demand forecasting, spot faults, coordinate storage and generation, and help operators get more from existing infrastructure, which is often quicker and cheaper than building new capacity.

There is a complication. AI is a growing consumer of electricity and, in some places, of water for cooling. The International Energy Agency's analysis of AI and energy examines both its rising electricity footprint and its potential to improve energy-system operation, and it presents a range of demand trajectories rather than a single forecast. Data centres accounted for roughly 1.5% of global electricity use in 2024, with projections of roughly a doubling by 2030 (approximate; to be checked against the latest IEA figures). The case for AI in essential services therefore holds only if the value it delivers exceeds the resources it consumes, and it argues for treating computing as part of national infrastructure planning rather than an afterthought.

### Infrastructure maintenance

Bridges, roads, railways, pipelines, dams and water systems need constant upkeep. Replacement is expensive, and neglect compounds: small defects become failures and interruptions. Drones, sensors and AI-assisted inspection can find defects earlier and shift maintenance from fixed schedules or emergency repair to repair timed by condition, directing limited budgets to the most urgent needs.

These applications attract less attention than AI doctors or self-driving cars, yet they address persistent obligations. A society can postpone buying a consumer gadget. It cannot indefinitely postpone clean water, electricity, working transport and safe structures. That is why demand in these sectors is less dependent on consumer enthusiasm or fashion: it persists through downturns, and the consequences of failure can be severe.

AI will not be indispensable in every part of these systems. Conventional engineering, investment and competent management remain essential. But as systems grow more complex and the cost of skilled human monitoring rises, AI-assisted management may become harder to dispense with.

### Construction and housing

Ageing societies still need homes, hospitals, care facilities and infrastructure, and they need them built by a shrinking pool of skilled tradespeople, many of whom are themselves ageing. Labour shortages and rising construction costs threaten the supply of exactly the buildings an older population requires. AI combined with machinery could improve site planning, inspection, excavation and repetitive tasks, and help coordinate complex projects. This is a natural application of the physical-AI argument made in section 6, and it belongs in Tier 2: construction can be delayed, but delay raises costs elsewhere.

## 4. Disaster Response and Public Health

The case for AI is strongest where delay turns a manageable event into a costly one. Floods, wildfires, heatwaves, storms and disease outbreaks can overwhelm emergency and health services, and their frequency or severity may rise as the climate changes and populations age. Older people are more vulnerable to heat, flooding and infection, which links this section directly to the demographic argument.

### Disaster prevention and response

**The need.** Extreme weather is expected to become more frequent or intense in many regions, and the cost of a poorly prepared response rises steeply with each hour of delay.

**The alternatives.** Preparedness does not depend on AI. Early-warning systems, land-use planning, building standards, flood defences, trained volunteers and well-rehearsed evacuation plans save lives and money. Many of the largest gains come from these.

**The bottleneck AI can relieve.** AI can improve the speed and resolution of forecasts, help target warnings, plan evacuation routes, allocate scarce resources such as crews and equipment, and assess damage quickly from satellite and drone imagery so that aid reaches the right places sooner. It can also help predict where infrastructure is most likely to fail under stress.

**Failure conditions.** Forecasts that are not trusted, warnings that do not reach vulnerable people, models trained on past conditions that no longer apply, and over-reliance on automated tools during a crisis can all undermine the benefit. Systems must also keep working when power and communications fail, which is exactly when disasters strike.

### Public health and epidemic surveillance

Health systems that are already stretched can be overwhelmed by a sudden surge. Earlier detection of outbreaks, better monitoring of disease patterns and smarter allocation of scarce beds, staff and supplies could reduce that risk. This overlaps with the healthcare discussion in section 1, so the point here is narrower: surveillance and allocation tools matter most when slack in the system is thinnest, which is increasingly the case in ageing societies. As elsewhere, evidence from real deployments is still limited, and privacy and governance of health data are preconditions rather than afterthoughts.

### Waste, sanitation and environmental monitoring

Waste collection, sewage treatment, pollution monitoring and automated sorting are essential services facing labour, cost and environmental pressures. AI can help, but the case for AI specifically is weaker than the case for reliable sanitation and good automation in general. These applications are best seen as Tier 3: useful, and plausible, without being decisive for the thesis.

## 5. Necessity Does Not Guarantee Adoption

The greater the social necessity of AI, the more dangerous it becomes to assume that the market will provide it at the right time, in the right places and at a price ordinary people can afford. This is the central practical lesson of the argument, not a footnote to it.

### Two kinds of return

In conventional business analysis, a company invests in AI when the expected financial return exceeds the cost. If the technology is too expensive, unreliable or hard to integrate, the company can wait. Essential services change the calculation, because there are two distinct forms of return:

- **Commercial return:** a business cuts costs or raises revenue.
- **Societal return:** a country avoids a crisis, maintains an essential service or prevents future spending that would otherwise become unaffordable.

An AI system that improves drought forecasting, for example, creates value by preventing losses that would fall on farmers, processors, consumers and governments. Its contribution includes the damage avoided, not only the revenue earned by its developer. In sectors facing structural shortages, the second form of return may become decisive, because the alternative to adoption is not the status quo but a service that cannot be sustained.

As the care example in section 2 showed, though, a strong societal return is not the same as a profitable investment. A reasonable case for AI on social grounds can coexist with a weak case on any single purchaser's books. Claims that AI will have a compelling return should therefore be read as conditional on total costs, avoided losses and payback periods being counted properly, and on the technology working in real conditions.

### Why necessary technologies still fail to spread

A society can urgently need a technology and still fail to deploy it. Six obstacles recur:

1. **Costs and benefits fall on different parties.** The buyer pays; the saving appears in someone else's budget.
2. **Governments cannot fund the initial investment,** even where the long-run return is clear.
3. **Regulation and liability make deployment difficult,** particularly where an error could harm a patient or a road user.
4. **Technology that works in demonstrations performs poorly in real conditions,** which are messier and less predictable.
5. **Essential services cannot tolerate failures,** even if average performance is good. A system that is right 99% of the time may still be unacceptable if the 1% is catastrophic.
6. **A country may rely on foreign systems it cannot afford, maintain or control.**

### What follows for policy

These obstacles imply that governments will need to redesign procurement, reimbursement and incentives. Pooled health and social-care budgets, outcome-based payment, long-term purchasing commitments and shared testing and certification for safety-critical systems are examples. Where such reforms fail, the technology may exist yet sit unused, and society will still bear the cost of not using it.

This connects the economics of AI adoption to public procurement, infrastructure policy, ownership and national sovereignty, the themes of the next sections.

## 6. Labour Scarcity and Physical AI

The examples so far share a deeper cause: the growing difficulty of supplying enough human labour to meet society's needs. Economic growth has long depended on a larger workforce, more productive workers and more productive capital. Demographic ageing shifts the balance between those who provide services and those who need them.

The problem is not confined to healthcare. Agriculture needs workers at planting and harvest. Construction needs skilled operators and tradespeople. Infrastructure maintenance needs technicians who can inspect, diagnose and repair complex systems. Energy networks need constant monitoring and specialist expertise. In many countries the skilled trades are ageing faster than the population as a whole.

### Why integration matters

A system that can only advise has limited power to relieve a shortage of people who must physically perform a task. This is why the distinction between the three layers of technology introduced earlier matters:

- **Digital intelligence** helps workers decide better and do paperwork faster. It is the most mature layer and the most widely deployable.
- **Physical automation** moves, lifts, inspects or harvests. Much of it is conventional machinery and does not require intelligence in any strict sense.
- **Integrated autonomous systems** combine perception, decision and action with limited human supervision. These address the labour shortage most directly, but are the hardest to make safe and reliable in unstructured settings such as homes, hospitals and building sites.

The thesis is about the business case for AI, so it is important not to credit AI with gains that come from ordinary automation or better sensors. Where the argument concerns labour scarcity, the strongest claim is for integrated systems, and it is also the one with the least evidence today.

By 2051, some societies may find that autonomous machinery is not merely a way to reduce labour costs. It is a way to maintain production and essential services despite a shortage of workers.

### Why the transition will be slower than the technology

The transition will not be automatic. Robots must operate safely in unpredictable environments, which are far messier than factory floors. Equipment must be maintained and repaired. Workers must be trained to supervise new systems, and many will need to move between occupations. Some tasks will remain hard to automate for decades. The countries that benefit most will be those that integrate machines into functioning organisations rather than merely purchase the technology.

It also bears repeating that deploying machines in place of scarce workers differs from displacing workers who want jobs. Where labour is genuinely scarce, automation can fill gaps. Where it is not, the social effects are very different, and the same technology can cause distress. Which of these situations applies will vary by sector and country, and policy on retraining and transition will matter.

## 7. Ownership, Dependence and National Power

If AI helps sustain essential services, the questions that matter most are political: who owns the systems, who depends on them, and who decides how the gains are shared.

### The welfare-state paradox

AI may help societies sustain pensions, healthcare and social services by raising productivity and reducing the labour needed to provide essential goods and services. But technology does not decide how the resulting wealth is distributed.

Suppose a country automates much care work, transport and manufacturing. The economy may then provide more with fewer workers. But who owns the robots, AI systems and infrastructure responsible for the savings?

If ownership is concentrated, much of the financial return may flow to a few companies and investors, and governments may depend on taxation and redistribution to ensure that older people and others can afford services. If ownership is broader, through pension funds, public wealth funds or cooperative models, a larger share could flow directly to households or collectively owned institutions.

The technology could therefore support very different arrangements: a welfare state preserved within a largely capitalist economy, an expansion of social ownership, or a hybrid of private enterprise and extensive public provision. What AI cannot do by itself is settle who is entitled to the wealth that automated production creates.

### The dependence paradox

There is a second tension. Governments may come to depend on AI to maintain the social contract while lacking direct control over the technology that essential services rely on. If a handful of foreign or private firms run the systems that operate a country's hospitals, grid or farms, then outages, price rises, policy disputes or sanctions become threats to public welfare.

The more indispensable these systems become, the more important their ownership, reliability, accessibility, transparency and governance. Questions that sound technical, such as who holds the data, who can update the software, and what happens if a supplier withdraws, become questions of national resilience.

### A new basis of national power

These developments also have geopolitical consequences. National power has rested on population, industrial capacity, natural resources, finance, scientific knowledge and military strength. AI will influence all of these, but its effect will vary with the capabilities a country already has and the problems it must solve.

Countries with advanced AI, robotics, capital and infrastructure may be able to produce more with fewer workers. Countries with severe labour shortages or environmental pressure may have stronger incentives to adopt automation, though perhaps fewer resources with which to do so.

The result is an uneven landscape:

- Some countries will use AI to reinforce existing advantages in agriculture, medicine, energy and manufacturing.
- Others will depend on foreign technology to keep essential services running.
- Still others may leapfrog older systems, as some countries did by moving directly to mobile telephony, without preserving extensive legacy infrastructure.

It will not be a simple split between advanced and backward states. A country may be highly capable in agricultural AI yet reliant on imported medical technology, or strong in manufacturing automation yet vulnerable to energy shortages. The relevant unit of analysis is increasingly the combination of technological capability, physical resources and institutional competence in particular essential sectors.

The strategic divide by 2051 may therefore be between societies that successfully integrate AI into essential systems and those that cannot. The latter may possess the technology in principle yet remain unable to deploy it effectively or affordably. For weaker countries, access to AI may become as important as access to energy or food, and dependence may arise not from a lack of talent but from an inability to operate independently the systems on which economic resilience rests.

The question will not simply be who develops the most advanced AI. It will be who can turn AI into reliable, accessible and affordable capacity where society needs it most.

## 8. What Would Have to Be True

The forecast is this: by 2051, AI combined with machinery will be part of how ageing, climate-stressed societies maintain care, food, water, energy and infrastructure at acceptable service levels, with the clearest need in Tier 1 sectors and the most uncertain in Tier 3. It does not predict that every technology will succeed or every sector adopt it. The table below separates strong claims from conditional ones.

| Claim | Assessment | What would strengthen it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ageing will intensify pressure on care and public finances | Strong | Demographic projections and fiscal estimates for selected countries |
| Climate change will raise food-system risk and volatility | Strong | Regional climate and yield evidence |
| AI can improve forecasting, monitoring and optimisation | Credible | Real-world results and cost comparisons |
| Robotics can offset some labour shortages | Credible but task-dependent | Evidence on reliability, cost and deployment in real settings |
| AI will have a compelling return in essential services | Conditional | Full cost accounting, avoided losses and payback periods |
| AI will be indispensable to essential services by 2051 | Plausible forecast, not established fact | Defining where alternatives become inadequate |
| AI will shape which countries stay resilient | Plausible geopolitical inference | Comparing national capabilities, dependencies and institutions |

The distinction that matters is between evidence for the problem and evidence for the solution. The problem is well established in several sectors; the best technological response remains uncertain.

### Objections

**"Demographic forecasts are uncertain."** Fertility, migration and longevity can all surprise. But the working-age cohorts of 2051 are largely already born, so the near-term arithmetic is far more certain than most long-range forecasts.

**"Technology has often disappointed in care and public services."** True. Many promised efficiencies in health IT and administration arrived late or not at all. This is why the argument rests on necessity and incentives, not on a guarantee of success. Necessity creates demand; it does not ensure good products or competent implementation.

**"Populations may reject machine care."** Trust is a real constraint, especially in care and driving. Acceptance depends on safety records, transparency, human oversight and culture, which vary widely. Societies with sharper shortages may accept machines sooner.

**"The gains may not reach those who need them."** If gains are captured by owners and not shared, adoption can proceed while the original problem, unaffordable care, persists for ordinary households. This is the distributional point in section 7.

**"AI's own resource demands may undercut the case."** Energy, water, chips and skilled engineers are all constrained. The argument holds only if AI delivers more value in essential services than the resources it consumes.

**"Other solutions may suffice."** Often they will, partly or wholly. The thesis does not require that alternatives fail everywhere, only that in some essential sectors they will not be enough alone.

### Three ways 2051 could look

Scenarios are not predictions, but they clarify what depends on what.

- **Managed adaptation.** Governments reform procurement and funding early; AI and robotics are deployed at scale in care, farming and infrastructure; ownership is reasonably broad; standards are strong. Ageing societies maintain services with a smaller workforce and share the benefits widely.
- **Uneven dependence.** The technology works, but a few suppliers and countries control it. Wealthy, well-organised states adapt; others rent essential capabilities on unfavourable terms. Sovereignty over critical systems becomes a major source of conflict and leverage.
- **Deferred crisis.** Institutions fail to adapt. Technology exists but is blocked by misaligned incentives, regulation, mistrust or cost. Care queues lengthen and infrastructure fails more often, and adoption comes late and in a hurry, under emergency conditions.

Reality will mix these, varying by country and sector.

### Signals to watch

Indicators that would show which way things are heading:

- Whether health and long-term care systems adopt AI and robotics through outcome-based contracts, or only through pilots.
- The real cost per hour of care and the share of national income devoted to it.
- The safety record and market share of autonomous vehicles in suburban and rural areas.
- Take-up of precision-agriculture tools by smallholders, not only large farms.
- How far national grids and water utilities depend on a few foreign vendors.
- The share of AI-generated returns going to broad-based owners such as pension and public funds.
- Measured results, with costs, from AI deployments in infrastructure maintenance and disaster response.

## Conclusion: The Cost of Not Using AI

The AI revolution will be shaped by many forces: competition between major powers, scientific breakthroughs, corporate investment, military applications and the struggle over who owns automated wealth. Beneath them lies a more persistent driver.

Societies are ageing. Climate conditions are changing. Essential infrastructure is expensive to maintain. Governments face growing obligations while labour and public finances are constrained. These pressures will not disappear if AI disappoints investors or falls out of fashion.

AI will not be the only answer, and it will not succeed everywhere. Some problems will be better addressed through policy reform, conventional engineering or additional investment, and not every application will prove worthwhile. AI cannot reverse climate change, abolish the realities of ageing or remove the need for competent government. But where essential demand keeps rising and human labour becomes scarcer or dearer, AI combined with robotics may become necessary to sustain acceptable service levels.

**The defining feature of the next phase of the AI revolution may be that adoption ceases to be a choice between innovation and the status quo. It becomes a choice between using technology to sustain essential systems and accepting a growing risk that those systems can no longer be maintained.**

The societies that fare best will be those that turn AI into reliable, accessible and affordable capacity where it is needed most, and that distribute the benefits in ways their populations can sustain. The ultimate test of AI may be whether it can help preserve the functioning of societies whose needs keep growing while their capacity to meet them comes under strain.

## Evidence Still to Anchor

This revision adds structure, worked reasoning and qualifications, but it is not yet a fully evidenced forecast. Figures marked "approximate" come from general knowledge and must be checked against current editions before publication. The worked example in section 2 is illustrative and should be replaced by measured data where possible. The following sources are the natural anchors:

- **Demography and fiscal pressure:** United Nations *World Population Prospects* (old-age dependency ratios by country to 2050); OECD work on ageing and public finances, including its 2025 working paper on fiscal pressures.
- **Care and health:** national statistics and OECD health and long-term-care data on workforce shortfalls, spending projections and the cost of care technology in real deployments.
- **Climate and food:** IPCC assessment reports on food security and adaptation; FAO analysis of digitalisation and automation for small-scale producers.
- **Energy and AI:** International Energy Agency analysis of AI and energy demand, including its range of demand trajectories.
- **Water, infrastructure and disasters:** utility loss data, infrastructure condition assessments, and evaluations of AI forecasting and inspection tools in real settings.
- **Evidence on AI itself:** peer-reviewed or independently audited results for monitoring, precision agriculture, predictive maintenance and robotics, separating observed outcomes from projections.
