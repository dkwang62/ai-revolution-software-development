# Side Story — The Intelligence That Does Not Need to Be Fast
## What Happens to Enterprise Compute When Attention Becomes Cheap

## 1. The Other Market for Artificial Intelligence

Most people's experience of artificial intelligence creates a misleading impression of what actually matters about it. You type a question into a chatbot and wait for an answer. If nothing appears after a few seconds, the system feels slow. The same is true of an AI coding assistant generating software, a voice assistant answering a question, or an image generator producing a picture — a human is sitting on the other side of the machine, waiting. The industry has, understandably, devoted enormous resources to reducing latency: the time between asking for intelligence and receiving it. That makes perfect sense for interactive AI, and it's the version of AI most people have actually used.

But there's another enormous category of intelligence for which latency barely matters at all. An auditor examining twenty million transactions doesn't need an answer in three seconds. A lawyer searching ten million documents doesn't care whether the analysis takes two hours or eight. A tax authority looking for anomalies across millions of returns can wait until tomorrow morning. For applications like these, the question that matters isn't *how quickly can the machine think* — it's *how cheaply can we afford to let it keep thinking*.

One clarification before going further, since this book is partly about what AI actually is: "intelligence" here doesn't mean consciousness, or anything resembling general human understanding. It means useful cognitive work — reading, comparing, classifying, reasoning, searching, checking, investigating. That narrower, more mechanical meaning is what makes the rest of this chapter's economics work, and it's worth keeping in mind every time the word "intelligence" appears from here on.

## 2. Intelligence Has Always Been Rationed

Human civilization produces vastly more information than humans can examine. Banks process billions of transactions. Companies generate invoices, contracts, emails, support tickets, and accounting records without end. Governments receive tax returns, customs declarations, and regulatory filings by the million. Scientists publish an ocean of papers. Computer systems generate mountains of logs. Aircraft, factories, and power stations continuously produce sensor data nobody is watching in real time. Most of this information receives essentially no human attention — not because it has no value, but because there simply isn't enough economically available human attention to examine all of it.

So institutions invented mechanisms for rationing that attention. Auditors sample transactions rather than examining every one. Tax authorities build risk scores to decide which returns deserve a closer look. Banks set thresholds to determine which suspicious transactions get escalated to a human. Lawyers use search tools to reduce millions of documents down to a manageable pile. Scientists specialize, so that no one has to try to read everything. Security teams triage alerts by severity. Companies investigate only a fraction of customer complaints in real depth.

These practices feel so natural that it's easy to mistake them for intrinsic features of the work itself, rather than what they actually are: responses to scarcity. If an organization has twenty million transactions and enough auditors to investigate twenty thousand of them, it has no choice but to decide which twenty thousand deserve attention. The underlying constraint, in every one of these examples, is the same: human attention is expensive.

It's worth giving this phenomenon a name, because it recurs throughout this chapter. Every organization is surrounded by *dark information* — information it already possesses but cannot economically afford to examine. Invoices nobody investigates. Logs nobody reads. Old support tickets nobody revisits once closed. Contracts nobody rereads after signing, even as the regulations around them change. Sensor anomalies nobody chases down. Scientific papers nobody inside the organization ever sees, despite being directly relevant to its work. None of this is hidden in the sense of being secret. It's hidden in the sense that examining it has never been worth what it would cost. Cheap intelligence, if it becomes cheap enough, illuminates dark information — not by making it secret no longer, but by making it finally worth looking at.

## 3. From Sampling to Examining Everything

Consider financial auditing as the clearest case. An auditor traditionally can't investigate every transaction with the depth of a human investigator, so accounting systems run automated checks, auditors assess risk, transactions get sampled, anomalies get flagged, and humans investigate whatever looks important. Now imagine extremely inexpensive AI agents instead. For every single transaction, an agent could pull up the invoice, the purchase order, the approval chain, the payment, the accounting entry, the supplier's history, and any relevant contract, and ask a battery of questions automatically: Does the invoice match the purchase order? Is the price unusual for this supplier? Has the supplier's bank account changed recently? Are several invoices being split just under an approval threshold? Is the supplier connected to an employee or to another supplier in a way that should have been disclosed?

Most of those investigations would find nothing at all — and that's exactly why the intelligence running them has to be cheap. If investigating a single transaction costs ten dollars, investigating twenty million of them is simply impossible. If it costs a fraction of a cent, the economics flip entirely.

That flip is really the whole chapter compressed into one sentence, and it's worth stating on its own rather than letting it pass by inside a paragraph:

> The auditor's task changes from *finding the transactions worth investigating* to *investigating every transaction and finding the results worth examining.*

That is a considerably more profound change than simply making today's audit cheaper to run. It changes what an audit fundamentally is. An annual audit could gradually become something closer to continuous assurance, with machines quietly examining every transaction overnight and human auditors spending their time on the small number of cases that actually require judgment.

## 4. The Economics of Abundant Attention

It's worth pausing here to name the economic idea underneath that flip, because it explains why the market for this kind of intelligence could turn out to be so much larger than it first looks.

Suppose artificial intelligence reduces the cost of an existing audit from a million dollars to three hundred thousand. That's valuable — but it's a conventional productivity story: roughly the same output, for considerably less money. The more interesting possibility is that the organization keeps spending the full million dollars, and gets a hundred times as much investigation for it instead. Instead of sampling transactions, it examines all of them. Instead of an annual review, it runs continuous analysis. Instead of investigating only the strongest signals, it chases the weak ones too.

This is the difference between a straightforward productivity gain and something closer to demand elasticity: when something becomes dramatically cheaper, an organization doesn't simply spend less money buying the same quantity of it. It finds uses that were previously uneconomic to even attempt. Cheap computing didn't just reduce the cost of the calculations companies were already running in 1960 — it produced billions of devices running calculations nobody in 1960 would have considered worth paying for at all. Cheap storage didn't just make corporate databases less expensive to maintain — it encouraged everyone to start saving photographs, messages, telemetry, and video at a scale nobody would have dreamed of budgeting for a generation earlier. Cheap intelligence looks likely to follow the same curve, and the examples that follow are really a survey of where that new demand shows up first.

## 5. Where Abundant Attention Goes to Work

**Tax authorities could investigate before selecting.** Tax administration has the same structure as auditing. Millions of individuals and companies file returns every year, but a tax authority has only enough skilled investigators to audit a tiny fraction in real depth — so authorities must first decide, from the outside, who even looks suspicious enough to warrant that scarce attention. Cheap machine intelligence could reverse that order entirely: an agent examines every corporate return against prior years, financial statements, related entities, and industry norms, and simply notices things a risk score would have missed — revenue up forty percent while taxable income fell sixty, driven by management fees paid to a related company that increased tenfold, worth tracing directly. Sometimes the answer turns out to be entirely legitimate. The point is that the cost of finding that out becomes small enough that running the investigation was worthwhile anyway. Suspicion no longer has to precede investigation; investigation can start producing the suspicion itself, with a human making the final call.

**Fraud detection becomes fraud investigation.** Credit-card companies already screen transactions in milliseconds, and that layer isn't going anywhere — the merchant and the customer are both standing there waiting, and a system genuinely can't spend ten minutes reasoning about a purchase in real time. But once a transaction gets flagged, it can be handed to a very different kind of machine: a background agent with no clock running, asking *why* it was suspicious, examining previous transactions, devices, merchants, and geographic patterns, and searching for the same pattern across other customers entirely. Traditional fraud systems are excellent at producing a signal. Cheap background intelligence can provide the investigative attention behind it.

**Sanctions and money laundering are problems of relationships.** Checking whether a name appears on a sanctions list is easy. The hard cases involve relationships deliberately built to be obscure — a company in one country paying a company in a second, which buys goods through an intermediary in a third, with the real beneficial owner several corporate layers away. That structure isn't a list. It's a graph, and investigating it requires enormous amounts of searching, reading, and reasoning down threads that mostly lead nowhere — which is exactly why human investigators can't chase all of them, and why cheap agents can.

**Law contains enormous amounts of latent machine work.** A large lawsuit can involve millions of documents, and while search technology already narrows that pile down, the constraint has always been human reading time. Cheap intelligence changes the instruction from *find the documents most likely to matter* to something closer to *read everything* — though it's worth being precise about what that actually means in practice. It doesn't mean pouring ten million documents into a single model's context at once and hoping for the best. It means building a system — retrieval, indexing, repeated targeted searches, structured extraction, cross-checking between agents — capable of systematically examining an entire corpus, rather than a system that simply selects a small fraction of it for a human to read. Legal research follows the same pattern: instead of finding a handful of relevant cases, agents can read hundreds, trace every precedent they cite, and construct the strongest version of an argument on both sides, with a second set of agents set loose specifically to attack the first set's reasoning.

**Science has the same attention problem.** No scientist can read everything published in her own field anymore. A background agent that continuously reads every new paper in a researcher's specific area — extracting hypotheses, methods, and contradictions, and comparing each new result against years of prior literature — doesn't need to read any one paper especially well. It needs to have looked everywhere at once, which no individual scientist ever could.

**Engineering becomes persistent.** A company can tell an agent, at the end of the working day, to examine the entire codebase, find likely bugs and security weaknesses, attempt to fix them, run the full test suite, investigate failures, try again, and hand the team a report by morning — an eight-hour loop of reason, modify, compile, test, fail, and retry that nobody needs to happen in half a second per step, because the number that matters is useful engineering work produced per dollar, not response time.

### The market is much larger than it first appears

Every example above deserves its own full treatment, and each has one. But it would be a mistake to leave the impression that this idea applies to five or six specialist professions. It's closer to a general property of institutional life, and a faster tour of where else it shows up is worth doing here, briefly, precisely to make that scale visible.

Insurance: every claim can receive a real preliminary investigation — policy terms, photographs, repair estimates, the claimant's history, and related parties — instead of only the claims large or unusual enough to trigger one today, and patterns invisible in any single claim can surface once a machine is comparing all of them against each other continuously. Procurement: every purchase can be checked for unusual pricing, duplicate invoices, approvals suspiciously split to dodge a threshold, and undisclosed conflicts of interest, not just the ones large enough to justify an audit. Cybersecurity: every weak alert can receive an actual investigation rather than a risk score and a shrug, with an agent tracing a single odd login attempt back through the same account's history the way a human analyst would if there were enough analysts to go around. Manufacturing: every unusual sensor reading can be compared against maintenance records and the failure histories of similar components, instead of waiting for a pattern obvious enough that a human notices it unassisted after something has already broken. Supply chains: every significant supplier can be watched continuously for financial distress, regulatory trouble, logistics disruption, or geopolitical exposure, rather than reviewed once a year during a formal audit, catching a problem while there's still time to route around it. Customer support: millions of individually handled complaints can be reread collectively, after the fact, to surface a product problem no single agent ever had enough visibility to recognize, because each of them only ever saw one complaint at a time. Corporate accounting: reconciliation can move from a periodic close toward something closer to continuous investigation, catching a miscoded entry the week it happens instead of the quarter it's discovered. Patent analysis: every new filing can be checked automatically against the full prior art and scientific literature, not just whatever a time-constrained examiner or attorney manages to search by hand. Investment research: every company in a portfolio can effectively have a standing background analyst continuously reading its filings, its competitors, and its industry, rather than receiving a fresh look only when an event forces one. Government expenditure: every payment, contract, and grant can receive machine scrutiny before an unusual case ever reaches a human investigator, at a scale no inspector general's office could ever staff to match.

None of these are exotic. Every one of them is an ordinary institutional function that already exists, running today at whatever depth its organization can afford to staff — which is to say, running shallower than anyone involved would prefer. That's the scale this chapter is actually describing: not a handful of clever new AI products, but an entire category of economic activity that has been sitting there, priced out of existence by the cost of attention, waiting for that cost to fall.

## 6. From Jobs to Standing Responsibilities

It's useful to separate three distinct time regimes AI can operate in, because they imply genuinely different things about how the work gets bought and delivered.

**Interactive** intelligence has a human waiting on the other end, and seconds matter — this is the chatbot, the coding assistant, the voice assistant, the world most people already associate with AI. **Batch** intelligence has a deadline rather than a person waiting: complete this analysis by tomorrow morning, and whether any individual step takes five seconds or five minutes inside that window is irrelevant. Most of the examples above, described as a single investigation running overnight, are really describing batch work. But there's a third regime, and it's the more consequential one: **persistent** intelligence, whose job was never to finish. Its instruction isn't *analyze this and report back* — it's *keep watching, and tell me when something matters.*

A sanctions-screening agent doesn't receive a case and close it. Its job is to keep watching the relationships around a company as they shift. A supply-chain agent keeps watching suppliers, not just once a year at renewal. A scientific-literature agent keeps reading new papers indefinitely, not just the ones published this week. A contract-compliance agent keeps comparing existing contracts against regulations that keep changing, long after the ink on the original contract dried.

This is a genuinely different relationship with the technology than the one most people currently have. Today, AI is mostly something you ask: explain this, summarize this, write this, find this. Persistent intelligence is something you assign: watch this, check this continuously, investigate everything, and tell me when something matters. An investment firm could assign a standing agent to every company it follows. A manufacturer could assign one to every product line. A corporation could have agents watching customer complaints, competitor activity, contracts, regulations, cybersecurity events, and financial transactions, all at once, indefinitely. Most of the time, these agents would have nothing to report — and that silence wouldn't represent wasted intelligence. It would mean the organization had purchased something new: standing attention, applied continuously to things that previously received none at all.

## 7. How Cheap Intelligence Gets Manufactured

All of this is a demand-side argument — it explains why organizations will want vastly more cognitive work than they currently buy. Demand of that shape, though, implies a different supply side too, and this is where the story becomes as much about enterprise infrastructure as about AI capability.

Interactive AI carries one non-negotiable constraint: the customer is waiting, so the infrastructure underneath it has to be provisioned for low-latency response no matter the cost. Background, persistent AI removes most of that constraint. If an audit has to finish by eight in the morning, it matters little whether a given piece of the analysis actually ran at eleven at night or four a.m. — and that flexibility opens up infrastructure optimized around cost rather than instantaneous response.

At low or moderate volumes, buying this intelligence from a cloud provider will almost certainly make the most sense, the same way most companies rent compute rather than build their own data center. A cloud provider treating cognitive work the way it already treats ordinary compute could price it in tiers by how much flexibility the buyer is willing to give up: immediate, low-latency intelligence stays expensive because it has to be provisioned as though every request could land at once; intelligence with an hour of slack costs less; intelligence that can wait until overnight costs less still; best-effort background intelligence, run whenever spare capacity happens to be sitting idle, could end up cheapest of all — the cognitive equivalent of a spot-instance market that already exists for ordinary compute. Call this a **public intelligence factory**: shared infrastructure, priced by flexibility, serving many organizations' background workloads out of the same pool of capacity.

But once an organization's demand becomes large enough, predictable enough, and continuous enough — and particularly once its data is sensitive enough that sending it to someone else's servers carries real cost of its own — the economics can start to favor a different model. A bank running continuous background cognition across fraud, sanctions, credit, audit, cybersecurity, and legal work simultaneously may consume enough machine intelligence, on a steady enough schedule, to justify a **private intelligence factory**: dedicated, throughput-optimized processors rather than the lowest-latency chip available; open-weight models the organization can inspect and audit directly; proprietary data and workflows that never leave the building; and evaluation systems built specifically against the institution's own workloads rather than a public benchmark. This isn't the default outcome for most organizations, and it shouldn't be presented as one — it's a threshold that only a genuinely large, continuous, sensitive workload clears. But for the organizations that do clear it, the case is real: sensitive information stays internal, models get tested against the institution's actual work rather than a generic benchmark, and the organization keeps control over the context, tools, and rules surrounding its own intelligence.

Most large enterprises will likely end up somewhere between the two — a **hybrid intelligence factory**, running their predictable, sensitive, high-volume workloads on internal or dedicated capacity, and bursting whatever is irregular, unusually difficult, or simply too small in volume to justify dedicated hardware out to public providers and frontier models instead. None of this displaces the Enterprise Intelligence Layer this book has already described — it's the compute foundation that layer sits on top of. Coordinating agents across a business's data and workflows is one problem; deciding whether the intelligence powering that coordination gets rented by the token, bought in discounted overnight blocks, or run on hardware the enterprise owns outright is a separate, and for a CIO or CFO, an unusually consequential decision — closer to the build-versus-buy calculations organizations have made about data centers for decades than to a choice between chatbot subscriptions.

## 8. Not Every Problem Needs the Best Intelligence

There's a further piece of this economics worth making explicit, because it's part of what actually makes the abundance affordable: cheap intelligence doesn't require *all* intelligence to become equally cheap. It requires organizing intelligence in a hierarchy, and spending the expensive kind only where it earns its cost.

Go back to the twenty million transactions. Nobody wants the most capable model available deeply reasoning about every ordinary, unremarkable invoice — that would be wasteful even if the model itself were cheap, because most of that reasoning would produce nothing. What actually makes the economics work is a cascade: a cheap, fast model screens all twenty million transactions and flags perhaps two hundred thousand as uncertain enough to deserve a second look. A more capable, more expensive model examines those two hundred thousand and narrows them down to five thousand genuinely difficult cases. A frontier-level model, reasoning at real depth, works through those five thousand and surfaces perhaps two hundred that are actually consequential. Only those two hundred reach a human auditor.

The abundance this chapter has been describing doesn't come only from token prices falling in general. It comes from allocating expensive intelligence precisely where it has high marginal value, and letting cheap intelligence handle the overwhelming majority of cases where it doesn't need help at all. That's a genuinely different engineering discipline from simply picking one model and running everything through it — closer to how a hospital triages patients through a chain of increasingly specialized attention, from a nurse's intake screen up through a specialist, than to a single machine processing a queue at one fixed level of effort.

This is also the detail that makes the earlier economics credible rather than merely aspirational. If examining twenty million transactions meant running the most expensive available model on every single one, the audit example wouldn't work at any price point worth discussing — the total bill would dwarf whatever it replaced. What actually makes it affordable is that the vast majority of the twenty million never need more than the cheapest tier at all; the cost of the whole exercise is dominated by the wide, shallow first pass, not by the narrow, expensive final one. The hierarchy is not a nice-to-have refinement on top of the core idea. It's the mechanism that makes "investigate everything" a sentence with real economics behind it, rather than a slogan.

## 9. A Token Is Not Yet a Commodity

One qualification matters here. It's tempting to describe cheap AI as the commoditization of intelligence, the way a kilowatt-hour from one generator is interchangeable with a kilowatt-hour from another. Models don't behave that way yet — different models carry different strengths, reasoning styles, and failure modes, and a workflow carefully built around one model can break when a cheaper one is substituted in.

What actually determines whether cheap intelligence is *usable* intelligence comes down to three things. Capability: can the model actually perform the task in front of it? Trust: can its output be verified well enough for the application it's being used in — a question that matters enormously more for a sanctions decision than for a first-pass invoice check? And continuity: can the organization rely on this model or provider economically, legally, and operationally, given that pricing, licensing, and availability can all shift under a vendor an enterprise doesn't control? An organization that gets all three right ends up building something more durable than access to any single model — a harness of data, tools, evaluation, and business rules that the underlying model sits beneath, and can eventually be swapped out of once it's been tested against the organization's own work rather than a generic benchmark.

## 10. The Scarce Resource Moves

For most of history, organizations have asked one version of the same question: *what is important enough to deserve investigation?*

Cheap machine intelligence may reverse that question. Machines can supply the breadth — reading, checking, comparing, testing, and investigating almost everything that economics previously forced an organization to ignore. Humans remain where scarcity actually matters: judgment, responsibility, and consequential decisions that shouldn't be made any other way.

The question therefore becomes not *what deserves intelligence*, but *what, after intelligence has already looked, deserves human attention next.*

That is the deeper promise of latency-indifferent AI, and the real point underneath the enterprise-compute argument this chapter has been building toward. Not simply cheap tokens. Abundant attention — and abundant attention, unlike a chatbot's response time, is something an enterprise actually has to plan, provision, and pay for on purpose.
