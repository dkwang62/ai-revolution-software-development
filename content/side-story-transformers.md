# Understanding Transformers: From Layers to Context and Coherent Language

Transformers, like Convolutional Neural Networks, are built from layers, and that shared structure is a useful place to start, because it gives us a foothold before anything else gets complicated. In a CNN, an image passes through a sequence of layers, each one transforming the numerical representation handed to it by the layer before. Early layers respond to simple visual patterns; deeper layers combine those patterns into progressively richer representations. A Transformer follows that same basic principle — information enters a layer, gets transformed, passes to the next layer, and gets transformed again. What differs is the tool each architecture relies on to do that transforming. A CNN is built around convolution. A Transformer is built around attention.

That comparison earns its keep because layers, in both architectures, let meaning develop gradually rather than all at once. With a CNN, it helps to picture changing perspective on a photograph: look closely and you see edges, curves, textures; pull back mentally and those details resolve into an eye, a nose, a face, and eventually a whole person. The CNN isn't literally zooming a lens in and out, but its layers do something analogous — combining information from small regions into progressively broader, more abstract visual representations. A Transformer also builds richer representations layer by layer, but it gets there differently. Instead of asking what visual patterns exist in a neighboring patch, each layer asks which pieces of information are relevant to which other pieces of information.

That distinction is easiest to see with an actual sentence: "The bank raised its interest rate because it feared inflation." The word "it," on its own, carries almost no information about what it refers to. We resolve it by relationship — "it" connects to "bank," "bank" connects to "interest rate," "interest rate" connects to "inflation." The meaning isn't sitting inside any one word; it emerges from how the words relate to one another. Discovering and exploiting exactly these kinds of relationships is the whole purpose of attention, the mechanism at the center of every Transformer.

## Attention and Context

Before a Transformer can do anything with a piece of text, it has to break that text into pieces called tokens — a token might be a whole word, part of a word, a punctuation mark, or some other small chunk. Each token then gets converted into a list of numbers, called a vector, through a step called embedding. The network can't compute directly with a concept like "bank" or "inflation"; it needs something numerical its layers can actually manipulate.

What matters is that those initial numerical representations don't stay fixed in isolation. Through attention, every token gets to weigh how relevant every other token is to its own current meaning. Take the word "bank" in two different sentences: "I deposited my money at the bank," and "We sat on the bank of the river." The word itself is identical in both cases, but its meaning clearly isn't. In the first sentence, "bank" leans heavily on its relationship with "deposited" and "money." In the second, it leans on its relationship with "river." Attention is what lets the Transformer build two genuinely different representations of the same word, shaped by whatever surrounds it each time.

This is also one of the clearest ways to see what separates a Transformer from the older architecture it largely replaced for language, the Recurrent Neural Network, or RNN. An RNN reads a sentence in order, carrying forward a single running summary from one word to the next. Information from early in a sentence has to survive that whole chain, position by position, to still matter by the time the network reaches something much later — and a lot can get diluted or lost along the way. A Transformer sidesteps that altogether: a word near the end of a paragraph can attend directly to something important near the beginning, without needing that information to have been carried, relay-style, through every word in between. That's also why it's fair to say a Transformer can examine the relationships inside an entire input more or less all at once. Given the sentence "The bank raised its interest rate because it feared inflation," the model isn't forced to fully process "The," then finish with it, then move to "bank," carrying one single thread of memory through the sentence the way an RNN would. Instead, attention can weigh relationships across many tokens within the same layer, essentially in parallel.

## What the Layers Add

A Transformer doesn't run attention once and arrive at a finished understanding of the text. Whatever one layer produces becomes the input to the next, and attention gets applied again, alongside other transformations, inside that next layer too — and the one after that. Each pass has the chance to refine every token's representation in light of everything the previous pass discovered.

It's worth borrowing the CNN's zooming intuition here, carefully, because it doesn't transfer over perfectly. In a CNN, going deeper genuinely means combining information across a progressively larger stretch of the image — depth and reach grow together. In a Transformer, attention can already connect distant tokens even in an early layer, so the network isn't moving through a fixed hierarchy from word to sentence to paragraph to document the way a CNN moves outward through physical space. What deepens instead, layer after layer, is how thoroughly those relationships get reconsidered and recombined. Each layer takes the relationships the last one found and refines them again, producing something a little richer and more contextual each time. Put simply: tokens turn into relationships, those relationships get folded into richer and richer representations, and what comes out the other end is a broader contextual understanding of the whole passage.

The important word in all of this is representation. A Transformer layer doesn't produce a tidy, human-readable sentence like "this paragraph is about inflation." It produces vectors — large collections of numbers — and those numbers carry information forward for later layers to use. What starts out as a bare representation of a single token gradually becomes a representation of that token *in this particular context*, shaped by everything around it.

If the context in question is an entire document, this same process can, in principle, stretch across that whole document. An idea introduced early on can influence how something much later gets interpreted. If an entire book fits inside what the model can look at in one pass — its context window — information from one chapter can shape how the model reads another. That doesn't mean the model distills the book down into one tidy "meaning of the whole book" summary somewhere. It means the representation of each individual token can be shaped by information drawn from elsewhere in the book.

This gives us one more useful way to separate the two architectures. A CNN is most intuitive in terms of spatial context — how visual information in one part of an image relates to the surrounding, and increasingly broader, regions around it. A Transformer is most intuitive in terms of relational context — how one piece of information ought to be interpreted in light of whatever other information turns out to be relevant to it, wherever in the sequence that information happens to sit.

## The Context Window

The context window is simply the amount of information a model has available to it during a given interaction — the prompt, and depending on the system, any standing instructions, retrieved material, and whatever conversation or generated text is being carried along as context. If a whole book fits inside that window, the Transformer can, in principle, draw on material from anywhere in that book while answering a question about it.

Suppose a book introduces the phrase "intent becomes software" in its first chapter, returns to and develops that idea across several later chapters, and brings it back one final time in the conclusion. As long as all of that material is still within the usable context, the model can interpret the conclusion in light of everything that came before — a genuinely different, richer act than simply reading the final paragraph in isolation.

But fitting inside the context window is not the same thing as understanding every part of it equally well. A very long context brings its own difficulties: details can get overlooked, attention doesn't always land perfectly on what matters most, and information that's technically available doesn't always exert the influence you'd hope for. The context window defines what's *potentially* available to the model. It doesn't guarantee flawless use of every part of it.

This distinction turns out to matter a great deal for understanding how modern AI systems actually work, because there are really three separate sources of information at play, and it helps to keep them straight: weights, context, and tools. Weights are what the model learned during training, baked permanently into the network. Context is whatever information is available during the current interaction. Tools are ways the system can go out and fetch more — searching the internet, pulling a document from a database — bringing new information into the context rather than the model's underlying weights.

## From Context to an Answer

Understanding how a Transformer processes context still leaves the more puzzling question sitting there unanswered: how does all of this actually produce the rich, coherent answers modern language models give us? The surprising part of the answer is that the model isn't secretly composing the whole response somewhere internally and then revealing it all at once. A language model like this generates its answer one token at a time.

Suppose you ask: "Why did Germany lose the Second World War?" That question gets broken into tokens and run through the Transformer's layers, where attention lets the tokens inform one another — building a richly contextualized representation of the whole question. In whatever sense its weights and current activations encode, the model now "knows" that the question concerns Germany, losing a war, the Second World War specifically, and — crucially — that the word "why" is asking for a causal explanation, not a date or a list of battles.

From there, the model calculates a probability across every token that could plausibly come next, and one gets selected. Say the answer begins with "Germany." That single word instantly becomes part of the context, and the model now faces a fresh version of the same prediction problem, this time asking what should follow the question plus the word "Germany." Perhaps "lost" comes next. Now the context includes "Germany lost," and the cycle repeats — every generated token folding into the material available for producing the next one. Generation, in other words, happens in a strict chain, one step after another.

This is an important qualification to the claim, made earlier, that Transformers process information in parallel. The prompt itself — everything you typed in — can genuinely be processed with parallel attention calculations all at once. But an autoregressive model, one that generates by predicting its own next output, still has to produce its final answer sequentially, because the hundredth word of the response depends on the ninety-nine words that came before it already having been written.

## Why "Next-Token Prediction" Is Misleadingly Simple

This is where the whole process starts to seem almost implausible. How can repeatedly guessing one word at a time produce an essay with a coherent argument, historical detail, worked examples, careful qualifications, and an actual conclusion? The phrase "next-token prediction" makes the underlying computation sound far simpler than it really is.

Take an unfinished sentence: "The Allied bombing campaign damaged German industrial capacity, but the more decisive problem was..." Predicting a genuinely good continuation here takes far more than knowing which words commonly trail after "was." It requires some working representation of grammar, of what the word "but" is doing in that sentence, of Germany, of industrial production, of the Second World War as a whole, of cause and effect, of what's already been said and what hasn't been covered yet, and of how a historical explanation is typically built.

That reveals something genuinely significant about how these models end up as capable as they are. To get very good at predicting what comes next in real human language, a large enough network ends up needing to learn a great deal of the structure that sits *beneath* the language itself — grammar, yes, but also concepts, relationships, facts, patterns of reasoning, and the shape of how explanations are usually built. None of that gets programmed in as an explicit rule anywhere. It emerges because representing it turns out to help the model make better predictions, and better predictions are exactly what training is pushing it toward.

If you write "Paris is the capital of…", a good prediction is "France" — and making that prediction reliably requires the model to have some working representation of the relationship between Paris and France specifically, not just a general sense that cities are followed by country names. Change the sentence to "Paris is the capital of France, while Marseille…" and the appropriate continuation shifts immediately, because the context shifted. At real scale, next-token prediction stops being a matter of learning which words tend to follow which other words, and starts requiring genuine sensitivity to the concepts and relationships that make those word sequences mean something in the first place.

## Training Supplies the Knowledge Behind the Prediction

Return to the question about Germany. The Transformer architecture gives a model the machinery for handling relationships and context — but that machinery, on its own, doesn't contain any historical knowledge. If a model genuinely never encountered useful information about the Second World War during training, and nobody supplied any in the prompt either, it simply couldn't answer the question reliably, no matter how sophisticated its attention mechanism is.

In the ordinary case, though, the model encountered enormous quantities of relevant text during training, and the training process repeatedly nudged its weights so that useful conceptual and statistical relationships became embedded throughout the network. The simplified version of that loop is familiar by now: training data goes in, the model predicts something, the error between prediction and reality gets measured as a loss, backpropagation works out how the weights should shift to shrink that error, the weights get adjusted — and that whole cycle repeats an enormous number of times.

What comes out the other side isn't anything like a conventional database entry reading "Germany lost WWII for reasons A, B, and C." Knowledge like that is distributed across the network's learned parameters instead. Relationships among concepts like Germany, the Soviet Union, the Eastern Front, industrial capacity, oil, the United States, military strategy, and logistics are represented diffusely, woven through the weights rather than filed away as discrete facts. When you ask the question, the prompt activates and combines the relevant parts of that learned structure. Attention lets the model relate everything currently in its context, and the process of generating one token at a time turns those internal representations back into language you can read. In short: knowledge learned into the weights, combined with whatever's currently sitting in the context, produces a context-informed prediction for the next token — repeated hundreds or thousands of times until the answer is complete.

## What Happens When the Model Does Not Know Something?

If the relevant knowledge was never adequately captured during training, there are two other routes by which the model can still get what it needs.

The first is simply to put the missing information directly into the context. Imagine a hypothetical model that had somehow learned almost nothing about the Second World War, but was otherwise a highly capable reasoner and writer. Hand it a detailed written history of the war and then ask: based on this material, what were the three most important reasons Germany lost? Now the model doesn't need the specific historical facts pre-loaded into its weights at all — they're sitting right there in the context it's currently working with. In effect, historical material supplied in the context, combined with the model's already-learned reasoning and language ability, is enough to produce a solid answer.

The second route is to give the system access to tools. It might, for instance, search the internet. The search results come back, relevant portions get pulled into the model's working context, and the Transformer processes that retrieved material exactly the way it processes anything else sitting in its context. The model isn't somehow reaching out and attending directly across the entire internet — the search happens first, as a separate step, and only the results that come back get folded into what the model actually sees.

That gives us a genuinely useful three-way distinction, worth keeping in mind whenever you're trying to understand why an AI system answered the way it did: weights are what the model learned, permanently, during training. Context is what the model has available to it right now, in this specific interaction. Tools are the ways the system can go out and fetch more information to add to that context. A surprising amount of how modern AI systems behave becomes clearer once you're keeping those three things separate in your head.

## How Coherence Builds Upon Itself

There's one more reason token-by-token generation manages to produce something coherent rather than a rambling mess: the answer, as it's being written, continually becomes part of its own context.

Suppose the model begins: "Germany's defeat resulted from three major factors." Those words now constrain what a sensible continuation looks like — the model has effectively made a linguistic commitment that there will be exactly three factors to come. If it then writes "First, Germany…", that adds another structural signal on top of the first. Later, producing "Second" becomes highly appropriate given everything written so far, and eventually "Third" becomes appropriate too. The model's own earlier output keeps reshaping the probabilities over what should come next.

None of this requires an explicit rule somewhere saying "if you said three factors, produce exactly three sections." The model has simply seen enormous numbers of real enumerations, essays, explanations, arguments, reports, and conversations during training, and during a later stage called post-training it's also been specifically trained to follow instructions and produce genuinely useful, well-organized responses. The structure it generates becomes self-reinforcing: "three reasons" sets up "First," which gets developed, which sets up "Second," which gets developed, which sets up "Third" — each stage quietly reshaping the context the next prediction has to work with.

This helps explain something that would otherwise seem almost mysterious. The model isn't necessarily planning out the whole paragraph, in any human sense, before it writes the first word. Coherence can emerge progressively instead, because every good continuation leaves behind a better-defined context for whatever continuation comes after it.

## Structure, Format and Style

A language model also picks up, from its training, that different kinds of communication carry different shapes. An essay doesn't look like a shopping list. A piece of code doesn't look like a letter. A legal memo doesn't read like casual conversation. Over the course of pretraining and post-training, the model absorbs the statistical patterns that go with each of these forms, and whatever instructions it's currently given strongly steer which form gets produced.

Ask "Give me three reasons Germany lost WWII," and several constraints land on the model at once — three, reasons, Germany, WWII — pointing it toward an explanatory structure built around three distinguishable causes. Ask instead for a two-thousand-word academic essay on the same topic, and the expected shape changes considerably. Ask for the same explanation in one paragraph, aimed at a twelve-year-old, and it changes again. The Transformer underneath all three requests might be identical. What's actually shifting is the context conditioning what gets generated.

There's a further layer on top of the user's own prompt, too. Modern assistant models go through post-training specifically meant to make them better at following instructions, answering questions well, holding up their end of a conversation, and sticking to whatever behavioral guidelines they've been given. The products built around them often add their own standing instructions as well, shaping tone and presentation further still. That's part of why two assistants, both built on broadly similar Transformer foundations, can end up with noticeably different writing habits — one leaning toward continuous, essay-like prose, another leaning toward headers, short paragraphs, and bullet points. These differences don't necessarily come from any deep difference in how attention works. They're much more likely to come from differences in training, post-training, standing instructions, and overall product design.

## The Transformer Is Both Parallel and Sequential

At this point it's worth resolving what looks like a contradiction. Earlier, the whole point of the Transformer's advantage over the RNN was that it could process relationships among input tokens largely in parallel. But we've also just said that a language model writes its answer one token at a time. Both of those statements are true, and they're true about two different stages of the same process.

When the model is processing an existing prompt — everything you've already typed — it can calculate the attention relationships across every position in that prompt more or less simultaneously, within a single layer. But when it's *generating* new text, each new token has to be produced before the one after it can be, because that next token's prediction depends on everything generated so far, including the token that was just written. So prompt processing is highly parallel, while autoregressive generation — the actual writing of the answer — is fundamentally sequential. Holding onto that distinction clears up a lot of otherwise confusing claims about how fast or how "parallel" these systems really are.

## Returning to Human Intuition

Between the CNN and the Transformer, we now have two complementary intuitions for how a neural network turns raw information into something meaningful.

With a CNN, picture looking at a photograph. You can focus on one small detail — the curve of an eye, the edge of a nose, the texture of a cheek — and then shift perspective until those details resolve into a larger structure. Layer after layer gives the network a computational route from small, local visual patterns toward increasingly broad and abstract representations.

The Transformer offers a different, but equally familiar, intuition. Picture reading a book. You don't understand each word in isolation. A sentence makes sense because of the sentences around it. A paragraph lands because of an argument the author set up several pages earlier. A line in the conclusion carries weight because you remember what was established back in the opening chapter. Human understanding is constantly moving back and forth between a given piece of information and what it means in relation to everything else around it — and attention is what gives the Transformer a computational way of building representations out of exactly that kind of relationship.

The comparison shouldn't be pushed too far in either direction. Transformers don't read the way people do, any more than CNNs see the way a human visual cortex does. But as an intuition, both comparisons earn their keep. A CNN can be thought of as progressively building the bigger visual picture out of patterns. A Transformer can be thought of as progressively building meaning out of relationships and context.

A language model adds one final step on top of all this. Once it has built up rich, contextual representations of everything in front of it, it converts that understanding back into language one token at a time, with each new token folding immediately into the context available for producing the next. That gives us the full chain, start to finish: a prompt gets broken into tokens; attention builds layers of increasingly contextual representation out of those tokens; the model calculates a probability distribution over what should come next; one token gets chosen and generated; that token becomes part of a new, slightly richer context; and the whole process repeats, over and over, until the answer is complete.

What comes out of that repeated cycle can be a single sentence, a tightly reasoned argument, a working piece of software, or an entire essay. It reads as planned and coherent not because the model secretly wrote the whole thing out somewhere internally before it ever showed you the first word, but because each individual token is generated from an extraordinarily rich combination of everything the model learned during training, everything currently sitting in its context, the relationships running through that context, and everything it has already written so far in this very answer. That's the real intuition worth carrying away from all of this: rich context makes each individual next-token decision far more intelligent than the phrase "predict the next word" would ever lead you to expect.
