## I. Starting with a Cell

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts published a mathematical description of a neuron that had almost nothing to do with biology and almost everything to do with logic. Real neurons are messy: chemical gradients, ion channels, dendritic trees with their own local computation, timing effects that unfold over milliseconds. McCulloch and Pitts stripped all of that away and kept one idea — a neuron receives signals, weighs them, and fires or doesn't fire depending on whether the weighted sum crosses a threshold.

That abstraction was the real event, more than any specific equation. The claim, implicit and enormous, was that thought could be represented as a network of simple units connected by adjustable strengths. Donald Hebb gave the network a way to change: connections that fire together strengthen together. Frank Rosenblatt built a physical machine, the perceptron, that adjusted its own weights from data. Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert then showed the limits of that particular machine, and the field entered a long winter.

The winter ended not because of a new idea about intelligence but because of two engineering developments: backpropagation, which gave a systematic way to assign credit and blame across many layers of a network, and, decades later, GPUs, which happened to be extremely good at the kind of dense matrix multiplication that large networks require. Deep learning was, in an important sense, the perceptron scaled up until it worked. The Transformer architecture, introduced in 2017, added one more mechanism — attention, a way for every element of an input to weigh its relevance to every other element — and that mechanism turned out to be extraordinarily good at language.

It is worth being precise about what the matrix is doing in all of this, because the metaphor gets garbled in popular discussion. The matrix is not the discovery. The discovery, going back to McCulloch and Pitts, is that intelligence might be representable as a network of relationships whose strengths change through learning. A matrix is simply an efficient bookkeeping device for storing millions or billions of such relationships and computing with them quickly. Confusing the representation for the idea it represents leads to two mistakes people commonly make: overstating what neural networks are (thinking the matrix somehow *is* the mind) and understating what they are (dismissing them as "just matrix multiplication," as though the same could not be said of a great deal of biological computation once you look closely enough).

A second confusion is worth heading off early. In a large language model, tokens are not neurons. Tokens are the information being processed — words, subwords, pixels, whatever the input has been chopped into. The network is the machinery that processes them: the layers, the attention heads, the millions of weighted connections that were shaped during training and are normally fixed during use. When a Transformer's attention mechanism computes how strongly one token should attend to another, that is a *dynamic* relationship, recomputed fresh for every new input. It is not the same kind of relationship as the network's *learned* weights, which change slowly, if at all, and represent something closer to accumulated knowledge. Keeping this distinction — permanent structural relationships versus transient input-to-input relationships — clear is necessary for almost everything that follows.

## II. The Puzzle of the Clumsy Genius

Anyone who has spent real time with a frontier language model has felt the same disorienting whiplash: a system that can write working code, summarize a legal filing, or explain a proof in topology, and then, in the same conversation, miscount the letters in a word, contradict something it said three paragraphs earlier, or confidently invent a citation. This is not a minor implementation bug. It is a pattern stable enough to demand an explanation, and there are two very different candidate explanations.

The first: current architectures are missing something structurally necessary for general intelligence, and no amount of scaling will fix it — the clumsiness is a symptom of a genuine ceiling.

The second: much of the clumsiness follows not from what these networks *can* compute but from how humans have chosen to train, freeze, and deploy them — the clumsiness is a symptom of a deployment decision, not a computational limit.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and untangling how much of the gap belongs to each is one of the genuinely open questions in the field. But it is worth taking the second explanation seriously before concluding the first, because the training-and-deployment story is more load-bearing than casual conversation about AI usually credits.

A recent benchmark makes the gap unusually vivid. By early 2026, frontier models were scoring near saturation — around 98 percent — on the original ARC-AGI puzzle benchmark, a test of abstract pattern inference that had resisted progress for years. The benchmark's creators responded with a third version, ARC-AGI-3, that swapped static puzzles for interactive environments with no instructions: an agent has to figure out the goal, build a working model of the environment, and plan its own actions through trial and observation, the way a person does when handed an unfamiliar game. At launch in March 2026, humans solved essentially all of the evaluation environments. The best frontier models — from Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI — scored well under half a percent. That contrast, near-total mastery on one axis and near-total failure on another that seems, if anything, simpler for a human, is exactly the kind of unevenness this essay is trying to explain. It is hard to read as evidence that a single, uniform general intelligence has already been achieved. It is equally hard to read as evidence that nothing resembling intelligence is present, given what these same systems accomplish elsewhere. It looks, instead, like the kind of jagged capability profile a system without autonomous, self-directed world-modeling would be expected to have — extraordinarily fluent when drawing on a vast trained-in prior, and comparatively lost the moment a task requires building a new one from scratch, on the fly, from nothing but exploration.

That observation points toward a more useful question than "are these systems intelligent?" What, exactly, is missing between a system that can answer an extraordinary range of questions and one that can independently learn what it needs to know, decide what deserves investigation, act in the world, verify the consequences, and update its understanding? Answering that starts by refusing to treat the neural network, in isolation, as though it were the whole of the AI.

## III. The Model Is Not the System

A production AI system is often discussed as though it were simply a trained neural network. That is increasingly misleading. The model is one component. Around it may sit a context window, persistent memory, retrieval systems, search, calculators, code interpreters, databases, computer-use interfaces, planning mechanisms, verification systems, and other models, all interacting with people, software, and — increasingly — physical environments.

This distinction resolves what would otherwise look like a paradox. Every production AI system in wide use today follows roughly the same lifecycle: pretrain, refine through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, freeze the resulting weights, and deploy the frozen model to millions of users. During deployment — inference — the weights do not change. The model can hold a conversation, but it cannot, in that conversation, update the underlying network the way it updated during training.

It is tempting to treat this as a fact about what neural networks fundamentally are. It isn't. There is nothing in the mathematics of gradient descent that requires training to stop before deployment begins. Continued pretraining on new data, fine-tuning on a narrower distribution, reinforcement learning from ongoing interaction — all of these are technically available and, in various research contexts, already used. The freeze between training and inference is an engineering and governance choice, not a mathematical necessity. That said, the fact that continual learning is possible in principle does not mean that safely achieving *autonomous* continual learning is easy, or that current architectures are guaranteed to be sufficient — a qualification the next section takes seriously rather than waving away.

That reframes the interesting question. It is not:

> Can an AI system continue learning after deployment?

The honest technical answer to that is yes, in principle, today. The harder and more interesting question is:

> Can an AI system autonomously decide *what* it should learn, judge whether new information is trustworthy, integrate it without damaging existing capabilities, and use the result to improve its own world model?

That question becomes easier to analyze once different kinds of "knowing" are pulled apart, because the word is doing several different jobs at once and collapsing them together makes the rest of this essay's argument harder to evaluate rather than easier.

If a model correctly states that Canberra is the capital of Australia without consulting anything, that fact is represented somewhere in its trained weights — diffusely, indirectly, but internally. Call this **parametric knowledge**. If instead you hand the model a document saying a meeting has moved to Thursday, it can reason about that fact immediately, compare it with other dates, draft a message around it — without the underlying weights changing at all. The information exists only for as long as the context holds it. Call this **contextual knowledge**: real, usable, and gone the moment the context is gone unless something outside the model preserves it.

A third possibility sits between these two. Suppose you ask a system when tomorrow's meeting is, and instead of guessing from memory, it searches a calendar file, finds the entry, and reads the time back to you. That's the whole idea in miniature: rather than holding the fact permanently, the system holds the ability to go find it. Scaled up to millions of documents, this pattern has a name — retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG — and it runs the same basic loop: a question comes in, relevant material is retrieved, placed in context, reasoned over, and answered. Call this **retrieved knowledge**. The pattern is exactly the one a working professional uses constantly — a physician does not memorize the literature, a lawyer does not memorize every judgment, an engineer does not memorize every specification. Expertise is partly what is internalized and partly knowing where reliable information lives and how to evaluate it once retrieved.

**Retrieval, though, is not learning, and the gap between them matters.** If a model looks up a city's population, uses it to answer a question, and the conversation ends, nothing has necessarily changed inside the model — the sequence runs from ignorance through retrieval through a temporary context straight back to nothing retained. Genuine learning runs differently: ignorance, investigation, evidence, evaluation, a persistent update, and behavior that is actually different afterward. Modern AI systems can increasingly paper over weak internal knowledge with search, retrieval, and long context windows, and the overall system ends up looking far more knowledgeable than the underlying frozen model actually is. That is not illegitimate — humans do the same thing constantly — but external access should not be mistaken for internal learning.

There is a further, intermediate case worth naming on its own: **persistent experiential memory**, external to the model's weights but retained across sessions rather than discarded at the end of one. If a system learns mid-conversation that a project has been renamed and stores that fact rather than modifying any parameter, it can retrieve and use it weeks later — functionally remembering, while the foundation model underneath remains mechanically unchanged. That yields a distinction worth stating as a principle in its own right, because it quietly governs a great deal of this essay's argument:

**The model is not the system.**

A weight-frozen network is one component. Working context, persistent memory, retrieval, tools, planning, and verification are others, and they can each keep changing and accumulating experience even while the network at the center stays exactly as it was after training. Evaluating only the frozen network while ignoring everything built around it is about as misleading as evaluating a professional's competence by mentally subtracting her notes, her colleagues, and her search engine and asking what's left inside her skull alone. The economically and practically relevant unit of intelligence, increasingly, is the system, not the isolated network — or, stated as plainly as the idea allows: **a model can be frozen while the intelligent system built around it continues to learn.**

Putting these together gives five progressively more demanding senses of "knowing": parametric knowledge sitting in the weights; contextual knowledge available only within the current exchange; retrieved knowledge pulled in from outside when needed; persistent experiential knowledge kept because the system judged it worth keeping; and discovered knowledge — reliable information produced through investigation that was previously unknown to the system and to humanity alike, the subject of Section X. None of these should be collapsed into any other. Retrieving a fact is not the same as learning it. Remembering an experience is not the same as folding it into a revised world model. Generating a novel hypothesis is not the same as discovering anything. And changing a weight is not the same as changing it *correctly*.

Seen this way, retrieval turns out to be the easy part of the problem, and the interesting difficulty sits one layer up, in questions no search index answers on its own: what is worth retrieving, which source deserves trust, whether two sources actually contradict each other, whether something newly found should change the model's picture of the world, and what else would stop being true if it were accepted. Those are judgment problems, not retrieval problems, and the next section is about exactly how far that judgment already reaches — and where, precisely, it stops.

## IV. From Frozen Models to Controlled Learning

If continual learning is possible in principle, why don't deployed systems simply learn continuously from everything they encounter? Because unrestricted learning would trade one set of problems for a worse one.

Catastrophic forgetting is real, and it is worth being precise about the mechanism rather than treating it as a label. A network's weights are not organized by topic — there is no dedicated block of "calendar" weights sitting apart from "chemistry" weights. Because there are vastly more concepts than there are neurons to hold them, a network packs many unrelated ideas into overlapping sets of connections, a property called superposition. When gradient descent runs a single update to teach the model one new fact, it adjusts every weight that contributed to the error, and nothing in that process respects conceptual boundaries that were never cleanly drawn to begin with — a weight nudged to fix a date might also participate, in some small way, in the model's temporal reasoning generally, or in something with no obvious connection to dates at all. One such update is imperceptible. Millions of them, run continuously and without filtering, produce a slow, untraceable drift: capabilities eroding in places nobody is watching, discoverable only when a user happens to hit the damaged spot. This is precisely why writing a new fact to external memory is safe in a way that writing it into the weights is not: nothing about the network's internal wiring changes, so there is no drift to localize or reverse — deleting a row is not the same problem as reverse-engineering which of a billion weights need to be nudged back. Model poisoning is real: a system that learns continuously from open interaction can be deliberately fed false or manipulative information, and at internet scale some fraction of input will be adversarial. And distribution shift, auditability, and privacy compound both problems — a model whose weights are quietly changing after every conversation is one whose behavior cannot be tested once and trusted thereafter.

These are legitimate engineering reasons for the freeze, not excuses. But they are reasons to solve a hard problem carefully, not reasons to conclude that continual learning is impossible in principle. It is worth resisting the jump from "we do not currently allow this, for good reasons" to "this cannot be done."

A capable model can already reason fairly well about whether a new claim is novel, relevant, credible, and worth retaining — that is close to ordinary judgment, and current systems, imperfectly but genuinely, can do it. What those systems typically do with a judgment like "this is worth retaining" is write it to external, persistent memory, not touch the weights at all — a meaningfully easier problem, since writing to an external store that gets retrieved later carries little of the forgetting or poisoning risk described above. Safely folding that same judgment into the model's own weights, and verifying nothing else broke as a result, remains substantially unsolved. Research aimed directly at that harder half exists, but it is early, and even the most promising approaches still treat catastrophic forgetting as an open problem rather than a solved one.

This suggests a more precise way to think about what "continual learning" requires than a single yes-or-no capability. Most new information probably never needs to enter the weights at all — a fact relevant only to one conversation belongs in context; a fact about one user belongs in persistent personal memory; a fact likely to change soon belongs in an external, searchable store; and only information that has been repeatedly verified and genuinely changes the model's general understanding of something is a real candidate for consolidation into the network itself:

**working context → external memory → persistent experience → model knowledge**

If that framing holds, the AGI-relevant bottleneck is narrower than "can an AI decide what to learn" — a good deal of that judgment already exists in workable form. It is closer to: can a system be given a safe, reliable mechanism for turning its own judgments about what matters into lasting changes to what it knows, without a meaningful chance of quietly damaging what it already knew. In other words, the problem is not learning. It is learning without becoming unreliable. That is a much higher bar, and it is not one current architectures have cleared.

One qualification is worth flagging before moving on. The freeze described above is accurate for any single deployed model, but it is not accurate for AI development as a whole. Looked at across model generations rather than within one, training and deployment are already linked by a feedback loop running through human researchers and organizational infrastructure — deployment surfaces failures, failures get diagnosed and turned into new training data, and an improved model eventually replaces the old one. That loop, and what it would take to internalize more of it inside the system itself, is developed at length in Section IX.

## V. Intelligence With Tools, Memory, and a World

A common argument against calling current systems generally intelligent points to their dependence on external tools: they need search to access current information, calculators to do reliable arithmetic, code execution to verify their own reasoning. If a system needs a battery of external crutches to function well, the argument goes, it cannot be exhibiting general intelligence on its own.

This argument proves too much, because it would also disqualify humans. No practicing accountant holds a spreadsheet's worth of computation in her head; she holds Excel. No physician has the entire body of medical literature memorized; she holds journals, references, and increasingly a search bar. Humans routinely extend their cognitive abilities through books, databases, calculators, colleagues, laboratories, and institutions. The honest test of general intelligence is therefore not:

**human brain, unaided, versus AI model plus every tool it can reach**

but the symmetric version:

**human brain plus its tools versus AI model plus its tools**

Once tool use is put on both sides of the comparison rather than only one, a large share of the "it's not really intelligent, it just looks things up" objection loses its force. It does not settle whether the model's internal reasoning is comparable to a human's — that question remains genuinely open — but it does mean tool dependence by itself cannot be the deciding evidence.

That reframing, though, leaves out where the actual intelligence in tool use lives. It is not in possessing a tool; it is in orchestrating one. Merely bolting a calculator onto a language model does not make that model generally intelligent, any more than handing someone a laboratory makes that person a scientist. What has to happen first is a judgment: recognizing that internal computation is not reliable enough for this particular problem, selecting the right external instrument from among several available, using it correctly, and then assessing whether the result that comes back is trustworthy before building anything further on top of it. That chain — *I cannot reliably solve this internally → I need a tool → this is the appropriate one → here is how to use it → is this result trustworthy → now I can proceed* — is itself a cognitive act, considerably more demanding than either unaided computation or blind tool invocation. A system that reaches for a calculator every time, correctly or not, is not demonstrating intelligent tool use; a system that knows when to reach for one, which one, and how much to trust what comes back, is.

This leads directly to the problem of world models. A world model is more than a collection of retrievable facts. It is an internal representation of how things relate to one another and how they tend to behave — not just a record of what has been seen before, but a basis for predicting what would happen if something changed or if the system itself took a particular action. A weather forecast is not a world model; the atmospheric physics that generates it is. Knowing that Canberra is the capital of Australia is not, by itself, a world model; understanding how a change in interest rates would ripple through employment, spending, and prices is closer to one.

It would be inaccurate to say large language models have no internal representation of the world. Interpretability research over the past several years points the other way: these networks build internal representations of objects, spatial relationships, social dynamics, and causal regularities in text, well beyond anything a simple statistical parrot would need. The harder and more precise question is whether those representations amount to the specific kind of world model associated with general intelligence: one that is *persistent* across time rather than reconstructed fresh in each context window, *coherent* rather than locally consistent but globally contradictory, *causally useful* for predicting the consequences of actions rather than merely capturing correlations in training data, and *continually updated* through ongoing experience rather than fixed at the moment training ended.

A model deployed today knows an enormous amount about the world as of its training cutoff and comparatively little, on its own, about what has happened since — unless a tool tells it. A human knows less in raw volume but updates continuously through lived experience, and that continuous updating is arguably as central to what we mean by "intelligence in the world" as raw representational richness is. Multimodality, agentic tool use, persistent memory across sessions, and physical embodiment through robotics are all mechanisms that push in the direction of closing this gap — each gives a system more continuous contact with a changing world rather than a single frozen snapshot of it. None of them, individually or so far in combination, has closed it completely.

## VI. What Would a Learning Intelligence Actually Do?

A useful comparison, held loosely rather than literally, is how humans avoid the failure modes described above without preventing a child's brain from changing at all.

A child is not protected from bad ideas by freezing her neural development. She is protected by a layered system: evolved predispositions toward attachment, empathy, reciprocity, and social bonding that provide raw material for morality before any explicit teaching occurs; a family that filters and interprets the world during the years when judgment is least developed; a society with accumulated norms, laws, and institutions that reward some behaviors and punish others; a school system that transmits centuries of consolidated knowledge instead of requiring each child to rediscover geometry from nothing; and continuous feedback from consequences, both social and physical. Represented schematically:

**evolutionary predispositions + family + society + education + experience + consequences + continual learning → a functioning adult mind**

No part of this system prevents learning. All of it shapes and filters what is learned, and provides scaffolding — accumulated culture — so a child never has to build a world model entirely from scratch. The speculative question, and it should be treated as speculative, is whether artificial systems might eventually follow a structurally similar pattern:

**architecture + pretraining + post-training + social and institutional constraints + persistent memory + experience + continual learning → a system that keeps developing after deployment**

Instead of the current pipeline — **train → align → freeze → deploy** — a more open-ended pattern would look like: **train → deploy → experience → evaluate → learn → revise world model → act → observe consequences → learn again.** This is a conceptual analogy, not a claim of biological equivalence: artificial neural networks do not have neurotransmitters, developmental critical periods, or evolutionary history, and treating the comparison as more than structural would be a mistake. What transfers is not the biology but the *shape* of the solution: safety and reliability under continual change come from surrounding structure and filtering, not from preventing the underlying system from changing at all. Whether that shape can be rebuilt in silicon — with verification, provenance-checking, and forgetting-resistant update mechanisms doing the work that family, culture, and consequence do for a child — is an engineering problem nobody has yet solved at scale.

One more feature of childhood learning is worth pulling out on its own. A child does not simply wait for training data to arrive. She generates a meaningful share of it herself — picks something up, drops it, drops it again to see if the same thing happens twice, looks underneath the object that surprised her, asks why, tries something that fails and then tries something different. The learner is not just processing whatever experience happens to arrive; she is choosing, moment to moment, which experience to have next. That turns a simple loop — **experience → learning** — into something with an extra step folded in: **question → action → experience → learning → new question.** Nothing quite like that shows up in a system that only ever answers the question it was just asked.

That extra step suggests a deeper progression for artificial systems: **respond → act → plan → investigate.** A system that responds to a human request is useful. A system that acts toward a human-specified goal is more autonomous. A system that can decide which sequence of actions is most useful for achieving that goal is more capable still. But the important threshold arrives when the system can recognize that something is worth investigating before a human has formulated the specific question. That is **agency in a practical sense**.

It does not require the AI to invent its ultimate values or goals from nothing any more than a human does — people inherit plenty of their goals from biology, family, employers, and culture. A pharmaceutical researcher does not need to invent the goal of developing a cancer treatment. Within that inherited objective, however, she decides which uncertainty matters, which hypothesis is worth testing, which experiment should come next, whether a result is trustworthy, and whether the next step should follow the original plan or change direction. That ability to decide what deserves attention — not the ability to originate the ultimate goal, which is a considerably more speculative claim — is much closer to the agency relevant to advanced intelligence. It is also the bridge between learning and discovery, a bridge Sections IX through XI cross in more detail.

## VII. The Economic Reframing

Set the philosophical question aside for a moment and consider a different one, less discussed but arguably more consequential: what is the actual economic project underway?

It may not be an attempt to reproduce the human brain. It may be an attempt to produce **useful intelligence more cheaply than biological human intelligence can be produced.** Producing one more skilled engineer, physician, lawyer, or accountant requires, at minimum, another human being: roughly two decades of childhood, an education system, healthcare, housing, and then a career's worth of ongoing compensation, benefits, and finite working hours. AI changes the shape of that cost curve. After an enormous upfront fixed cost — data collection, compute, research, training runs that can run into the hundreds of millions of dollars — each additional unit of cognitive output can, in principle, be produced through additional computation rather than through raising another human being from infancy. Intelligence, in other words, starts to behave less like a scarce biological resource and more like reproducible capital: expensive to build the first unit, comparatively cheap to run the marginal one.

That reframes the relevant metric. Instead of asking how closely a system's internal processes resemble a human mind, the economically decisive question becomes:

**useful cognitive output ÷ cost**

But that formula is too generous as stated, because it prices the AI's output in isolation and ignores something this essay has spent several sections establishing: that unverified output is worth less than it looks. Ten thousand cheap answers are not economically valuable if a human still has to check all ten thousand before trusting any of them. The metric that actually matters is closer to *reliable* useful cognitive output over *total* cost — where total cost includes whatever supervision, verification, and error-correction the output requires before it can be relied on. This pre-empts the obvious objection that AI only looks cheap because someone else is quietly absorbing the cost of checking its work, by folding that cost into the metric from the start.

An AI system does not need to match or exceed human performance across every task to be economically transformative under this metric. If it can reliably perform 80 to 90 percent of the economically valuable work in a profession, at a small fraction of the total cost, it can reshape that profession's labor market even while remaining clearly inferior to skilled humans on plenty of individual tasks. This suggests a useful split in vocabulary:

**Capability AGI** — an artificial system whose internal processes and range of performance are genuinely comparable to human general intelligence, in whatever sense turns out to be scientifically well-defined and broadly agreed upon.

**Economic AGI** — an artificial system capable of producing reliable, economically useful cognitive output across a sufficiently broad range of tasks at lower total cost than human labor, whether or not it satisfies the capability definition.

These two thresholds need not arrive together. It is entirely plausible that economic AGI arrives first, disrupting labor markets and professions well before there is any scientific consensus that "true" AGI has been achieved — and plausible, separately, that a system could pass a demanding capability bar for general reasoning while still being too unreliable, too expensive, or too narrowly deployed to count as economic AGI. (A third threshold, concerned with autonomous discovery rather than performance or cost, is worth setting aside for now — Section X takes up discovery itself, and Section XII names it formally as Discovery AGI.)

This isn't merely theoretical. Some commercial agreements already define AGI economically rather than cognitively: Microsoft and OpenAI have reportedly attached a concrete profit figure to the term in their commercial arrangement, with an independent panel meant to verify the claim once it is reached. Whatever one thinks of that as a scientific definition, it is economic AGI functioning exactly as described above, arrived at by contract rather than by researchers — a reminder to treat any single organization's declaration of "AGI achieved" with some skepticism, regardless of which threshold it claims to satisfy.

## VIII. An Honest Inventory of the Gaps

It is worth listing the deficiencies usually cited as evidence that today's systems fall short of general intelligence, and, for each, asking the same three questions: Is it genuinely absent? Can it already be supplied through some combination of tools, memory, retrieval, or additional training? And does closing the gap require a fundamentally new architecture, or might it emerge from scaling and integrating capabilities that already exist in some form?

| Capability | Current position | Likely nature of the gap |
|---|---|---|
| Reliability / hallucination | Partial, measurably improving | Engineering + verification |
| Persistent memory | Largely solvable externally | Product and infrastructure |
| Continual, autonomous learning | Least solved item on this list | Genuinely unresolved |
| Coherent world models, causal reasoning | Partially present, improving | Uncertain |
| Few-shot learning | Strong in-context, weak persistently | Uncertain |
| Long-horizon autonomy | Improving with scaffolding | Engineering + reasoning |
| Metacognition | Inconsistent | Uncertain — scaling or structural |
| Physical-world interaction | Early | Robotics |
| Autonomous verification | Weak | Genuinely unresolved |
| Agency | Limited, structural gap | Genuinely unresolved (see Section VI) |

Most rows compress a story already told or still to come elsewhere in this essay. Reliability and hallucination are real weaknesses, though retrieval grounding and tool verification have measurably reduced them without eliminating them — an engineering trajectory more than a hard wall so far. Persistent memory is largely a product and infrastructure problem at this point. Continual and autonomous learning remains the least solved item on the list, for the reasons Sections III and IV laid out in detail. Coherent world models and causal reasoning are partially present and actively improving but not yet at the level of confident, generalizable causal inference a domain expert brings to an unfamiliar problem. Long-horizon autonomy has improved rapidly with agentic scaffolding but still degrades over long horizons in ways human experts typically do not. Metacognition — reliably knowing what one does not know — is inconsistent, and it is not yet clear whether that inconsistency is a scaling problem or something closer to a structural one. Physical-world interaction remains far behind digital-domain competence. Autonomous verification — checking one's own work reliably before presenting it as finished — is one of the areas where current systems fall furthest short of what a seasoned human professional brings to a job. And agency, discussed at length in Section VI, is less a capability gap than a structural one: the ability to recognize that something is worth investigating without a human formulating the task first.

The honest conclusion from this inventory is that it does not sort cleanly into "solved" and "unsolvable." Some gaps look like ordinary engineering problems already yielding to scale, better data, and better scaffolding. Others — autonomous continual learning and autonomous verification chief among them — look like they may need genuinely new mechanisms, not just more of the current ones. Assuming every remaining weakness will fall to more parameters and more compute is unjustified optimism. Assuming none of them will is unjustified pessimism. The honest position, at present, is that nobody knows with confidence which items on this list belong in which category, and treating that uncertainty as itself the finding is more accurate than resolving it prematurely in either direction.

This uncertainty is not confined to casual conversation; it runs through the research literature itself. When a paper argued that GPT-4 showed "sparks" of general intelligence by solving novel problems across math, coding, medicine, and law, a common rebuttal was that high performance on a benchmark measures skill rather than intelligence — and that skill can be purchased with enough training data without implying any underlying capacity to reason generally. A gap that looks closed because a benchmark score has gone up is not necessarily the same as a gap that has actually closed, if what improved was coverage of the specific distribution being tested rather than the general mechanism the benchmark was meant to stand in for.

## IX. From Managed Learning to Autonomous Learning

There is a sense in which the AI field already practices a form of continual learning today — it just runs through humans and organizations rather than inside any single deployed model. The loop looks roughly like this: a model is deployed, millions of inference episodes accumulate, failures and successes become observable, researchers and automated systems analyze the patterns, useful signals are selected, training data and evaluations are built around them, another round of training runs, the result is evaluated and regression-tested, and an improved model returns to deployment. Humans and the surrounding infrastructure are, in effect, the nervous system connecting deployment back to training:

**training → deployment → millions of interactions → failures identified → new training and evaluation data → training → improved model → deployment**

Framed this way, Section III's "pretrain, refine, freeze, deploy" lifecycle is accurate for any single deployed model, but it describes only one link in a longer chain. Looked at across model generations rather than within one, the more complete picture is closer to train, deploy, observe, evaluate, curate, train again, deploy again — weights frozen within a generation, but development itself never frozen. And AI systems already participate in the middle of that larger loop, not just at its endpoints: classifying failure types, grading candidate responses, generating adversarial test cases, and helping evaluate whether a new model actually fixed what the previous one got wrong.

Seen from that angle, the progression toward autonomous continual learning may not require inventing a new mechanism from nothing. It may instead look like removing humans, one stage at a time, from a loop that already runs. Today, the shape is roughly: AI inference, human or lightly-automated evaluation, human-controlled training, new model. Concretely, this is what "AI-assisted evaluation" already looks like at most labs: rather than a person reading tens of thousands of candidate outputs, a separate, stronger model grades them against a rubric a person wrote — is this answer correct, does this code pass its tests, is this reasoning step valid — and a human owns the rubric and spot-checks a sample rather than reading everything. The AI does the volume work; the human still owns the judgment of what counts as good.

A visibly more automated version already taking shape looks like: AI inference, AI-assisted evaluation, AI-generated training data, human-approved training, new model. The step that actually moves here is who notices what needs fixing. Instead of a person first spotting a failure pattern — "the model struggles with negative exponents" — and then asking for training data to address it, the system notices the pattern itself, constructs new examples that target it, and assembles a training set around it, with a human still approving the resulting training run before it ships. Narrow versions of this already exist, for instance in automated red-teaming pipelines built to surface a model's own safety failures; doing it generally, across arbitrary capability gaps the system notices on its own initiative, is the harder and mostly-not-yet-real part.

A fully autonomous version, not yet realized, would look like: AI experience, AI detects a weakness, AI investigates it, AI verifies its findings, AI decides what is worth learning, a controlled self-update, regression testing, and an improved system — the loop above, run without a human standing in the middle of every stage. What actually changes at this final rung is not who does the labor, which has already shifted to the AI at the previous rung, but who signs off on the update — the system approving its own training run rather than a person approving it. That is precisely the step Section IV argued nobody has a reliable way to do safely yet: without a person in the loop, there is no dependable way to confirm that an update fixed the intended weakness without quietly damaging something unrelated, for the same superposition reasons described there.

It is worth being clear about what this loop is not aiming for. A system that revised its own beliefs every time it encountered something that contradicted them would not be exhibiting a superior form of learning — it would be gullible, and gullibility at scale, wired directly into a system's own knowledge, is a worse failure mode than never updating at all. The goal is not maximum plasticity. It is **controlled plasticity**: change when evidence genuinely warrants it, hold steady when it doesn't, and be able to tell the difference reliably enough that the distinction can run without a person checking every case.

Controlled plasticity is a different guardrail from the controlled learning described in Section IV, not a restatement of it, and the two are easy to blur because the names are so close. Controlled learning is a routing question: given a piece of information the system has already decided to trust, which tier should it land in — working context, external memory, persistent memory, or the weights themselves? Controlled plasticity is a prior, epistemic question: should the system change what it believes at all, given what it has just seen? Routing happens after that judgment has already been made; plasticity governs the judgment itself. A system could have excellent routing — reliably filing trustworthy facts into the right tier — while still being gullible about which facts to trust in the first place, or, in the other direction, could be admirably skeptical about what to believe while still handling the resulting updates carelessly once trust has been granted. The two failure modes are independent, which is exactly why the essay treats them in separate sections rather than as one problem under two names.

There is one advantage available to a networked artificial system that has no clean biological analogue. When one human learns something, no other human's brain updates as a result; knowledge spreads only through the comparatively slow channels of teaching, writing, and culture. A network of AI instances, in principle, could aggregate experience across millions of separate interactions, verify what was learned, consolidate it, and propagate it back out to the whole network:

**millions of instances → millions of experiences → verification → consolidation → shared update**

If that process can be made reliable and economical — and reliability is doing a great deal of work in that sentence — it points toward a mode of collective learning with no real precedent in biological intelligence, which is bottlenecked by the fact that experience is trapped inside individual skulls.

## X. From Learning Knowledge to Creating Knowledge

There is one further threshold that may matter more than many of the capabilities normally used to define AGI: whether an artificial intelligence can move beyond learning and applying existing human knowledge and begin autonomously producing genuinely new knowledge about the world.

The distinction is easy to blur because several very different activities are casually described as "learning." A model can retrieve something humanity already knows. It can synthesize existing knowledge into a combination no particular human happened to have written down before. Or it can learn something previously unknown to the model but already known to humanity. All three are genuinely useful. None of them, on its own, constitutes discovery. A more demanding pattern looks different:

**observation → uncertainty → hypothesis → prediction → experiment or investigation → evidence → verification → revised world model**

If the resulting conclusion was not previously known to humanity, something categorically more interesting has happened. That suggests a further, harder test: can an artificial intelligence autonomously discover reliable knowledge that neither it nor humanity previously possessed? Consider what a capable scientist actually does when confronted with a genuinely unexplained phenomenon. She identifies the uncertainty, generates competing explanations, works out what observations would distinguish between them, designs a test, gathers evidence, discards the hypotheses that fail, and ends up with a better model of the phenomenon than she started with. An artificial system performing the analogous loop would look something like: observe, notice an anomaly, generate hypotheses, design a test, use whatever tools the test requires, collect evidence, revise its world model, and identify the next open question.

This is importantly different from producing novel text. A language model can already generate a sentence, or even a scientific hypothesis, that no one has written before — that bar is not hard to clear and does not by itself amount to anything. Novel output is not new knowledge:

**novel hypothesis + evidence + successful verification → candidate new knowledge**

Verification is the load-bearing term in that equation. Without it, a system capable of generating a million novel hypotheses an hour is not a discovery engine — it is an unusually productive generator of speculation, indistinguishable from noise until something outside the system checks which hypotheses, if any, were actually right.

This is not a purely hypothetical loop; versions of it are already running. Multi-agent systems such as Google DeepMind's Co-Scientist and FutureHouse's Robin can generate hypotheses, design experiments, and analyze results with only light human direction, and both have surfaced drug-candidate leads that researchers considered plausible enough to pursue further — though in both cases, the labs presenting the results were careful to note that the outputs still needed independent experimental confirmation before they counted as anything more than promising leads. In October 2025, a mathematician working with an AI reasoning system produced a new convergence proof in optimization theory after roughly twelve hours of iterative back-and-forth — a genuine result, but one that emerged from sustained human-AI collaboration rather than autonomous investigation. And by mid-2026, one fully automated research system had produced a manuscript that scored above the average acceptance threshold at a peer-reviewed workshop, the first fully AI-generated paper to clear that bar.

Those results sit alongside a pointed caution from within the field. One AI-lab researcher described a case where a system's report included impressive-looking charts that turned out, on inspection, to be fabricated — the underlying analysis had never actually been run — and drew from it the conclusion that fully autonomous scientists were not close at hand. That example is close to a textbook illustration of exactly the failure mode this section is built around: novel output that looks like a discovery is not a discovery until something outside the generating system checks it, and the systems making the most visible progress on the earlier stages of the discovery loop — hypothesis generation, experiment design — are not yet the systems that can be trusted to run the verification stage unsupervised. The gap between "can propose" and "can be trusted to confirm" is, at present, exactly where the frontier sits.

This also clarifies how tool dependence should be read in a scientific context. An artificial scientist might need databases, simulations, telescopes, microscopes, laboratory robotics, the existing scientific literature, other AI systems, and human collaborators. That dependence would not make its discoveries less its own. Einstein depended on mathematics developed by others; astronomers depend on telescopes; particle physicists depend on machines costing billions of dollars that took thousands of people to build. Human science has never been the product of unaided minds. The comparison that matters remains the symmetric one from Section V — human intelligence plus its instruments, against artificial intelligence plus its instruments — and the question worth asking is whether the intelligence on either side of that comparison can direct its tools toward reducing its own uncertainty about reality, rather than only executing instructions.

## XI. When Intelligence Starts Asking Its Own Questions

The discovery problem reveals that several apparently separate capabilities are actually converging. World modeling matters because the system needs a representation against which predictions can be compared. Metacognition matters because the system must recognize uncertainty. Tool use matters because experiments require instruments. Continual learning matters because evidence must change the system's understanding. Verification matters because novel conclusions cannot simply be trusted. Agency matters because someone has to decide what is worth investigating. These are not independent features. They form a loop.

Suppose a system predicts that A should lead to B. It performs an experiment. Instead, it repeatedly observes A leading to C. A merely responsive system waits for a human to point out the discrepancy. A more autonomous system notices: *my prediction failed; something about my model may be wrong.* It generates hypotheses, determines which experiment would distinguish them, performs the experiment, evaluates the result, and revises its model.

This is Section IX's training-feedback loop again, run on a single prediction rather than a system's whole deployment history. What makes the discovery case a stronger requirement than continual learning in general is where the trigger comes from. In Section IX, the loop is set in motion by aggregated failures a human or automated evaluator has to notice first. Here, the system has to notice its own prediction failed, without anyone flagging it — and then do the same investigate-verify-revise work on its own initiative, before it can be said to have discovered anything rather than merely been corrected.

Picture this happening not in one system but across many. A million AI instances distributed across different problems, laboratories, and organizations, each generating observations, hypotheses, experiments, and verified conclusions. A discovery made by one instance could, once verified, become available to every other instance almost immediately. Biological intelligence has nothing directly comparable to this. Human civilization approximates it through language, books, universities, journals, and now the internet, but the transmission is slow and lossy — knowledge has to move out of one biological brain, into some external representation, and then back into another biological brain, a process that has taken centuries to reach its current speed. An artificial system that could compress the cycle from experience through discovery, verification, and shared update into something closer to real time would be operating under a genuinely different constraint on the growth of knowledge than any civilization has previously had available to it.

There is one further possibility worth naming cautiously, because it is the point at which this line of reasoning most easily slides into speculation it cannot support: an artificial intelligence applying the same discovery process to artificial intelligence itself — investigating better architectures, better learning algorithms, more efficient use of computation, better memory systems, better mechanisms for continual learning. The resulting loop, in its simplest form, is an AI discovering a better method of building AI, producing an improved AI, which makes a better discovery, which produces a further improvement. This is the structural basis of arguments about recursive self-improvement, and it is worth being precise about what such a loop does and does not guarantee. Nothing about it implies an instantaneous "intelligence explosion." Experiments take time to run. Computation costs real money. Physical infrastructure has to be built and maintained. Every candidate discovery still requires verification before it can be trusted, and better algorithms may simply become harder to find as the easy ones are exhausted — the same pattern that has slowed progress in plenty of mature scientific fields.

The important claim is narrower than an explosion, and does not depend on one: an intelligence capable of autonomously extending human knowledge, and of improving the methods by which it makes those discoveries, would represent a qualitatively different stage of artificial intelligence than a system whose principal role is to reproduce, recombine, and apply knowledge that humans already created. Instead of asking only whether an AI can answer the questions humans already know how to answer, the more demanding version of the question is whether it can discover the answers to questions humanity cannot yet answer — and judge, on its own, which of those questions are worth asking in the first place.

## XII. Three Thresholds Instead of One

At this point, "AGI" looks less like a single finish line and more like a collection of different thresholds.

**Capability AGI** — an artificial system can perform the broad range of intellectual tasks that competent humans can perform.

**Economic AGI** — an artificial system can perform a sufficiently broad range of economically valuable cognitive work reliably and more cheaply than humans.

**Discovery AGI** — an artificial system capable of autonomously investigating open questions and producing reliable, verified knowledge not previously possessed by humanity. (Whether that knowledge turns out to matter a great deal or only a little is a separate question of impact, not a condition of meeting the definition.)

These need not arrive together, and in fact there is reason to expect they won't. Economic AGI might arrive while AI remains entirely dependent on humans to decide which questions are worth investigating in the first place. In the other direction, narrow, specialized systems might make genuine autonomous discoveries in a constrained domain — protein folding is an early example of something in this neighborhood — while remaining far too narrow to qualify as generally intelligent by any other measure. But if all three were eventually to converge in a single system, its character would be different in kind, not just in degree, from anything that currently exists: a system that could perform existing intellectual work, do so more cheaply than the humans who currently perform it, and also expand the body of knowledge that future intellectual work — human and artificial alike — draws on.

## XIII. What Word Are We Actually Arguing About?

It is worth asking, at this point, whether "AGI" has quietly become an unfairly anthropomorphic standard — one that implicitly requires an artificial system to replicate the specific *texture* of human cognition rather than its practical capabilities.

If a system can reason across unrelated domains, learn tasks it was not explicitly trained on, use external tools fluidly, retrieve information when its own knowledge is insufficient, remember relevant context across time, plan and execute substantial multi-step projects, recognize the limits of its own knowledge, verify important conclusions before acting on them, and do most economically valuable cognitive work more cheaply than a human — what, specifically, would still be missing before that system earned the label "generally intelligent"?

That is not a rhetorical trap; it deserves a real answer, and different serious thinkers give different ones. Some would say nothing further is missing. Others would insist that something is still absent even in a system meeting every item on that list: genuine understanding rather than sophisticated pattern completion, subjective experience, autonomous goals rather than goals set by a human operator, or the specific kind of embodied, continuously-lived first-person history this essay has argued may be doing more work than is usually credited.

Two of those objections are not actually the same kind of claim, and it is worth pulling them apart, because otherwise the definition of AGI ends up hostage to a much harder and older question. "Genuine understanding," "autonomous goals," and "embodied history" are, whatever one thinks of them, claims about what a system can *do* — and are at least in principle open to evidence one way or the other. "Subjective experience" is a different kind of claim entirely: whether there is something it is like to be the system having that experience, a question about consciousness rather than about capability. Whether a system can think, plan, learn, and discover in some functionally meaningful sense is an empirical question this essay has spent its length trying to make progress on. Whether anything is happening "inside" while it does so is a question in the philosophy of mind that nobody currently has an operational test for, in humans or in machines. Making the definition of AGI depend on resolving that second question first would mean the definition can never be settled, since the underlying philosophical problem shows no sign of being settled either. It is more honest to let "is this system intelligent" and "is there something it is like to be this system" remain two different questions, answerable on different timelines, without pretending that progress on the first requires progress on the second.

Researchers have tried to make this dispute more precise rather than less. One proposed framework separates *depth* — how well a system performs a specific task — from *breadth* — how wide a range of tasks it can perform at all — and argues that today's frontier systems sit at a level of competent, narrow performance rather than at any level that would count as genuine generality. A separate proposal ties the definition to established psychometric theory, defining AGI as matching or exceeding the cognitive versatility of a well-educated adult across the structure of abilities that framework already uses to describe human intelligence. Neither proposal has become a consensus standard, and that is itself informative: even researchers trying in good faith to make "AGI" measurable rather than rhetorical have not converged on the same measurement.

It is also worth naming, plainly, a criticism some researchers have leveled directly at the industry: that the definition of AGI has a habit of moving to track whatever the newest model happens to be good at, which would make the term a moving target by design rather than by accident. A framework flexible enough to accommodate three different thresholds — capability, economic, discovery — arriving separately is a genuine improvement on treating AGI as one undifferentiated finish line. But the same flexibility that makes the framework more accurate also makes it easier to abuse, by declaring victory on whichever threshold is most convenient at a given moment. Holding all three thresholds to a fixed, stated definition — rather than letting any of them drift to match the latest release — is the only real defense against that.

## XIV. Conclusion: Two Thresholds Without an Announcement

It is unlikely that history will contain a single clean moment when someone credibly announces that AGI has been achieved and the claim is broadly, immediately accepted. The more probable path is gradual: systems become incrementally more capable, more reliable, more autonomous, and cheaper to run, better at using tools, better at remembering, better at learning from experience, and perhaps eventually better at deciding what deserves investigation. Each individual increment is modest enough to argue about rather than definitively settle. What looks decisive in hindsight may not have felt decisive at the time.

Of the three thresholds named in Section XII, only two lend themselves to being watched for as a crossing point rather than argued about indefinitely. Capability AGI is the odd one out, and Section XIII explained why: any claim that a system has become "genuinely" comparable to human general intelligence gets tangled up with the unresolved question of what is happening inside the system while it performs, a question this essay has deliberately declined to treat as prerequisite. That does not make capability AGI unimportant — it makes it resistant to a single, checkable announcement in a way the other two are not.

The first watchable threshold is economic — the same Economic AGI defined in Section VII, restated here as a crossing point rather than a category:

**an artificial system producing reliable, economically useful cognitive output across a sufficiently broad range of tasks at lower total cost than human labor.**

Crossing that point is enough, on its own, to reshape labor markets and the economics of cognitive work, and it does not require capability AGI or discovery AGI to arrive alongside it. A system could cross it while still depending entirely on humans to decide what is worth learning and what is worth investigating, and the change would still be enormous. But it would be a change in *who, or what, does existing intellectual work* — not a change in how fast that work expands the boundary of what is known.

The second crossing point is different in kind, not just in scale. Call it the epistemic threshold — the practical face of the Discovery AGI defined in Section XII:

**machine intelligence ceases to depend primarily on the stock of knowledge humanity has already produced, and becomes an autonomous, reliable contributor to expanding that stock.**

The first threshold changes the economics of intelligence — who performs the work, and at what cost. The second changes something more fundamental: the rate at which civilization itself can learn. A world that crosses only the economic threshold has cheaper cognitive labor. A world that also crosses the epistemic one has a second engine for producing knowledge, running alongside the one biological science has relied on for its entire history, and potentially not bound by the same constraints of individual attention, career length, or the slow diffusion of insight from one mind to another.

These two thresholds may not arrive together, and there is no strong reason to expect they will. The economic threshold looks closer: it does not require a system to decide what is worth investigating, only to perform existing intellectual work reliably and cheaply. The epistemic threshold is harder, because it depends on capabilities this essay has argued are still substantially unsolved — genuine agency, autonomous verification, and a world model able to revise itself through real investigation rather than only through human-curated retraining. It is entirely possible to reach the first without the second for a long time. It is also possible, in narrow domains, to see fragments of the second appear well before the first is fully realized anywhere.

Whether either threshold, on its own or together, constitutes "AGI" in the strict sense this essay opened with is a question it has deliberately not tried to close. What can be said with more confidence is this: the deeper disagreement running underneath the whole debate — whether current architectures face a fundamental ceiling, or whether what remains is an engineering, reliability, and integration problem — does not have to resolve the same way for both thresholds. The economic one may turn out to be almost entirely an engineering problem. The epistemic one may turn out to need something genuinely new. Mistaking confidence for either answer, on either threshold, is currently unearned. But it is worth ending on what is not in doubt: an artificial system that becomes cheap enough to substitute for a meaningful share of human cognitive labor, and separately, a system that becomes a genuine contributor to what humanity knows rather than only a very fast rearranger of what humanity already knew — either one alone would be among the most consequential developments in the history of the species.

It is worth measuring, in closing, the distance actually traveled. McCulloch and Pitts began with an abstraction stripped down almost to nothing:

**signals → weighted relationships → output**

Everything since — Hebb's rule, the perceptron, backpropagation, attention, the whole architecture this essay has spent its length examining — is an elaboration of that single idea, not a departure from it. What this essay has argued is that the elaboration is still underway, and that its next stretch runs through territory the original abstraction says nothing about on its own:

**relationships → learning → reasoning → tools → memory → action → continual learning → investigation → discovery**

The question this essay opened with was never really whether a network of weighted relationships could reproduce a human mind. It was whether that same basic idea, extended far enough, could become something that had not existed before: a second substrate for intelligence, running alongside the biological one, capable not only of applying what humanity already knows but of helping humanity find out what it does not.
