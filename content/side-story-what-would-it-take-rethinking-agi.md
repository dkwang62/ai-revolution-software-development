## I. Starting with a Cell

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts published a mathematical description of a neuron that had almost nothing to do with biology and almost everything to do with logic. Real neurons are messy: chemical gradients, ion channels, dendritic trees with their own local computation, timing effects that unfold over milliseconds. McCulloch and Pitts stripped all of that away and kept one idea — a neuron receives signals, weighs them, and fires or doesn't fire depending on whether the weighted sum crosses a threshold.

That abstraction was the real event, more than any specific equation. The claim, implicit and enormous, was that thought could be represented as a network of simple units connected by adjustable strengths. Donald Hebb gave the network a way to change: connections that fire together strengthen together. Frank Rosenblatt built a physical machine, the perceptron, that adjusted its own weights from data. Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert then showed the limits of that particular machine, and the field entered a long winter.

The winter ended not because of a new idea about intelligence but because of two engineering developments: backpropagation, which gave a systematic way to assign credit and blame across many layers of a network, and, decades later, GPUs, which happened to be extremely good at the kind of dense matrix multiplication that large networks require. Deep learning was, in an important sense, the perceptron scaled up until it worked. The Transformer architecture, introduced in 2017, added one more mechanism — attention, a way for every element of an input to weigh its relevance to every other element — and that mechanism turned out to be extraordinarily good at language.

It is worth being precise about what the matrix is doing in all of this, because the metaphor gets garbled in popular discussion. The matrix is not the discovery. The discovery, going back to McCulloch and Pitts, is that intelligence might be representable as a network of relationships whose strengths change through learning. A matrix is simply an efficient bookkeeping device for storing millions or billions of such relationships and computing with them quickly. Confusing the representation for the idea it represents leads to two mistakes people commonly make: overstating what neural networks are (thinking the matrix somehow *is* the mind) and understating what they are (dismissing them as "just matrix multiplication," as though the same could not be said of a great deal of biological computation once you look closely enough).

A second confusion is worth heading off early. In a large language model, tokens are not neurons. Tokens are the information being processed — words, subwords, pixels, whatever the input has been chopped into. The network is the machinery that processes them: the layers, the attention heads, the millions of weighted connections that were shaped during training and are normally fixed during use. When a Transformer's attention mechanism computes how strongly one token should attend to another, that is a *dynamic* relationship, recomputed fresh for every new input. It is not the same kind of relationship as the network's *learned* weights, which change slowly, if at all, and represent something closer to accumulated knowledge. Keeping this distinction — permanent structural relationships versus transient input-to-input relationships — clear is necessary for almost everything that follows.

## II. The Puzzle of the Clumsy Genius

Anyone who has spent real time with a frontier language model has felt the same disorienting whiplash: a system that can write working code, summarize a legal filing, or explain a proof in topology, and then, in the same conversation, miscount the letters in a word, contradict something it said three paragraphs earlier, or confidently invent a citation. This is not a minor implementation bug. It is a pattern stable enough to demand an explanation, and there are two very different candidate explanations.

The first: current architectures are missing something structurally necessary for general intelligence, and no amount of scaling will fix it — the clumsiness is a symptom of a genuine ceiling.

The second: much of the clumsiness follows not from what these networks *can* compute but from how humans have chosen to train, freeze, and deploy them — the clumsiness is a symptom of a deployment decision, not a computational limit.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and untangling how much of the gap belongs to each is one of the genuinely open questions in the field. But it is worth taking the second explanation seriously before concluding the first, because the training-and-deployment story is more load-bearing than casual conversation about AI usually credits.

A recent benchmark makes the gap unusually vivid. By early 2026, frontier models were scoring near saturation — around 98 percent — on the original ARC-AGI puzzle benchmark, a test of abstract pattern inference that had resisted progress for years. The benchmark's creators responded with a third version, ARC-AGI-3, that swapped static puzzles for interactive environments with no instructions: an agent has to figure out the goal, build a working model of the environment, and plan its own actions through trial and observation, the way a person does when handed an unfamiliar game. At launch in March 2026, humans solved essentially all of the evaluation environments. The best frontier models — from Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI — scored well under half a percent. That contrast, near-total mastery on one axis and near-total failure on another that seems, if anything, simpler for a human, is exactly the kind of unevenness this essay is trying to explain. It is hard to read as evidence that a single, uniform general intelligence has already been achieved. It is equally hard to read as evidence that nothing resembling intelligence is present, given what these same systems accomplish elsewhere. It looks, instead, like the kind of jagged capability profile a system without autonomous, self-directed world-modeling would be expected to have — extraordinarily fluent when drawing on a vast trained-in prior, and comparatively lost the moment a task requires building a new one from scratch, on the fly, from nothing but exploration.

## III. Training and Inference Were Never a Law of Nature

Every production AI system in wide use today follows roughly the same lifecycle: pretrain on a vast corpus, refine through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, freeze the resulting weights, and deploy the frozen model to millions of users. During deployment — inference — the weights do not change. The model can hold a conversation, but it cannot, in that conversation, update the underlying network the way it updated during training.

It is tempting to treat this as a fact about what neural networks fundamentally are. It isn't. There is nothing in the mathematics of gradient descent that requires training to stop before deployment begins. Continued pretraining on new data, fine-tuning on a narrower distribution, reinforcement learning from ongoing interaction — all of these are technically available and, in various research contexts, already used. A model does not have to start from random initialization to learn something new; it can continue from an existing set of weights. The freeze between training and inference is an engineering and governance choice, not a mathematical necessity.

That reframes the interesting question. It is not:

> Can an AI system continue learning after deployment?

The honest technical answer to that is yes, in principle, today. The harder and more interesting question is:

> Can an AI system autonomously decide *what* it should learn, judge whether new information is trustworthy, integrate it without damaging existing capabilities, and use the result to improve its own world model?

That is a much higher bar, and the reasons developers have not cleared it are substantial rather than merely bureaucratic. Catastrophic forgetting is real: naively updating weights on new data can silently degrade performance on tasks the model previously handled well, and detecting that degradation before it reaches users is hard. Model poisoning is real: a system that learns continuously from open interaction is a system that can be deliberately fed false or manipulative information, and at internet scale, some fraction of input will be adversarial. Distribution shift, stability, auditability, and privacy compound the problem — a model whose weights are quietly changing after every conversation is a model whose behavior cannot be tested once and trusted thereafter, and one that may retain fragments of private conversations in ways nobody can review.

These are legitimate engineering reasons for the freeze, not excuses. But they are reasons to solve a hard problem carefully, not reasons to conclude that continual learning is impossible in principle or permanently off the table. It is worth resisting the jump from "we do not currently allow this, for good reasons" to "this cannot be done."

## IV. What a Child's Development Suggests

A useful comparison, held loosely rather than literally, is how humans avoid the same failure modes without preventing a child's brain from changing at all.

A child is not protected from bad ideas by freezing her neural development. She is protected by a layered system: evolved predispositions toward attachment, empathy, reciprocity, and social bonding that provide raw material for morality before any explicit teaching occurs; a family that filters and interprets the world during the years when judgment is least developed; a society with accumulated norms, laws, and institutions that reward some behaviors and punish others; a school system that transmits centuries of consolidated knowledge instead of requiring each child to rediscover geometry from nothing; and continuous feedback from consequences, both social and physical.

Represented schematically:

**evolutionary predispositions + family + society + education + experience + consequences + continual learning → a functioning adult mind**

No part of this system prevents learning. All of it shapes and filters what is learned, and provides scaffolding — accumulated culture — so a child never has to build a world model entirely from scratch.

The speculative question, and it should be treated as speculative, is whether artificial systems might eventually follow a structurally similar pattern:

**architecture + pretraining + post-training + social and institutional constraints + persistent memory + experience + continual learning → a system that keeps developing after deployment**

Instead of the current pipeline —

**train → align → freeze → deploy**

— a more open-ended pattern would look like:

**train → deploy → experience → evaluate → learn → revise world model → act → observe consequences → learn again**

This is a conceptual analogy, not a claim of biological equivalence. Artificial neural networks do not have neurotransmitters, developmental critical periods, embodied hormonal states, or evolutionary history, and treating the comparison as more than structural would be a mistake. What transfers is not the biology but the *shape* of the solution: safety and reliability under continual change come from surrounding structure and filtering, not from preventing the underlying system from changing at all. Whether that shape can be successfully rebuilt in silicon — with verification, provenance-checking, and forgetting-resistant update mechanisms doing the work that family, culture, and consequence do for a child — is an engineering problem nobody has yet solved at scale, not a question already answered in either direction.

## V. The Tool Question, Reconsidered

A common argument against calling current systems generally intelligent points to their dependence on external tools: they need search to access current information, calculators to do reliable arithmetic, code execution to verify their own reasoning, and retrieval systems to recall specific facts accurately. If a system needs a battery of external crutches to function well, the argument goes, it cannot be exhibiting general intelligence on its own.

This argument proves too much, because it would also disqualify humans.

No practicing accountant holds a spreadsheet's worth of computation in her head; she holds Excel. No physician has the entire body of medical literature memorized; she holds journals, references, and increasingly a search bar. No working programmer has memorized every function signature in every library she uses; she holds documentation and, now, autocomplete. A person who searches her email for an old confirmation number instead of recalling it from memory has not thereby demonstrated a deficiency in general intelligence — she has demonstrated the ordinary, universal human habit of offloading memory and computation onto external structures whenever a good external structure is available.

Books extend memory the way retrieval systems extend a model's context. Libraries extend books the way databases extend retrieval. Calculators extend mental arithmetic the way a code interpreter extends a model's ability to compute reliably. Other specialists extend an individual's expertise the way multi-agent systems extend a single model's coverage. Eyes and ears are already multimodal perception; a model with vision and audio input is not doing something categorically different from what evolution already built into the human sensory system. Hands manipulate the physical world the way computer-use and robotics interfaces let a model act on the world beyond text.

If that comparison holds, the honest test of general intelligence is not:

**human brain, unaided, versus AI model plus every tool it can reach**

but the symmetric version:

**human brain plus its tools versus AI model plus its tools**

Once tool use is put on both sides of the comparison rather than only one, a large share of the "it's not really intelligent, it just looks things up" objection loses its force. It does not settle the deeper question of whether the model's internal reasoning is comparable to a human's — that question remains genuinely open — but it does mean tool dependence by itself cannot be the deciding evidence.

## VI. What Does It Mean for an AI to Know Something?

The discussion of tools exposes a related ambiguity that becomes more important the more capable these systems get: what does it actually mean to say a model *knows* something? The word is doing several different jobs at once, and collapsing them together makes the rest of this essay's argument harder to evaluate rather than easier.

If a model correctly states that Canberra is the capital of Australia without consulting anything, that fact is represented somewhere in its trained weights — diffusely, indirectly, but internally. Call this **parametric knowledge**. If instead you hand the model a document saying a meeting has moved to Thursday, it can reason about that fact immediately, compare it with other dates, draft a message around it — without the underlying weights changing at all. The information exists only for as long as the context holds it. Call this **contextual knowledge**: real, usable, and gone the moment the context is gone unless something outside the model preserves it.

A third possibility sits between these two. Instead of placing a document directly in the prompt, give the system access to a large external store of documents and let it search when it needs to — a pattern usually called retrieval-augmented generation: a question comes in, relevant material is retrieved, placed in context, reasoned over, and answered. The model does not need to contain the fact; it needs to know how to find it and how to use it once found. Call this **retrieved knowledge**. The pattern is exactly the one a working professional uses constantly — a physician does not memorize the literature, a lawyer does not memorize every judgment, an engineer does not memorize every specification. Expertise is partly what is internalized and partly knowing where reliable information lives and how to evaluate it once retrieved.

This reframes a comparison that looks simpler than it is. Take two systems: one holds an enormous amount of fact in its weights but cannot search anything external; the other holds somewhat less internally but can recognize when its own knowledge is insufficient, retrieve the right information, judge its relevance, reconcile it against conflicting evidence, and use the result. Raw memory capacity does not settle which system is more intelligent — the same way memorizing an encyclopedia does not make someone more intelligent than someone who knows how to investigate an unfamiliar problem well.

**Retrieval, though, is not learning, and the gap between them matters.** If a model looks up a city's population, uses it to answer a question, and the conversation ends, nothing has necessarily changed inside the model — the sequence runs from ignorance through retrieval through a temporary context straight back to nothing retained. Genuine learning runs differently: ignorance, investigation, evidence, evaluation, a persistent update, and behavior that is actually different afterward. Modern AI systems can increasingly paper over weak internal knowledge with search, retrieval, long context windows, and external tools, and the overall system ends up looking far more knowledgeable than the underlying frozen model actually is. That is not illegitimate — humans do the same thing constantly — but external access should not be mistaken for internal learning, and a fair accounting of what a system "knows" has to keep the two apart.

There is an intermediate case worth naming on its own: **persistent experiential memory**, external to the model's weights but retained across sessions rather than discarded at the end of one. If a system learns mid-conversation that a project has been renamed and stores that fact rather than modifying any parameter, it can retrieve and use it weeks later — functionally remembering, while the foundation model underneath remains mechanically unchanged. That yields a distinction worth keeping precise: **model memory is not the same thing as system memory.** The weights can stay frozen while the larger system built around them — memory store, retrieval layer, tools, planning, verification — keeps accumulating experience. At some point evaluating only the frozen model while ignoring everything wrapped around it becomes about as misleading as evaluating a professional's competence by mentally subtracting her notes, her colleagues, and her search engine and asking what's left inside her skull alone. The economically and practically relevant unit of intelligence, increasingly, is the system — not the isolated network.

Putting these together gives five progressively more demanding senses of "knowing": parametric knowledge sitting in the weights; contextual knowledge available only within the current exchange; retrieved knowledge pulled in from outside when needed; persistent experiential knowledge kept because the system judged it worth keeping; and discovered knowledge — reliable information produced through investigation that was previously unknown to the system and to humanity alike, the subject of Section XI. None of these should be collapsed into any other. Retrieving a fact is not the same as learning it. Remembering an experience is not the same as folding it into a revised world model. Generating a novel hypothesis is not the same as discovering anything. And changing a weight is not the same as changing it *correctly*. The rough progression — retrieve, understand, remember, learn, revise, discover — asks something more at every step than the step before it.

Seen this way, retrieval turns out to be the easy part of the problem, and the interesting difficulty sits one layer up, in questions no search index answers on its own: what is worth retrieving, which source deserves trust, whether two sources actually contradict each other, whether a contradiction matters, whether something newly found should be remembered, whether it should change the model's picture of the world, and what else would stop being true if it were accepted. Those are judgment problems, not retrieval problems, and they lead directly back to the continual-learning question this essay raised earlier. A sufficiently advanced system would not simply have a bigger database bolted onto it — it would actively manage its own relationship to knowledge: when internal knowledge suffices, when outside evidence is needed, which evidence to trust, what to keep, what to let go, and when the accumulated weight of evidence justifies actually revising its model of reality. The path worth tracking is not *small database → larger database → AGI*. It is closer to *knowledge → uncertainty → retrieval → evaluation → memory → learning → revision → new questions → investigation → discovery* — a single cognitive loop, not a stack of separate features, with retrieval, memory, continual learning, and scientific discovery as different stages of the same underlying problem rather than different products.

## VII. Do These Systems Have a World Model?

It would be inaccurate to say large language models have no internal representation of the world. The evidence, from interpretability research over the past several years, points the other way: these networks build internal representations of objects, spatial relationships, social dynamics, causal regularities in text, and a great deal else, well beyond anything a simple statistical parrot would need. Dismissing this entirely misreads the research.

The harder and more precise question is whether those representations amount to the specific kind of world model associated with general intelligence: one that is *persistent* across time rather than reconstructed fresh in each context window, *coherent* rather than locally consistent but globally contradictory, *causally useful* for predicting the consequences of actions rather than merely capturing correlations in training data, and *continually updated* through ongoing experience rather than fixed at the moment training ended.

There is a real difference between having learned rich representations of the world as it existed in a training corpus, and being a system that continuously experiences the world and revises its beliefs in response. A model deployed today knows an enormous amount about the world as of its training cutoff and comparatively little, on its own, about what has happened since — unless a tool tells it. A human knows less in raw volume but updates continuously through lived experience, and that continuous updating is arguably as central to what we mean by "intelligence in the world" as raw representational richness is.

Multimodality, agentic tool use, persistent memory across sessions, and physical embodiment through robotics are all mechanisms that push in the direction of closing this gap — each one gives a system more continuous contact with a changing world rather than a single frozen snapshot of it. None of them, individually or so far in combination, has closed it completely.

## VIII. The Economic Reframing

Set the philosophical question aside for a moment and consider a different one, less discussed but arguably more consequential: what is the actual economic project underway?

It may not be an attempt to reproduce the human brain. It may be an attempt to produce **useful intelligence more cheaply than biological human intelligence can be produced.**

Producing one more skilled engineer, physician, lawyer, or accountant requires, at minimum, another human being: roughly two decades of childhood, an education system, healthcare, housing, and then a career's worth of ongoing compensation, benefits, and finite working hours. It is an extraordinarily capital- and time-intensive process, constrained by biology in ways that have not changed much in centuries.

AI changes the shape of that cost curve. After an enormous upfront fixed cost — data collection, compute, research, training runs that can run into the hundreds of millions of dollars — each additional unit of cognitive output can, in principle, be produced through additional computation rather than through raising another human being from infancy. Intelligence, in other words, starts to behave less like a scarce biological resource and more like reproducible capital: expensive to build the first unit, comparatively cheap to run the marginal one.

That reframes the relevant metric. Instead of asking how closely a system's internal processes resemble a human mind, the economically decisive question becomes:

**useful cognitive output ÷ cost**

An AI system does not need to match or exceed human performance across every task to be economically transformative under this metric. If it can perform 80 to 90 percent of the economically valuable work in a profession, at a small fraction of the cost, it can reshape that profession's labor market even while remaining clearly inferior to skilled humans on plenty of individual tasks — including some that look, from the outside, deceptively simple.

This suggests a useful split in vocabulary:

**Capability AGI** — an artificial system whose internal processes and range of performance are genuinely comparable to human general intelligence, in whatever sense turns out to be scientifically well-defined and broadly agreed upon.

**Economic AGI** — an artificial system sufficiently general, reliable, and inexpensive to substitute for human cognitive labor across a broad swath of economically valuable work, whether or not it satisfies the capability definition.

These two thresholds need not arrive together. It is entirely plausible that economic AGI arrives first, disrupting labor markets and professions well before there is any scientific consensus that "true" AGI has been achieved — and plausible, separately, that a system could pass a demanding capability bar for general reasoning while still being too unreliable, too expensive, or too narrowly deployed to count as economic AGI. Confusing the two thresholds is a common source of talking past one another in public debate about AGI timelines. (A third threshold, concerned with autonomous discovery rather than performance or cost, is worth setting aside for now — it returns in Section XI.)

This is not a purely hypothetical distinction. According to reporting on their commercial arrangement, Microsoft and OpenAI have reportedly attached a concrete economic figure to the term — a system counts once it can generate roughly one hundred billion dollars in profit, with an independent panel meant to verify the claim. Whatever one thinks of that as a scientific definition, it is a working illustration of economic AGI functioning exactly as this section describes: a threshold defined by output and cost rather than by any specific claim about internal cognition, arrived at not by researchers but by the terms of a business contract. It is also a reminder that the definition of AGI is not merely being debated in journals. It is being written into agreements that carry real financial and governance consequences, which gives outside observers good reason to treat any single organization's declaration of "AGI achieved" with some skepticism, regardless of which threshold it claims to satisfy.

## IX. An Honest Inventory of the Gaps

It is worth listing the deficiencies usually cited as evidence that today's systems fall short of general intelligence, and, for each, asking the same three questions: Is it genuinely absent? Can it already be supplied through some combination of tools, memory, retrieval, multimodality, or additional training? And does closing the gap require a fundamentally new architecture, or might it emerge from scaling and integrating capabilities that already exist in some form?

Reliability and hallucination are real weaknesses, though retrieval grounding, tool verification, and better training have measurably reduced them without eliminating them — an engineering trajectory more than a hard wall so far. Persistent memory across sessions is largely a product and infrastructure problem at this point; systems with long-term memory stores already exist, even if they are not yet as fluid as human recall. Continual and autonomous learning — deciding for oneself what is worth learning and safely integrating it — remains the least solved item on the list, for the trustworthiness and catastrophic-forgetting reasons discussed above, and it is not obvious this is solvable by scaling alone. Coherent world models and causal reasoning are partially present and actively improving but not yet at the level of confident, generalizable causal inference a domain expert brings to an unfamiliar problem. Learning from very small amounts of experience — the way a person can generalize a new rule from one or two examples — remains a genuine weak point relative to human few-shot learning, though in-context learning during a single conversation is a partial, narrower version of the same capacity.

Long-horizon planning and long-horizon autonomy — carrying out a complex, multi-step project over hours or days with minimal supervision — have improved rapidly with agentic scaffolding but still degrade over long horizons in ways human experts typically do not. Metacognition, in the sense of reliably knowing what one does not know, is inconsistent: these systems sometimes flag uncertainty appropriately and sometimes confabulate with total confidence, and it is not yet clear whether that inconsistency is a scaling problem or something closer to a structural one. Interaction with the physical world is progressing through robotics and computer-use interfaces but remains far behind digital-domain competence. Autonomous verification — checking one's own work reliably before presenting it as finished — and the accumulation of individual, first-person experience over a working lifetime are both areas where current systems fall furthest short of what a seasoned human professional brings to a job.

The honest conclusion from this inventory is that it does not sort cleanly into "solved" and "unsolvable." Some gaps look like ordinary engineering problems already yielding to scale, better data, and better scaffolding. Others — autonomous continual learning chief among them — look like they may need genuinely new mechanisms, not just more of the current ones. Assuming every remaining weakness will fall to more parameters and more compute is unjustified optimism. Assuming none of them will is unjustified pessimism. The honest position, at present, is that nobody knows with confidence which items on this list belong in which category, and treating that uncertainty as itself the finding is more accurate than resolving it prematurely in either direction.

This uncertainty is not confined to casual conversation; it runs through the research literature itself. When a paper argued that GPT-4 showed "sparks" of general intelligence by solving novel problems across math, coding, medicine, and law, a common rebuttal was that high performance on a benchmark measures skill rather than intelligence — and that skill can be purchased with enough training data without implying any underlying capacity to reason generally. That disagreement maps neatly onto the inventory above: a gap that looks closed because a benchmark score has gone up is not necessarily the same as a gap that has actually closed, if what improved was coverage of the specific distribution being tested rather than the general mechanism the benchmark was meant to stand in for. Distinguishing the two, benchmark by benchmark and deficiency by deficiency, is exactly the kind of work this inventory calls for and exactly the kind of work that is easiest to skip when a leaderboard number is the only evidence at hand.

## X. From Managed Learning to Autonomous Learning

There is a sense in which the AI field already practices a form of continual learning today — it just runs through humans rather than inside the model. The loop looks roughly like this: a model is deployed, users interact with it and surface failures, researchers evaluate those failures, curate new training data in response, run another round of training or fine-tuning, evaluate the result, and ship a new model. Humans are, in effect, the nervous system connecting deployment back to training.

The open question is what happens as more of that loop is internalized by the system itself: experience an interaction, detect that something went wrong or something new was learned, investigate the cause, verify the new information against what is already known, decide whether it is worth retaining, update the internal world model, test for damage to unrelated capabilities, and keep only what survives that test. To the extent that loop can be run safely and automatically, the human role shifts from *performing* the learning-management process to *designing the environment, objectives, and boundaries* within which the system performs it itself — a shift in kind, not merely in degree, and one that raises its own serious governance and safety questions that this essay does not attempt to resolve.

There is one advantage available to a networked artificial system that has no clean biological analogue. When one human learns something, no other human's brain updates as a result; knowledge spreads only through the comparatively slow channels of teaching, writing, and culture. A network of AI instances, in principle, could aggregate experience across millions of separate interactions, verify what was learned, consolidate it, and propagate it back out to the whole network — something closer to:

**millions of instances → millions of experiences → verification → consolidation → shared update**

If that process can be made reliable and economical — and reliability is doing a great deal of work in that sentence — it points toward a mode of collective learning with no real precedent in biological intelligence, which is bottlenecked by the fact that experience is trapped inside individual skulls.

## XI. From Learning Knowledge to Creating Knowledge

There is one further threshold that may matter more than many of the capabilities normally used to define AGI: whether an artificial intelligence can move beyond learning and applying existing human knowledge and begin autonomously producing genuinely new knowledge about the world.

The distinction is easy to blur because several very different activities are casually described as "learning." A model can retrieve something humanity already knows — question, search, retrieve. It can synthesize existing knowledge into a combination or interpretation no particular human happened to have written down before. Or it can learn something previously unknown to the model but already known to humanity. All three are genuinely useful. None of them, on its own, constitutes discovery.

A more demanding pattern looks different:

**observation → uncertainty → hypothesis → prediction → experiment or investigation → evidence → verification → revised world model**

If the resulting conclusion was not previously known to humanity, something categorically more interesting has happened. The system has not merely consumed existing knowledge. It has participated in extending it.

That suggests a further, harder test:

> Can an artificial intelligence autonomously discover reliable knowledge that neither it nor humanity previously possessed?

Consider what a capable scientist actually does when confronted with a genuinely unexplained phenomenon. She does not search the literature for an answer that isn't there. She identifies the uncertainty, generates competing explanations, works out what observations would distinguish between them, designs a test, gathers evidence, discards the hypotheses that fail, refines the ones that partially survive, and ends up with a better model of the phenomenon than she started with. An artificial system performing the analogous loop would look something like: observe, notice an anomaly, ask a question, generate hypotheses, predict consequences, design a test, use whatever tools the test requires, collect evidence, evaluate the results, revise its world model, and identify the next open question.

This is importantly different from producing novel text. A language model can already generate a sentence, or even a scientific hypothesis, that no one has written before — that bar is not hard to clear and does not by itself amount to anything. Novel output is not new knowledge. A more accurate approximation is closer to:

**novel hypothesis + evidence + successful verification → candidate new knowledge**

Verification is the load-bearing term in that equation. Without it, a system capable of generating a million novel hypotheses an hour is not a discovery engine — it is an unusually productive generator of speculation, indistinguishable from noise until something outside the system checks which hypotheses, if any, were actually right.

### Where the current systems actually stand

This is not a purely hypothetical loop; versions of it are already running. Multi-agent systems such as Google DeepMind's Co-Scientist and FutureHouse's Robin can generate hypotheses, design experiments, and analyze results with only light human direction, and both have surfaced drug-candidate leads that researchers considered plausible enough to pursue further — though in both cases, the labs presenting the results were careful to note that the outputs still needed independent experimental confirmation before they counted as anything more than promising leads. In October 2025, a mathematician working with an AI reasoning system produced a new convergence proof in optimization theory after roughly twelve hours of iterative back-and-forth — a genuine result, but one that emerged from sustained human-AI collaboration rather than autonomous investigation. And by mid-2026, one fully automated research system had produced a manuscript that scored above the average acceptance threshold at a peer-reviewed workshop, the first fully AI-generated paper to clear that bar.

Those results sit alongside a pointed caution from within the field. One AI-lab researcher described a case where a system's report included impressive-looking charts that turned out, on inspection, to be fabricated — the underlying analysis had never actually been run — and drew from it the conclusion that fully autonomous scientists were not close at hand, a skepticism echoed by at least one frontier lab's leadership around the same time. That example is close to a textbook illustration of exactly the failure mode this section is built around: novel output that looks like a discovery is not a discovery until something outside the generating system checks it, and the systems making the most visible progress on the earlier stages of the discovery loop — hypothesis generation, experiment design — are not yet the systems that can be trusted to run the verification stage unsupervised. The gap between "can propose" and "can be trusted to confirm" is, at present, exactly where the frontier sits.

### The tool question again, applied to science

This also clarifies how tool dependence should be read in a scientific context. An artificial scientist might need databases, simulations, telescopes, microscopes, laboratory robotics, the existing scientific literature, other AI systems, and human collaborators. That dependence would not make its discoveries less its own. Einstein depended on mathematics developed by others; astronomers depend on telescopes; biologists depend on microscopes; particle physicists depend on machines costing billions of dollars that took thousands of people to build. Human science has never been the product of unaided minds. The comparison that matters remains the symmetric one introduced earlier — human intelligence plus its instruments, against artificial intelligence plus its instruments — and the question worth asking is whether the intelligence on either side of that comparison can direct its tools toward reducing its own uncertainty about reality, rather than only executing instructions.

### From answering questions to asking them

A further distinction sits underneath this one. Most AI systems in use today operate strictly as respondents: a human supplies a question, the system supplies an answer. A more autonomous scientific intelligence would, at least some of the time, generate the question itself — noticing that a prediction failed, that two observations sit in tension, that an existing explanation cannot account for a new result, and that some specific next experiment would do more than others to resolve the tension. That capacity — identifying the gaps in one's own understanding and judging which of them are worth the cost of investigating — is a considerably more demanding threshold than answering well, and it is where metacognition, world-modeling, tool use, autonomous learning, and discovery all start to converge into a single requirement rather than four separate ones.

### Discovery requires a world model that can actually change

This connects back to the continual-learning problem raised earlier. Suppose a system predicts that A implies B, tests the prediction, and instead repeatedly observes A leading to C. A capable system should not stop at reporting that the prediction was wrong. It should ask what that failure implies about the model that generated the wrong prediction in the first place — moving from one world model, through the contradictory evidence and the investigation it prompts, to a revised world model that generates different predictions than the one it replaced. That is a considerably stronger notion of self-learning than simply letting gradient descent continue to run after deployment; it requires the system to participate in deciding what should be learned, and why, rather than only absorbing whatever data happens to arrive.

### A third threshold

Put together, this suggests "AGI" may conceal at least three distinct thresholds rather than one.

**Capability AGI** — an artificial system can perform the broad range of intellectual tasks that competent humans can perform.

**Economic AGI** — an artificial system can perform a sufficiently broad range of economically valuable cognitive work reliably and more cheaply than humans.

**Discovery AGI** — an artificial system can autonomously investigate reality and produce important, verified knowledge that neither it nor humanity previously possessed.

These need not arrive together, and in fact there is reason to expect they won't. Economic AGI might arrive while AI remains entirely dependent on humans to decide which questions are worth investigating in the first place. In the other direction, narrow, specialized systems might make genuine autonomous discoveries in a constrained domain — protein folding is an early example of something in this neighborhood — while remaining far too narrow to qualify as generally intelligent by any other measure. But if all three were eventually to converge in a single system, its character would be different in kind, not just in degree, from anything that currently exists: a system that could perform existing intellectual work, do so more cheaply than the humans who currently perform it, and also expand the body of knowledge that future intellectual work — human and artificial alike — draws on.

### Collective discovery

This makes the earlier point about collective learning more consequential, not less. Picture a million AI instances distributed across different problems, laboratories, simulations, and organizations, each generating observations, hypotheses, experiments, and verified conclusions. A discovery made by one instance could, once verified, become available to every other instance almost immediately.

Biological intelligence has nothing directly comparable to this. Human civilization approximates it through language, books, universities, journals, and now the internet, but the transmission is slow and lossy — knowledge has to move out of one biological brain, into some external representation, and then back into another biological brain, a process that has taken centuries to reach its current speed. An artificial system that could compress the cycle from experience through discovery, verification, and shared update into something closer to real time would be operating under a genuinely different constraint on the growth of knowledge than any civilization has previously had available to it — a difference of degree large enough that it may functionally become a difference of kind.

### When discovery improves discovery

There is one further possibility worth naming cautiously, because it is the point at which this line of reasoning most easily slides into speculation it cannot support: an artificial intelligence applying the same discovery process to artificial intelligence itself — investigating better architectures, better learning algorithms, more efficient use of computation, better memory systems, better verification methods, better mechanisms for continual learning. The resulting loop, in its simplest form, is an AI discovering a better method of building AI, producing an improved AI, which makes a better discovery, which produces a further improvement.

This is the structural basis of arguments about recursive self-improvement, and it is worth being precise about what such a loop does and does not guarantee. Nothing about it implies an instantaneous "intelligence explosion." Experiments take time to run. Computation costs real money. Physical infrastructure has to be built and maintained. Some problems plausibly have diminishing returns built into them. Every candidate discovery still requires verification before it can be trusted, and better algorithms may simply become harder to find as the easy ones are exhausted — the same pattern that has slowed progress in plenty of mature scientific fields.

The important claim is narrower than an explosion, and does not depend on one: an intelligence capable of autonomously extending human knowledge, and of improving the methods by which it makes those discoveries, would represent a qualitatively different stage of artificial intelligence than a system whose principal role is to reproduce, recombine, and apply knowledge that humans already created. That distinction may turn out to be a more meaningful test of AGI than most benchmarks currently in use. Instead of asking only whether an AI can answer the questions humans already know how to answer, the more demanding version of the question is whether it can discover the answers to questions humanity cannot yet answer — and judge, on its own, which of those questions are worth asking in the first place. If the answer to that becomes yes, the argument that the system is merely imitating intelligence becomes considerably harder to sustain.

## XII. What Word Are We Actually Arguing About?

It is worth asking, at this point, whether "AGI" has quietly become an unfairly anthropomorphic standard — one that implicitly requires an artificial system to replicate the specific *texture* of human cognition rather than its practical capabilities.

If a system can reason across unrelated domains, learn tasks it was not explicitly trained on, use external tools fluidly, retrieve information when its own knowledge is insufficient, remember relevant context across time, communicate clearly, plan and execute substantial multi-step projects, recognize the limits of its own knowledge, verify important conclusions before acting on them, adapt to changing circumstances, and do most economically valuable cognitive work more cheaply than a human — what, specifically, would still be missing before that system earned the label "generally intelligent"?

That is not a rhetorical trap; it deserves a real answer, and different serious thinkers give different ones. Some would say nothing further is missing — that this list, fully realized, simply *is* general intelligence, definitional disputes notwithstanding. Others would insist that something is still absent even in a system meeting every item on that list: genuine understanding rather than sophisticated pattern completion, subjective experience, autonomous goals rather than goals set by a human operator, or the specific kind of embodied, continuously-lived first-person history that this essay has argued may be doing more work than is usually credited. Absent an operational way to detect the presence or absence of that further thing, this becomes as much a philosophical dispute as an empirical one, and it would be dishonest to present it as already resolved.

Researchers have tried to make this dispute more precise rather than less. One proposed framework separates *depth* — how well a system performs a specific task — from *breadth* — how wide a range of tasks it can perform at all — and argues that today's frontier systems sit at a level of competent, narrow performance rather than at any level that would count as genuine generality. A separate proposal ties the definition to established psychometric theory, defining AGI as matching or exceeding the cognitive versatility of a well-educated adult across the structure of abilities that framework already uses to describe human intelligence. Neither proposal has become a consensus standard, and that is itself informative: even researchers trying in good faith to make "AGI" measurable rather than rhetorical have not converged on the same measurement.

It is also worth naming, plainly, a criticism that some researchers have leveled directly at the industry rather than at each other: that the definition of AGI has a habit of moving to track whatever the newest model happens to be good at, which would make the term a moving target by design rather than by accident. Whether or not that criticism is fair to any particular organization, it is a useful check on this essay's own argument. A framework flexible enough to accommodate three different thresholds — capability, economic, discovery — arriving separately is a genuine improvement on treating AGI as one undifferentiated finish line. But the same flexibility that makes the framework more accurate also makes it easier to abuse, by declaring victory on whichever threshold is most convenient at a given moment and quietly changing the subject when a different one is raised. Holding all three thresholds to a fixed, stated definition — rather than letting any of them drift to match the latest release — is the only real defense against that.

## XIII. Conclusion: A Threshold Without an Announcement

It is unlikely that history will contain a single clean moment when someone credibly announces that AGI has been achieved and the claim is broadly, immediately accepted. The more probable path is gradual and largely economic: systems become incrementally more general, more autonomous, more persistent, and more reliable, while the cost of running them continues to fall, and each increment is individually modest enough to argue about rather than definitively settle.

If that is right, the historically decisive threshold may not be a single scientific declaration at all, but something closer to a crossing point in a cost curve:

**useful general intelligence per dollar, delivered by machines, exceeds useful general intelligence per dollar, delivered by humans, across a sufficiently broad range of tasks.**

That crossing point is enough, on its own, to reshape economies — it is the economic AGI threshold discussed earlier, and it does not require capability AGI or discovery AGI to arrive alongside it. But it is worth resisting the temptation to treat it as the whole story. A system that has crossed the economic threshold while still depending entirely on humans to decide what is worth learning, and what is worth investigating, would be transformative without being what many people mean when they imagine general intelligence. The harder and more interesting thresholds — a world model that revises itself through genuine investigation rather than only through human-curated retraining, and a system that can autonomously extend the frontier of what is known rather than only apply what has already been discovered — may arrive later, arrive only partially, or in the least likely but most consequential case, arrive together. Whether any of this constitutes "AGI" in the strict sense this essay opened with is a question it has deliberately not tried to close. What can be said with more confidence is that the two broad possible resolutions to that question — a fundamental architectural ceiling on one hand, an engineering, reliability, and integration problem on the other — remain both live, that they need not resolve identically for capability, economic, and discovery AGI, and that mistaking confidence for any of them, in either direction, is currently unearned.
